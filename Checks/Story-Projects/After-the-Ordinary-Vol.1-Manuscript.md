# After the Ordinary — Volume 1
## The Rumour That Started It All

**Manuscript status:** In progress; publication prose is being written chapter by chapter.  
**Canonical edition:** English  
**Series:** After the Ordinary  
**Volume:** 1 of 5  
**Target length:** Approximately 50,000–60,000 words, adjusted to the needs of the complete story rather than padded to hit a number.

> **Continuity note for the author:** The Story Bible fixes Volume 1 to April–June, but also places the cultural festival in early autumn while requiring the festival rumour to end Volume 1. To preserve the five-volume calendar and the explicit Volume 1 ending, this manuscript currently places the festival on the final weekend of June. This is a transparent working resolution of that internal conflict, not a silent change to the Story Bible. The owner can revise it later if desired.

## Volume 1 — Chapter plan

The volume is designed as a substantial, complete first instalment: Itsuki's ordinary life is established before the catalyst; the ensemble is introduced through recurring situations rather than character-list exposition; the festival incident arrives late enough to matter; and the volume closes with Mio choosing to investigate. The rumour begins here, but it does not resolve the series' larger emotional arcs.

1. **Prologue — The Last Day of Nothing**  
   April 1. Nanami's sixteenth birthday, Itsuki's quiet apartment, the siblings' ordinary rhythm, and the small difference between a place to stay and a place that feels like home.
2. **Chapter One — A Perfectly Ordinary Start**  
   April 2. Itsuki turns eighteen, begins his final school year, and tries to keep the day unremarkable. A brief exchange with a teacher establishes his ability to explain English without making him a prodigy.
3. **Chapter Two — The People Who Don't Need Invitations**  
   Riko's established friendship with Itsuki, their dry back-and-forth, and the first hints of the different face she shows at home.
4. **Chapter Three — A Familiar Name on the Attendance Sheet**  
   The new class arrangement brings Ayane back into Itsuki's daily orbit. Their families' closeness is shown through an ordinary shared meal, while the awkwardness between the two teenagers remains unresolved.
5. **Chapter Four — The Girl Everyone Seems to Know**  
   Akari's social life is shown from the outside and then from within her own class. She is friendly, busy, and comfortable with attention; Itsuki remains a name she recognises, not a romantic interest.
6. **Chapter Five — A Quiet Seat by the Window**  
   Mio appears through school and library routines. Itsuki notices her quiet habits, but they do not yet become close.
7. **Chapter Six — A City Made of Small Errands**  
   Itsuki's commute, apartment chores, convenience-store routines, and Nanami's separate school life give Hoshimiya texture. The siblings' father remains a present, respectful part of their lives.
8. **Chapter Seven — The Things We Don't Ask Twice**  
   Itsuki and Ayane attempt to recover an old ease during a family errand. They discover that remembering the same childhood does not mean they experienced it in the same way.
9. **Chapter Eight — A Test with Too Many Questions**  
   School assessments expose Akari's effort and frustration without turning her into a helpless student. Itsuki's calm explanation of a difficult English passage is noticed, but Akari does not immediately seek him out.
10. **Chapter Nine — The Committee Nobody Wanted**  
    Festival preparations begin. Riko's mischief, Akari's class responsibilities, and Itsuki's habit of accepting practical tasks bring the cast into the same spaces without forcing instant intimacy.
11. **Chapter Ten — Before the Doors Open**  
    The festival's final preparations. Small problems accumulate; Akari is separated from her group while trying to solve one. Itsuki is nearby on an unrelated errand.
12. **Chapter Eleven — The Corridor Between Crowds**  
    The ordinary act of help: a crowd bottleneck, a minor mishap, an awkward moment, and Itsuki carrying an item while Akari regains her footing. Neither treats it as romantic.
13. **Chapter Twelve — Everyone Saw Something**  
    Several students witness fragments. Their accounts differ honestly, and a poorly angled photograph gives the fragments a shape none of them deserves.
14. **Chapter Thirteen — The Simplest Explanation**  
    The rumour spreads. Akari tries to contain it publicly; Itsuki gives literal answers that sound evasive. The comedy comes from character and timing, while the rumour begins to have real social consequences.
15. **Chapter Fourteen — A Question with No Easy Answer**  
    Riko, Ayane, and Akari respond in their own ways. Mio notices contradictions and approaches Itsuki with specific questions. She does not solve the mystery; she decides to investigate it.
16. **Epilogue — The First Version**  
    The school day ends with the rumour still alive. Itsuki realises that ignoring something does not always make it disappear. Mio begins arranging the fragments into a timeline.

# Prologue — The Last Day of Nothing

April 1 was the kind of day that made people suspicious of calendars.

The city had not quite decided whether spring had arrived. Cherry blossoms lined the road beside Hoshimiya Station, but the wind still carried a chill from the previous week. A few petals had collected in the gutter. Others drifted across the pavement and caught against the wheels of bicycles parked outside the convenience store.

Itsuki Kurose watched one land on the toe of his shoe.

He waited for it to move.

It did not.

“Are you planning to negotiate with that?”

Itsuki looked up.

Nanami stood in the doorway of his apartment with a paper shopping bag in one hand and a small cake box balanced on top of it. Her long warm-brown hair fell over the shoulders of her pale cardigan, and the ends moved slightly in the breeze. Her amber eyes were fixed on the petal near his shoe.

“I was waiting for it to leave.”

“It's a flower.”

“I noticed.”

“Then perhaps you should be the one to move.”

“I'm comfortable where I am.”

Nanami stepped around him and entered without waiting for an invitation. She had a spare key. The fact that she used it with the confidence of someone who paid rent was one of the more persistent mysteries of Itsuki's life.

“Shoes,” he said.

“I know.”

“You're still wearing them.”

“I was getting to that.”

“You've been getting to that for three years.”

Nanami placed the bag on the small kitchen counter, slipped off her shoes, and lined them beside his. She did it neatly enough to undermine her own argument.

Itsuki closed the door.

The apartment was modest: a narrow entrance, a compact kitchen, a living space barely large enough for a low table and two people to sit comfortably, and a separate sleeping area divided by a sliding partition. It was clean because Itsuki disliked searching for things more than he disliked cleaning. A drying rack stood near the balcony door. Two mugs were upside down beside the sink. A school timetable was pinned to the refrigerator with a magnet shaped like a blue fish.

Nanami glanced around.

“You cleaned.”

“I live here.”

“That wasn't my question.”

“Then ask a better one.”

She opened the shopping bag. “I brought dinner.”

“That's not dinner.”

“It's part of dinner.”

The cake box made a soft cardboard sound as she moved it onto the counter.

Itsuki looked at it, then at her.

“Happy birthday.”

Nanami paused.

For a moment, her expression lost its usual quickness. Then she looked away and began taking things from the bag.

“You remembered.”

“It's on the calendar.”

“Your calendar has school deadlines, garbage collection, and the day the water company is supposed to inspect the meter.”

“It's a useful calendar.”

“And my birthday.”

“An important administrative event.”

She gave him a flat look. “You're terrible at this.”

“I bought the cake.”

“You didn't buy the cake. I bought the cake.”

“I allowed you to.”

“That's not how gifts work.”

“I'll remember that next year.”

“You'd better.”

Nanami's sixteenth birthday had arrived on a Tuesday, at the beginning of spring break. Their mother had sent a message that morning. Their father—Nanami's father, though Itsuki had never found the distinction particularly useful in daily conversation—had called the previous evening because he had an early business meeting today. Nanami had spent most of the afternoon at her own school helping a friend prepare for the new term.

Now she was here, in Itsuki's apartment, as if the arrangement required no explanation.

It usually didn't.

She unpacked a container of simmered vegetables, a packet of fried chicken, and a small bag of strawberries. The cake was from the bakery near the station. Itsuki recognised the box.

“You went to the one by the east exit?”

“It's your favourite.”

“It's a bakery.”

“You always buy the strawberry shortcake there.”

“Because it's close.”

“Of course.”

He opened the refrigerator and moved a bottle of tea to make room for the food. Nanami watched him.

“You're doing that thing again.”

“What thing?”

“Pretending not to be happy.”

“I haven't said anything.”

“Exactly.”

Itsuki shut the refrigerator.

Nanami smiled, satisfied with herself.

They ate at the low table. The television stayed off. Outside, a bicycle bell rang twice, followed by the distant announcement of a train arriving at the station. Nanami talked about a classmate who had managed to lose her indoor shoes before the new term had even started. Itsuki listened, occasionally asking a question when the story became difficult to follow.

“Anyway,” Nanami said, “she blamed the cleaning committee.”

“Reasonable.”

“She was the cleaning committee.”

“Less reasonable.”

“She said she had been betrayed by the system.”

“An unfortunate system.”

Nanami pointed her chopsticks at him. “You would have liked her.”

“I doubt that.”

“You both think being quiet is a personality.”

“I don't think that.”

“You just demonstrated it.”

Itsuki took another piece of chicken.

Nanami was good at filling a room without making it feel crowded. She could talk for ten minutes about something that had happened at school, then sit quietly for another ten without seeming to need a reason. When she visited, she sometimes rearranged the cushions, complained about his lack of snacks, and left a hair tie on the table. Itsuki had stopped returning the hair ties. They appeared again the next week anyway.

After dinner, she brought out the cake.

It was small, with strawberries arranged around the top and a thin chocolate plate that read *Happy Birthday, Nanami*. The writing leaned slightly to the left.

“Sixteen,” Itsuki said.

“Don't say it like that.”

“Like what?”

“Like you're announcing a weather report.”

“Sixteen degrees. Light rain.”

She laughed, then shook her head.

He found two plates. Nanami cut the cake with more care than the occasion required, making sure each slice had a strawberry. She gave him the slightly larger one.

“You're the birthday person,” he said.

“I know. That's why I'm allowed to decide.”

“Convenient.”

“Very.”

They ate slowly.

When the plates were empty, Nanami leaned back against the sofa. Her gaze moved to the refrigerator, where the blue fish magnet held Itsuki's timetable in place.

“Your school starts tomorrow, right?”

“Orientation. Classes begin next week.”

“Third year.”

“Unfortunately.”

“Graduation year.”

“That's what the timetable says.”

“You really haven't thought about what comes after?”

“Not today.”

“That's not an answer.”

“It's the answer I have.”

Nanami considered him. She had inherited their father's habit of looking at a person as if she could hear the thought they had decided not to say. It was a habit Itsuki disliked because it occasionally worked.

“I'm not asking you to decide tonight,” she said. “Just don't let the year finish before you start thinking.”

“I'll put it on the calendar.”

“Don't.”

“Too late. April first: think about the future.”

She threw a cushion at him.

He caught it.

For a while, the apartment settled into the comfortable quiet that followed a meal. Nanami checked her phone. Itsuki washed the plates. The ordinary tasks made a small rhythm: water, ceramic, the cupboard door closing, a message notification from somewhere in the room.

His phone vibrated on the counter.

A message from their father.

*Happy birthday to Nanami. I hope she had a good day. Tell her I will call after work tomorrow. And Itsuki—your school year starts soon. Take care of yourself.*

Itsuki read it twice, then placed the phone face down.

Nanami had seen the name.

“Dad says he'll call tomorrow?”

“He says he'll call after work.”

“That's what I said.”

“You asked a question. I answered it.”

She stood and came to the kitchen. “Did you tell him I was here?”

“He knows.”

“Of course he does.”

“He asked whether you were staying for dinner.”

“And you told him?”

“That you were bringing dinner.”

Nanami gave him a suspicious look. “You two talk about me like I'm a delivery service.”

“You did bring the food.”

“I'm never bringing you anything again.”

“Understood.”

She took the dish towel from his hand and dried the last plate.

Itsuki let her.

There were things about their family that had become easier to live around than to explain. Nanami's father had once been the person who came home to the same house as both of them. Their mother had later left that marriage and built another household. Itsuki had not followed her into it. Nanami moved between places more often, trying to make each arrangement feel normal without pretending they were the same.

No one had asked Itsuki to stop caring about the man who had raised him for years. No one had asked Nanami to choose one home over another. That did not mean the arrangements were simple.

But tonight was Nanami's birthday.

There was no reason to turn a cake into a family meeting.

When the kitchen was clean, Nanami retrieved her cardigan from the chair.

“I should go.”

“It's getting cold.”

“I have a coat.”

“You left it in the entrance.”

“Then the problem is solved.”

She put it on, checked her bag, and reached for the door. Before opening it, she turned back.

“Tomorrow is your birthday.”

“I know.”

“Eighteen.”

“Apparently.”

“Do you want anything?”

“Peace and quiet.”

“You already have those.”

“I'd like to keep them.”

Nanami smiled. “I'll see what I can do.”

“That sounds threatening.”

“It is.”

She opened the door, then leaned back into the apartment.

“Happy early birthday, Onii-chan.”

“It's not early if it's tomorrow.”

“Then I'll say it again tomorrow.”

“You don't have to.”

“I know.”

She left before he could answer.

Itsuki locked the door and stood in the entrance for a moment. The apartment returned to its usual quiet. On the table, two plates waited to be put away. A few crumbs remained beside the cake box. The blue fish magnet held tomorrow's timetable against the refrigerator.

Nothing had changed.

That was usually what he wanted.

Still, when he looked at the calendar, he found himself noticing the date.

April 1.

The last day before his final year began.

He turned off the kitchen light.

# Chapter One — A Perfectly Ordinary Start

Itsuki turned eighteen at 12:00 a.m.

Nothing happened.

No sudden wisdom arrived. No hidden talent unlocked itself. The ceiling remained the same shade of off-white, the refrigerator continued its low mechanical hum, and the neighbour upstairs dropped something heavy at 12:07.

Itsuki lay on his back and considered whether adulthood was supposed to be louder.

At 6:40, his alarm answered with a cheerful electronic melody that sounded increasingly unreasonable.

He turned it off.

The first day of April was cold enough that he wore a light jacket over his shirt. He made toast, boiled water for tea, and checked his school bag. His timetable was still pinned to the refrigerator, though the date had changed.

A message waited on his phone.

*Happy birthday, Itsuki. Eighteen already. I hope this year is kind to you. Please eat properly. — Mom*

He read it, then placed the phone beside the kettle.

A second message arrived before he finished breakfast.

*Happy birthday. You're officially an adult, so I expect you to become more responsible. — Nanami*

Itsuki typed back.

*I'll try to become less responsible instead.*

Her reply came almost immediately.

*I knew you would disappoint me.*

He put the phone away.

The school stood on a gentle slope beyond the station district. Hoshimiya Municipal High School was not especially old or especially modern. Its main building had been repainted a few years earlier, but the older gymnasium still had faded court markings and windows that rattled when the wind came from the north. The grounds were wide enough for a proper sports field and a row of cherry trees, though the bicycle parking area always seemed to be full.

Itsuki arrived ten minutes before the orientation assembly.

Students gathered in the courtyard in loose groups. Some had already found their friends. Others stood beside the class lists, scanning names and pointing at the paper as if the arrangement might change if they looked long enough.

Itsuki checked the board.

Class 3-B.

He found his name halfway down the list.

There were a few familiar names. One belonged to a boy from his second-year English class. Another belonged to a girl who had once borrowed his notes and returned them with a packet of biscuits.

He did not see anyone he particularly needed to find.

That was fine.

“Morning.”

A voice came from behind him.

Itsuki turned.

Riko Tachibana stood with one hand raised in greeting. Her short caramel-brown hair was tucked behind one ear, and her golden-brown eyes carried the bright, amused look of someone who had already decided the day would be entertaining.

“Morning,” he said.

She leaned sideways to read the class list over his shoulder.

“Three-B. Same class.”

“I noticed.”

“You don't look happy.”

“I was trying not to look anything.”

“That's your natural expression.”

“Then it's working.”

Riko laughed. She had been doing this for long enough that Itsuki no longer remembered which of them had first decided they were friends. He suspected she had simply started talking to him one day and refused to stop. She was one of the few people who could turn his silence into a conversation without making him feel obliged to fill it.

“Did you do anything for your birthday?” she asked.

“It's today.”

“I know.”

“Then why are you asking?”

“To see if you had a party.”

“I ate toast.”

Riko stared at him.

“That's tragic.”

“It was good toast.”

“I'm sure the bread was honoured.”

“Thank you.”

She narrowed her eyes. “You're getting sarcastic earlier than usual.”

“It's a new-year improvement.”

“Maybe eighteen suits you.”

“It's been less than nine hours.”

“Still.”

A group of students passed behind them. Riko moved aside to let them through, then glanced at the class list again.

“Do you know anyone else in our class?”

“Some people.”

“That's not useful.”

“It's accurate.”

“Fine. I'll do the social work.”

“You enjoy it.”

“I enjoy making people regret underestimating me.”

“That's different.”

“Not really.”

The assembly bell rang.

They walked toward the gym with the crowd. Riko talked about a teacher who had apparently been assigned to their year again. Itsuki listened and occasionally gave a short response. He had no strong opinion about the teacher. He had no strong opinion about most things before breakfast.

The gym smelled faintly of floor polish and old wood. Students sat by class while teachers moved along the aisles, checking attendance. Itsuki took a place near the end of the third-year row. Riko sat two spaces away and immediately began whispering commentary about the principal's microphone.

The principal spoke for several minutes about responsibility, opportunity, and the importance of making the most of one's final year.

Itsuki looked at the stage clock.

He had always respected clocks. They did not ask him to make the most of anything.

After the assembly, Class 3-B gathered in their assigned room. The windows faced the sports field, where a groundskeeper was collecting fallen branches. A faint smell of chalk lingered near the blackboard.

Their homeroom teacher, Mr. Hasegawa, introduced himself. He was in his early forties, with a neat haircut and the tired but patient expression of someone who had spent enough years teaching teenagers to recognise the difference between a quiet student and a sleeping one.

He read out the class responsibilities, explained the schedule, and handed out forms.

“Choose your committee preferences by the end of the week,” he said. “You don't need to volunteer for everything. But do choose something. This is your final year, and the school still needs people to keep things running.”

Riko raised her hand.

“Does choosing nothing count as a choice?”

“It counts as a conversation with me after class.”

“Understood.”

Itsuki kept his eyes on the form.

There were several committee options. Library, health, class events, student council support, festival preparation. He marked library as his first preference, mostly because it seemed unlikely to involve a microphone.

Riko leaned over.

“Library?”

“It's quiet.”

“That's your entire reason?”

“It's a good reason.”

“You should choose festival preparation. It would be funny.”

“For whom?”

“For me.”

“Then I see the problem.”

She grinned and marked something on her own form.

A few minutes later, Mr. Hasegawa began collecting the papers. He stopped beside Itsuki's desk.

“Kurose.”

Itsuki looked up.

“Your English test from last term was strong. Not the highest score in the year, but your written explanations were clear.”

“Thank you.”

“Would you be willing to help with the class study-support board this term? Nothing complicated. Just examples of how to approach the reading questions.”

Itsuki glanced at the form.

“I'm not sure I'm the best person for that.”

“Why not?”

“Someone who scores higher might be better.”

“Someone who scores higher might also explain less clearly.”

That was a reasonable point. Itsuki disliked reasonable points because they were difficult to argue with.

“I'll think about it,” he said.

“That's all I'm asking.”

Mr. Hasegawa moved on.

Riko waited until he was out of earshot.

“Study-support board?”

“Apparently.”

“Look at you. A public service.”

“I'm considering a career change.”

“To what?”

“Unemployment.”

“Stable hours.”

“Exactly.”

The morning passed in forms, schedules, and introductions. Itsuki learned that the boy sitting in front of him was named Sota Miyake and that he played basketball. The girl by the window, Emi Naruse, had transferred from another class and was already organising a list of people willing to help with the classroom display.

He also noticed a familiar name on the seating chart.

Ayane Fujimoto.

She was in the same class.

Itsuki found her near the window after lunch. She was speaking with two girls, her shoulder-length black hair tucked behind one ear. When she turned and noticed him, her expression brightened with recognition.

“It'suki.”

He stopped.

“Fujimoto.”

The girls beside her exchanged a quick glance and moved away with the tact of people who knew they had arrived in the middle of something.

Ayane smiled, but there was a small hesitation beneath it.

“It's been a while.”

“We saw each other at New Year's.”

“That's three months ago.”

“That's a while.”

She laughed softly. “I suppose it is.”

They had known each other since they were small enough to sit under the same dining table during family gatherings. Their parents had been close for years. At one point, Itsuki had spent so many afternoons at Ayane's house that he knew which floorboard creaked outside the kitchen.

That had been a long time ago.

They still spoke when their families met. They still knew each other's names, birthdays, and general habits. But there were gaps now—ordinary gaps, made from school schedules and different friends and the assumption that the other person was busy.

“How was spring break?” Ayane asked.

“Quiet.”

“That's good.”

“How was yours?”

“Busy. My mother had me help with the neighbourhood association, and I had to organise some things for school.”

“Sounds busy.”

“It was.”

A pause settled between them.

Neither seemed to know what to do with it.

Ayane glanced at his school bag. “You're still carrying the same one.”

“It's still a bag.”

“I meant you haven't changed it.”

“It's not broken.”

She smiled again. “Right.”

Itsuki realised he had made the conversation more difficult than it needed to be.

“Your hair's a little shorter,” he said.

Ayane touched the ends of it. “I had it trimmed last month.”

“It suits you.”

Her eyes widened slightly, then softened.

“Thank you.”

He looked toward the window.

The compliment had been a normal thing to say. He was not sure why it felt like he had stepped onto an unfamiliar staircase.

Before either of them could continue, the classroom door opened and Mr. Hasegawa called for everyone to return to their seats.

Ayane gave him a small nod.

“See you later, Itsuki.”

“Yeah.”

He returned to his desk.

Riko was waiting with her chin resting on one hand.

“You talked to Fujimoto.”

“She talked to me.”

“Important distinction.”

“Very.”

“Childhood friend?”

“Family friend.”

“That's a different answer.”

“It's the accurate one.”

Riko studied him for a moment, then smiled in a way that suggested she had stored the conversation for future use.

Itsuki looked down at his timetable.

The first day of his final year had been uneventful.

He had met his teacher, confirmed his class, received several forms, and spoken to two people he already knew. He had also turned eighteen, though the event had failed to produce any noticeable improvement in his circumstances.

A perfectly ordinary start.

He decided to count that as a success.

# Chapter Two — The People Who Don't Need Invitations

Riko had a talent for appearing in places where she had not been invited.

It was not that she ignored invitations. She simply treated their absence as a technicality.

At 3:18 p.m., Itsuki discovered her sitting on the low wall beside the school bicycle parking area, eating a melon bread she had apparently acquired from somewhere.

He stopped.

She looked up.

“Good afternoon.”

“School ended twenty minutes ago.”

“Then you're late.”

“For what?”

“Your appointment.”

“I don't have one.”

“That's why you're late.”

Itsuki considered walking away. Riko lifted the second melon bread from the paper bag and held it out.

He stopped considering.

“Bribery,” he said.

“Hospitality.”

“There's a difference.”

“Not when it works.”

He accepted the bread.

The afternoon sun had warmed the concrete wall. Students moved past them in small groups, their voices drifting toward the main gate. A baseball club member jogged across the field with a bag over one shoulder. Somewhere behind the gym, someone was practising the same brass passage repeatedly, each attempt ending at the same uncertain note.

Riko swung one leg.

“Are you going straight home?”

“Yes.”

“That's boring.”

“It's a destination, not a performance.”

“Come to the shopping street with me.”

“Why?”

“I need to buy something.”

“You can buy it without me.”

“I could.”

“Then I don't see the difficulty.”

She looked at him as if he had disappointed her personally.

“It's a heavy thing.”

“What is it?”

“I don't know yet.”

“That sounds difficult.”

“Exactly. I need a second opinion.”

Itsuki took a bite of the bread. It was sweet enough to qualify as a small argument against his usual preference for plain food.

“What are you buying?”

“Something for the classroom.”

“You don't have a classroom assignment.”

“I might.”

“That's not how assignments work.”

“Not with that attitude.”

He should have gone home. There were dishes in the sink from breakfast, laundry to bring in, and an English workbook he had promised himself he would finish before the weekend. None of those tasks was urgent.

That was how Riko won most of their arguments. She did not need a convincing reason. She only needed the alternative to be slightly less interesting.

“Fine,” he said.

Riko hopped down from the wall.

“Excellent. You're learning to live.”

“I was doing that before I met you.”

“Debatable.”

They walked toward the station district.

The shopping street was narrow, with low buildings and shopfronts that had accumulated their own histories. A stationery store displayed notebooks in the window. The florist had buckets of pale flowers arranged outside. A small café had placed two chairs beneath its awning, though no one was sitting in them. At the far end, the road widened near a crossing where the traffic light took an unreasonable amount of time to change.

Riko stopped outside the stationery store.

“Pens,” she announced.

“You brought me here for pens?”

“I said I needed to buy something.”

“You said it was heavy.”

“The decision is heavy.”

Itsuki looked at the store window. A display of coloured markers stood beside a stack of poster paper.

“Get black.”

“Why?”

“Because you use black.”

“That's a very narrow view of the world.”

“It's a pen.”

Riko opened the door.

A small bell rang above them. Inside, the shop smelled of paper and plastic packaging. The owner greeted them from behind the counter. Riko answered brightly, then wandered toward the display shelves.

She picked up a set of pastel markers, put them down, chose a pack of black pens, then returned to the markers.

Itsuki stood near the notebooks.

“You're not helping,” she said.

“You haven't asked for help.”

“I'm asking now.”

“Buy the pens.”

“What if I regret it?”

“Then you'll have several pens to regret.”

She laughed, but she bought the markers.

At the counter, she also picked up a pack of plain sticky notes. Itsuki noticed the corner of a folded worksheet sticking out of her bag.

“Homework?” he asked.

“Evidence of an unfortunate system.”

“Math?”

“Don't say it like that.”

“Like what?”

“Like you know.”

He looked at the worksheet. There were several problems marked in red, not all of them wrong.

“You missed the negative sign here.”

Riko followed his finger.

“That's a decorative mark.”

“It's part of the equation.”

“It's ruining the composition.”

“It's a minus sign.”

“Exactly. Very negative.”

He returned the worksheet.

Riko paid, thanked the shop owner, and stepped outside. She walked a little more slowly than before, turning the packet of markers over in her hands.

“You could ask Hasegawa-sensei for help,” Itsuki said.

“I could.”

“You don't have to pretend the work is impossible.”

“I don't.”

“You just called the minus sign decorative.”

“That's because it is.”

He did not press the point. Riko was not stupid. She could understand the material when she wanted to, but she often lost patience halfway through a problem and decided the problem had personally offended her.

They stopped at a vending machine near the corner. Riko bought a canned peach drink. Itsuki chose tea.

“Are you going to help with the study board?” she asked.

“Maybe.”

“You should.”

“Why?”

“Because you're good at explaining things.”

“I've explained one minus sign.”

“And I understood it.”

“You've rejected it.”

“Understanding and acceptance are different.”

Itsuki opened his tea.

Riko looked down the street. A group of younger students passed, their bags bouncing against their backs. One of them called to a friend across the pavement. The friend waved both arms.

“Do you ever think about what you'll do after graduation?” Riko asked.

“Not often.”

“Why not?”

“Because it's April.”

“That's not an answer.”

“It's the current month.”

She rolled her eyes.

“You're impossible.”

“I've been told.”

“By me.”

“Frequently.”

Riko took a sip from her drink. Her expression had gone thoughtful, though she did not seem unhappy.

“My parents already have a plan for me,” she said.

Itsuki glanced at her.

“School?”

“Everything. School, grades, what I should do after. They have a list.”

“Do you agree with it?”

“Sometimes.”

“And when you don't?”

She smiled.

“Then I come to school and become a public nuisance.”

“That seems unrelated.”

“It's very related.”

She said it lightly. Itsuki did not ask what she meant. There were parts of Riko's life he knew only in outline. She was lively at school, quick with a joke, and almost impossible to embarrass. Her family was strict, she had once mentioned. That was all.

A phone vibrated in her pocket.

Riko checked the screen. Her expression changed—not dramatically, but enough for Itsuki to notice. The smile settled into something more polite.

“It's my mother,” she said.

She answered.

“Hello, Mom. Yes, I'm on my way. I bought the things for school. No, I haven't forgotten the workbook. I'll be home before dinner.”

Her voice was softer than the one she used with him. She listened, gave a few short replies, and promised to send a message when she reached the station.

When the call ended, she returned the phone to her pocket.

“Everything all right?” Itsuki asked.

“Of course.”

“You sound different.”

“Do I?”

“A little.”

Riko shrugged.

“That's because I have a phone voice.”

“An entire voice?”

“Several. You're only familiar with the one I use to bother you.”

“I suspected there were others.”

She nudged his shoulder with hers.

“Don't sound so disappointed.”

They continued toward the station. At the crossing, Riko waited beside him while the signal stayed red.

“Thanks for coming,” she said.

“You bought pens.”

“And you provided emotional support.”

“I corrected a minus sign.”

“Exactly. You were there for me.”

The light changed.

Riko crossed first, waving without looking back. Itsuki followed at a more ordinary pace.

At the station entrance, they separated. She went toward the bus stop; he headed for the local train platform.

His phone buzzed before he reached the ticket gate.

A message from Riko.

*Don't forget your birthday cake tomorrow. You owe me a slice.*

Itsuki typed back.

*You already ate the cake.*

*That was Nanami's cake. I'm talking about yours.*

*I don't have one.*

*Then buy one.*

He put the phone away.

It was possible, he thought, that the final year would be less quiet than he had planned.

He decided not to mention that to anyone.

# Chapter Three — A Familiar Name on the Attendance Sheet

The first family gathering of the new school year was not meant to be a celebration.

Itsuki knew this because his mother had said so three times.

“It's just dinner,” she had told him over the phone. “Ayane's parents are coming, and I thought it would be nice if you joined us.”

“Why?”

“Because we haven't all eaten together in a while.”

“We ate together at New Year's.”

“That was three months ago.”

“Nanami used the same argument yesterday.”

His mother had gone quiet for half a second.

“Then perhaps she's right.”

Itsuki had considered refusing. He did not dislike Ayane's parents. He had known them for most of his life, and they had always treated him kindly. The difficulty was not the people. It was the place.

His mother's current home was a house he had never chosen to live in, shared with a man he had never chosen to call family. The third husband was polite. He asked about school, offered food, and made an effort to include Itsuki in conversations. Itsuki understood that those were decent things to do.

He also understood that politeness did not create closeness by itself.

His mother had eventually suggested that dinner could be held at the Fujimotos' house instead. Itsuki agreed.

So on Saturday evening, he took the train two stops north and walked through a residential neighbourhood where the houses stood close together behind low fences. The air smelled faintly of cut grass and cooking oil. A child was riding a small bicycle in circles at the end of the street.

The Fujimoto house looked much as he remembered it. The front garden had changed; a row of potted herbs now stood beside the steps. The wind chime near the entrance was the same one that had been there when he was in primary school.

Ayane opened the door.

“Itsuki.”

“Evening.”

She wore a soft yellow cardigan over a simple dress. Her hair was tucked behind one ear, and she looked as if she had been about to go back to the kitchen.

“You came.”

“Your mother invited me.”

Ayane smiled. “I know. I just wasn't sure if you would.”

There was no accusation in it. That made it harder to answer.

“Your house is close,” he said.

“That's true.”

She stepped aside to let him in.

The familiar smell of the entranceway reached him first: wood polish, laundry detergent, and something simmering in the kitchen. His shoes found their old place beside the cabinet without him having to think about it.

Ayane noticed.

“You still remember.”

“It's the only place the shoes fit.”

She laughed.

The living room was already occupied. Ayane's father stood near the table, talking with Itsuki's mother. Her mother was bringing dishes from the kitchen. The third husband had not come; Itsuki's mother had said he had a work commitment. Itsuki was relieved, though he did not say so.

His mother turned when he entered.

“Itsuki. Thank you for coming.”

“Hello.”

She looked at him for a moment, as if checking whether he seemed well. Then she reached up and adjusted the collar of his jacket.

“You've grown your hair out.”

“It's the same length.”

“It looks longer.”

“Maybe the collar is different.”

She lowered her hand.

“Have you been eating properly?”

“Yes.”

“Really?”

“Most days.”

His mother sighed. Ayane's father laughed from the table.

“He's always been like that. If you ask him whether he's eaten, he'll tell you the number of meals rather than what they were.”

“That's useful information,” Itsuki said.

“It isn't,” his mother replied.

Dinner was warm and ordinary. Ayane's mother had made grilled fish, rice, miso soup, and a small dish of vegetables. They talked about the new school year, the neighbourhood, and a relative who had recently moved closer to the station. Itsuki answered when asked and otherwise listened.

Ayane sat across from him.

She had become more comfortable speaking with the adults than he remembered. She helped her mother serve food, asked about Itsuki's school, and laughed at her father's jokes. When she spoke to Itsuki directly, however, she still paused a little before each question.

“Are you in the same class as Riko?” she asked.

“Yeah.”

“She's the one who always teases you, right?”

“That's one description.”

“I remember her from the sports festival last year.”

“She was probably causing trouble.”

Ayane smiled. “Probably.”

“She's not as bad as people say,” Itsuki added.

He was not sure why he said it. Riko was capable of defending herself. She would probably have found it funny that he was doing so.

Ayane looked at him with a small expression of surprise.

“You sound close.”

“We're friends.”

“That's good.”

The conversation moved on.

After dinner, Ayane's mother asked them to bring a box of old photo albums from the storage room. Itsuki followed Ayane down the hall.

The storage room was narrow and cool. A cardboard box sat on the lower shelf, marked with a faded label. Ayane crouched and pulled it forward.

“Careful,” Itsuki said. “It's heavier than it looks.”

“I know. We put the albums in last year.”

“You could have asked your father.”

“He's talking to your mother.”

“Then you could have asked me.”

She glanced at him.

“I did.”

He looked at the box.

“Fair.”

They lifted it together and carried it to the living room. Ayane set it on the low table and opened the lid.

Inside were several albums, envelopes of printed photographs, and a small stack of school drawings. Ayane picked up the top album.

“This one is from primary school.”

Itsuki sat beside the table.

The first few pages showed a class trip, a sports day, and a summer festival. There were photographs of children in hats, children holding paper fans, children standing in rows with expressions of forced patience.

Then Ayane turned a page.

Itsuki was there.

He was younger, his hair shorter, his expression less carefully blank. He stood beside Ayane beneath a cherry tree. Both of them held paper cups. Ayane was laughing at something outside the frame.

“I remember that day,” she said.

“I don't.”

“You spilled juice on your shirt.”

“That sounds familiar.”

“You said the stain looked like a map.”

“I might have been right.”

“You were very proud of it.”

“I was seven.”

Ayane laughed.

The next photograph showed them sitting on the floor of the Fujimoto living room, surrounded by building blocks. Itsuki had one hand on a half-finished tower. Ayane was leaning toward him, holding a piece that did not seem to fit anywhere.

“We used to play here all the time,” she said.

“Your house had more space.”

“Your apartment was smaller.”

“We didn't live in the apartment then.”

“I know.”

She turned another page.

There were more photographs. A school trip. A birthday party. A rainy afternoon with several children gathered around a board game. Their families appeared in the background, smiling at the camera.

Itsuki recognised the people, but the moments came back only in fragments.

A smell. A voice. A colour.

He remembered being close to Ayane. He did not remember the exact day they had stopped being close.

Perhaps there had not been one.

“Do you ever miss it?” Ayane asked.

He looked at her.

“The old days?”

She nodded.

“Sometimes.”

“Me too.”

She closed the album carefully.

“I thought maybe we could talk more this year,” she said. “Since we're in the same class.”

“That's fine.”

Ayane's expression softened, but she did not look entirely satisfied.

“Not because we have to,” she added. “I just thought it might be nice.”

Itsuki understood the difference. He was not sure he knew how to respond to it.

“Yeah,” he said. “It might.”

It was not a promise. It was not a rejection.

For now, it was honest.

They carried the albums back to the living room. Their parents had moved to the kitchen, where Itsuki's mother was helping clear the dishes. Ayane returned the box to the table.

When Itsuki left later that evening, Ayane walked him to the front door.

“See you at school,” she said.

“See you.”

She hesitated, then smiled.

“And happy birthday, by the way. I didn't get to say it on Wednesday.”

“Thanks.”

“I hope it was good.”

“It was ordinary.”

“That's not necessarily bad.”

“No,” he said. “I suppose not.”

He stepped outside.

The wind chime moved once behind him, making a small glass sound.

On the walk back to the station, he thought about the photographs. The children in them seemed familiar, but not quite like people he knew now. He wondered whether Ayane felt the same way.

He did not have an answer.

The train arrived before he reached one.


# Chapter Four — The Girl Everyone Seems to Know

Akari Fujisaki arrived at school with three people talking to her.

This was not unusual.

Itsuki had seen her in the corridor before, usually surrounded by classmates or being stopped by someone who wanted to ask a question. She was easy to recognise even from a distance: long dark reddish-brown hair, a friendly smile, and the sort of relaxed posture that made it look as if she had never once worried about where to put her hands.

He knew her name because everyone knew her name.

That was not the same as knowing her.

On Thursday morning, she stood near the shoe lockers, listening to a girl from another class explain something with great urgency. Akari nodded, asked a question, and laughed at the answer. When another student approached, she turned to include him in the conversation without making the first girl feel dismissed.

It looked effortless.

Itsuki suspected it wasn't.

He changed his indoor shoes and headed upstairs.

“Morning, Kurose.”

The voice belonged to Miyake, who was carrying a sports bag over one shoulder.

“Morning.”

“You know Fujisaki?”

Itsuki followed his glance toward the shoe lockers.

“Not really.”

“She's in 3-A, right? I heard she's helping with the welcome event.”

“Maybe.”

Miyake looked at him.

“You don't know much.”

“That's why I said maybe.”

Miyake laughed and continued toward the stairs.

Itsuki entered Class 3-B. Riko was already at her desk, drawing something in the margin of a worksheet.

“Is that a cat?” he asked.

“It's a teacher.”

“Which one?”

“I'm still deciding.”

The drawing had pointed ears and a very displeased expression.

Itsuki sat down.

“Ambitious.”

“Thank you.”

The morning lessons passed without incident. During the break before lunch, the class representative announced that the student committee would be collecting suggestions for the spring welcome event. The event was a small school tradition for new first-year students: club displays, a guided tour, and a short performance in the gym.

Riko raised her hand.

“Can we suggest cancelling it?”

“No,” the representative said.

“Can we suggest cancelling the suggestion box?”

“No.”

“It's a hostile system.”

Itsuki continued writing the date at the top of his notes.

Across the courtyard, the welcome-event committee had gathered near the main entrance. Akari stood with two other students, holding a clipboard. She listened while one of them pointed toward the gym, then checked a list and made a note.

A first-year student approached them, looking uncertain. Akari stepped forward and spoke to her. The girl relaxed almost immediately.

Itsuki looked away.

There was nothing particularly interesting about someone being kind to a nervous student. It was simply the sort of thing that happened at school.

At lunch, Riko sat on the edge of his desk.

“Do you have plans after school?”

“Homework.”

“That's not a plan.”

“It's a scheduled activity.”

“Come help me carry something.”

“What?”

“Something.”

“Is it heavy?”

“Probably.”

“Then ask someone stronger.”

“I'm asking someone more available.”

“I'm not available.”

Riko looked at his empty lunch box.

“You're finished eating.”

“That's unrelated.”

She slid off the desk.

“Fine. I'll ask Miyake.”

“Good.”

She walked away, then turned around.

“Unless you're scared.”

Itsuki looked at her.

“Of carrying something?”

“Of being useful.”

“That's a serious accusation.”

“I know.”

He went after her.

They ended up helping the class representative move a box of event leaflets from the staff room to the student committee office. It was not heavy. Riko had exaggerated, which was a common feature of her requests.

The corridor outside the office was busy. Students passed carrying poster boards, stacks of paper, and a bundle of extension cords. A teacher asked them to keep the walkway clear.

Akari came out of the office with a clipboard tucked under one arm.

“Sorry—could you let me through?”

Riko stepped aside.

“Of course.”

Akari smiled at her. “Thanks.”

Her eyes moved briefly to Itsuki.

“Thank you.”

He nodded.

That was all.

She continued down the corridor with another committee member. Riko watched her go.

“She's nice,” she said.

“Seems so.”

“Everyone likes her.”

“Probably.”

“Do you think she knows everyone?”

“Not everyone.”

“Maybe she has a list.”

“Maybe.”

Riko turned to him. “You really have no curiosity.”

“I have some.”

“About?”

“Whether the box is going to be put down.”

Riko laughed and took the other side of it.

They carried the leaflets into the office.

Later that afternoon, Itsuki saw Akari again near the courtyard. She was speaking with a teacher, pointing to a page on her clipboard. The teacher nodded, then gave her another sheet. Akari looked at it, asked something, and wrote down the answer.

She seemed busy.

That was the only impression Itsuki took away.

He did not know that she had spent the previous evening reviewing a set of English questions until the words began to blur together. He did not know that she had asked her older cousin for advice about a future she was not sure she wanted. He did not know that being liked by so many people could make it difficult to say no to any of them.

Those things belonged to Akari's day, not his.

For now, they were students in the same school who happened to pass each other in the corridor.

That was enough.

# Chapter Five — A Quiet Seat by the Window

The library was the quietest place in the school, provided nobody had decided to whisper.

Itsuki preferred it to the classroom after lessons. The room was on the second floor of the old building, with tall windows facing the residential streets beyond the sports field. The shelves were arranged in a way that suggested several generations of librarians had disagreed about where books belonged. A small sign near the entrance asked students to return borrowed materials to the desk rather than reshelve them.

Itsuki approved of the sign.

He returned a book on modern Japanese history and checked the English section for a collection of short essays. He had not planned to borrow anything. He simply liked having something to read that did not come with a test date.

A chair moved softly near the windows.

He glanced over.

A girl sat at the far table, one hand resting beside an open notebook. Her hair was long and dark enough to appear almost blue where the afternoon light touched it. She wore a simple cardigan over her uniform and had arranged her books in a neat line.

Mio Kanzaki.

Itsuki knew the name from the class list. She was in 3-C. He had seen her in the library once or twice, usually alone.

She was reading.

That was not remarkable. It was a library.

He selected a book and moved toward the desk.

As he passed, he noticed that her notebook contained a small diagram. Several names were written in the margin, connected by lines. It looked like a family tree, or perhaps an outline for a history assignment.

Mio turned a page.

Itsuki kept walking.

At the desk, the librarian stamped the date inside the book.

“Back in two weeks,” she said.

“Thank you.”

He left the library.

The corridor outside was warmer than the room. Students were leaving in groups, and the noise from the courtyard had grown louder. Itsuki adjusted the strap of his bag and headed downstairs.

He did not think about Mio again until the following Tuesday.

He had stayed after class to finish a worksheet. When he reached the library, the same seat by the window was occupied.

Mio was not reading this time. She was looking out at the sports field, where a group of students were practising badminton under the late-afternoon sun. Her notebook lay open beside her, but she did not appear to be writing.

Itsuki chose a seat two tables away.

He worked through the first page of his English assignment. The passage was about urban planning, a subject he found more interesting than the questions made it seem. He underlined a sentence, wrote a short explanation, and checked the next paragraph.

A chair scraped softly.

He looked up.

Mio had stood and was returning a book to the shelf. She moved carefully, making sure the spine lined up with the others. Then she took another book from the shelf and returned to her seat.

No conversation.

No greeting.

A perfectly ordinary library afternoon.

When Itsuki finished, he closed his notebook and packed his things. As he passed the window table, Mio's pencil rolled off the edge.

It stopped near his shoe.

He picked it up and placed it on the corner of her desk.

“Thanks,” she said.

Her voice was quiet, but clear.

“Sure.”

That was the entirety of their conversation.

He left.

Outside, the sky had begun to turn pale gold. A train passed somewhere beyond the school buildings, its sound briefly rising above the traffic.

Itsuki walked toward the station.

He had learned one thing about Kanzaki Mio.

She used a mechanical pencil with a blue grip.

It was not a particularly useful fact.

He suspected he would forget it by tomorrow.

# Chapter Six — A City Made of Small Errands

Itsuki's apartment was close enough to school that he could walk if he felt like it and far enough away that he usually took the train.

The distinction mattered mostly in bad weather.

On Saturday morning, he walked to the shopping street with a list in his pocket. Rice, detergent, eggs, dish soap. He had written the list the previous night and folded it twice. The handwriting was neat. The order was not.

He bought rice first because it was heavy, then detergent because the store had a discount, then eggs because he had nearly forgotten them. At the small vegetable shop, the owner asked whether he was cooking for one.

“Usually.”

“You should eat more vegetables.”

“I do.”

The owner looked at the instant curry packet in his basket.

Itsuki looked at it too.

“Sometimes,” he amended.

The owner added two carrots to the bag.

He paid for them.

On the way home, he stopped at the convenience store near the station. A new poster advertised a spring campaign. Someone had drawn a small smile on the corner of the display card. It was probably a staff member. It was probably against the rules.

Itsuki bought tea and a rice ball.

Outside, he found a message from Nanami.

*Are you home?*

He typed back.

*Almost.*

*Good. I'm coming over.*

*You could have asked first.*

*I did. You answered.*

He looked at the message.

It was technically true.

“You're early.”

“I carried groceries.”

“That's a weak excuse.”

She took the lighter bag from his hand and followed him inside.

The first thing she did was open the refrigerator.

“Carrots.”

“Observant.”

“Eggs.”

“Very observant.”

“Rice.”

“I'm impressed.”

“Apparently.”

“I don't mind. She's good at making things, and I'm better at keeping the list organised.”

Another student wanted to include a small display about the history of the neighbourhood. “I know.”

“You can answer if you want.”

“I know.”

“Okay.”

“Probably.”

Itsuki locked the door.

He left the pencil mark.

# Chapter Seven — The Things We Don't Ask Twice

Rain began during the last period on Monday.

It tapped against the classroom windows while Mr. Hasegawa explained the week's schedule. By the time the final bell rang, the sports field had darkened and the path beside the gym was shining with water.

Itsuki had brought an umbrella.

Riko had not.

She stood near the shoe lockers, looking outside with an expression of deep personal betrayal.

“Forgot yours?” Itsuki asked.

“I was optimistic.”

“About the weather?”

“About the world.”

He opened his umbrella.

Riko looked at it.

“Are you offering?”

“No.”

“Good. I wouldn't want to accept.”

She stepped under it.

Itsuki glanced at her.

“You just accepted.”

“I changed my mind.”

They walked toward the station together. Riko lived in the opposite direction from the bus stop, but she had decided that the first part of the route was shared territory. She talked about a teacher who had assigned an essay over the weekend and a classmate who had written the title at the bottom of the page.

At the crossing, they separated.

“Don't catch a cold,” Riko said.

“Try not to lose your homework.”

“No promises.”

She ran toward the bus stop.

Itsuki continued along the pavement.

Near the entrance to the shopping street, he saw Ayane standing beneath the awning of a closed flower shop. She held a transparent umbrella in one hand and a paper bag in the other. A few drops of rain had caught in her hair.

“Itsuki?”

The conversation loosened a little.

“Do you remember this place?”

“Yeah.”

“Your mother used to bring snacks.”

“You called them tiny fish.”

“That seems accurate.”

Ayane smiled.

“I remember you used to line them up before eating them.”

Itsuki looked at her.

“I did?”

“Every time.”

“I don't remember that.”

“You were very particular.”

“Maybe I was hungry.”

“Maybe.”

They stood beneath their umbrellas for a moment, watching the rain collect in the shallow dips of the playground path.

“I used to think we'd always come here,” Ayane said.

Itsuki followed her gaze.

“Kids think things like that.”

“Do they?”

“Probably.”

She nodded, but did not seem convinced.

“I don't think we stopped being friends because anything happened,” she said. “We just got busy. Then it became normal not to call.”

Itsuki adjusted the umbrella handle.

“Yeah.”

“I wasn't sure if you wanted me to.”

He looked at her.

“Call?”

“Or talk more. I didn't know if you preferred being left alone.”

“I usually do.”

Ayane smiled faintly.

“I know.”

“Not always.”

She looked up.

Itsuki realised he had said more than he intended. He could have left it there, but the rain made the silence feel too noticeable.

“I don't mind talking,” he said. “I just don't always know what to say.”

Ayane's expression softened.

“That's okay.”

A train announcement sounded from the station.

They continued walking.

At the entrance, Ayane stopped beside the ticket machines.

“Thanks for walking with me.”

“We were going the same way.”

“Still.”

She shifted the paper bag to her other hand.

“Maybe we could have lunch together sometime. At school.”

“Sure.”

“Really?”

“It's lunch. I usually eat.”

Ayane laughed.

“Right. I'll ask you later.”

She went through the gate.

Itsuki watched her disappear into the crowd, then tapped his travel card against the reader.

The train was three minutes away.

He stood on the platform and listened to the rain against the roof.

He had not promised anything significant. Lunch was lunch. A conversation was a conversation. There was no reason to treat either as a turning point.

Still, he found himself hoping he would remember to say yes when she asked.

# Chapter Eight — A Test with Too Many Questions

The first English assessment of the year was scheduled for Friday.

It was not a major exam. Mr. Hasegawa called it a diagnostic test, which meant the school wanted to know what students remembered before the real work began.

Itsuki finished the reading section with twelve minutes left.

He checked his answers once, corrected a careless article, and turned the page over.

Across the room, Riko was staring at question fourteen.

She had been staring at question fourteen for some time.

When the bell rang, she put her pencil down with the careful movement of someone leaving a dangerous object behind.

“How was it?” Itsuki asked as they left the classroom.

“Unfair.”

“Which part?”

“All of it.”

“That's broad.”

“It's a broad injustice.”

He glanced at the worksheet in her hand.

“You missed the second passage?”

“I read it.”

“Then?”

“I disagreed with it.”

“It's a reading test.”

“Exactly. They should test whether the passage is worth reading.”

He did not answer.

Riko folded the paper and slipped it into her bag.

“Don't look at me like that.”

“Like what?”

“Like you're about to explain the answer.”

“I wasn't.”

“You were thinking about it.”

“Maybe.”

“Keep your thoughts to yourself.”

They reached the corridor outside the staff room. A few students were waiting to ask about their scores. Mr. Hasegawa stood near the door, speaking with another teacher.

“Ah, Kurose,” he called when he saw Itsuki. “A moment?”

Itsuki stopped.

Riko leaned toward him.

“Good luck with your promotion.”

“Go to class.”

“Cruel.”

She walked away.

Mr. Hasegawa held out a paper.

“You did well. Your answers are accurate, and your explanation for the final question is particularly clear.”

Itsuki looked at the page. A red mark circled the paragraph he had written.

“Thank you.”

“Would you mind if I used this as an example for the class? I'll remove your name.”

“That's fine.”

“Good. It may help some students see how to support an answer with the passage rather than guessing from the wording.”

Itsuki nodded.

Mr. Hasegawa placed the paper on his folder.

“By the way, Fujisaki from 3-A asked whether the school has any peer-support sessions for English. I told her we'd look into it.”

Itsuki did not know why the teacher had mentioned that.

“Okay.”

“She seems to be putting in effort. Some students have a good ear for language but struggle with reading structure. It can be frustrating.”

“Yeah.”

Mr. Hasegawa smiled.

“Enjoy the rest of your day.”

Itsuki returned to class.

He did not think much about the conversation.

At lunch, the class representative announced that the diagnostic results would be returned the following week. Riko asked whether the school would provide emotional compensation for low scores. The representative said no.

After lessons, Itsuki went to the library to return his essay collection.

Akari was there.

She stood near the reference shelves with a notebook open in one hand. Her expression was focused, and the usual group around her was absent. She read a paragraph, frowned, then tried again.

Itsuki approached the desk.

The librarian was sorting returned books. He placed his book on the counter and waited.

Akari looked up at the sound.

“Sorry,” she said, stepping aside.

“It's fine.”

She glanced at the book in his hand.

“Are you returning that?”

“Yeah.”

“Was it good?”

“Some of it.”

“That's a very strong review.”

“It's a collection.”

She smiled.

“Fair enough.”

The librarian stamped the return date. Itsuki moved away from the desk.

As he passed the reference shelves, he noticed that Akari's notebook contained a list of vocabulary words. Several had been underlined twice. She was not wasting time.

“Do you know what 'subsequently' means?” she asked suddenly.

Itsuki stopped.

“After that. Something happens later as a result of what came before.”

“That's what I thought.”

She looked at the sentence in the book.

“Then why does it sound wrong here?”

He read the paragraph from a respectful distance.

“The sentence is using it to connect two events, but the second one isn't really a consequence. It's just the next event.”

Akari looked at the page again.

“So it's not the word. It's the relationship between the sentences.”

“Right.”

She considered that, then made a small note.

“Thanks.”

“Sure.”

There was no dramatic change in her expression. She did not suddenly look at him as if she had discovered a hidden genius. She simply understood the sentence better than she had a minute earlier.

“Are you in 3-B?” she asked.

“Yeah.”

“I thought so. I'm Akari Fujisaki.”

“I know.”

Her smile turned a little amused.

“Of course. Everyone knows everyone here.”

“Not everyone.”

“Maybe not.”

She closed her notebook.

“Thanks again, Kurose.”

He nodded and left.

Outside the library, the late-afternoon light fell across the corridor floor. Itsuki adjusted his glasses and headed for the stairs.

He had answered a question.

That was all.

He did not know that Akari would remember the explanation later, when she was trying to understand why a paragraph refused to make sense. He did not know that she would begin to notice how patiently he listened when someone asked him something. And he certainly did not know that, a few weeks from now, she would consider asking him for help again.

For now, she was simply a classmate from 3-A.

And he had somewhere to be.



# Chapter Nine — The Committee Nobody Wanted

By the middle of May, the school had developed a new sound.

It was the sound of people asking where the forms were.

The first-year welcome event had passed, the class photographs had been taken, and the teachers had begun using the phrase “the first term is moving quickly” with increasing confidence. The school corridors were no longer full of students who looked lost. Everyone had found a route between classrooms, a preferred vending machine, and at least one place where they could sit without being asked to move.

Itsuki had found a place near the library windows.

Riko had found three places where she was not supposed to sit.

The school festival was still more than a month away, but preparation had begun in the form of a committee notice pinned beside the staff room. Each class would choose an activity, submit a plan, and assign students to the work. The notice included a list of approved options and a reminder that food preparation required supervision.

Riko read it over Itsuki's shoulder.

“Let's do a haunted house.”

“No.”

“You haven't heard the proposal.”

“I heard the first three words.”

“That's enough to make an informed decision.”

“Then we're done.”

She leaned closer to the paper.

“What about a café?”

“Someone will spill something.”

“That's why we have towels.”

“What about a quiz room?”

“People will complain.”

“A game booth?”

“People will cheat.”

Riko turned to him.

“You're very good at finding problems.”

“Thank you.”

“It's not a compliment.”

“I'll accept it anyway.”

Their class representative, Emi Naruse, approached with a clipboard.

“Have either of you decided what you'd like to help with?”

Riko pointed at Itsuki.

“He's our risk-assessment department.”

“I didn't agree to that.”

“You're doing a good job.”

Emi looked between them, then wrote something on her clipboard.

“I'll put you down for general preparation.”

Itsuki watched the pen move.

“That's not a role.”

“It is now,” Riko said.

Emi smiled apologetically. “We need people who can help move things, check supplies, and keep track of the schedule. You don't have to decide the actual activity yet.”

Itsuki considered asking to be removed.

Then he saw the stack of forms in Emi's arms and the way she was trying to keep them from sliding onto the floor.

“Fine,” he said. “I can help with the schedule.”

“Thank you.”

Riko gave him a pleased look.

“You're growing.”

“I'm being exploited.”

“Those are not mutually exclusive.”

The class eventually voted for a small café with a display of local photographs and a corner for simple games. It was not the most original idea, but it was manageable. Emi would coordinate the plan. Riko volunteered to handle decorations, which worried Itsuki more than the budget.

“What?” she said when he mentioned it.

“Nothing.”

“Your face says something.”

“My face is neutral.”

“Your face is filing a complaint.”

She had already begun sketching ideas in the corner of her notebook. Most involved paper lanterns, coloured signs, or something that looked suspiciously like a cardboard monster.

Itsuki pointed at the monster.

“What is that?”

“A mascot.”

“For a café?”

“It's a hungry customer.”

“That's not reassuring.”

“People like honesty.”

Across the room, Ayane was discussing the class activity with Emi. She had been assigned to help organise the display board and had already made a list of students who could bring photographs of the neighbourhood. When she noticed Itsuki looking, she gave him a small wave.

He returned it.

Riko noticed.

“Fujimoto's helping with the display.”

“I can see that.”

“You could help her.”

“I am helping the class.”

“That's not what I meant.”

Itsuki looked at her.

Riko smiled innocently.

He returned to the schedule.

The following week, the class began collecting ideas for the display. Ayane brought a folder of photographs from the local community centre. Most showed familiar streets: the station before its renovation, the shopping street during a summer event, the river path in winter. She explained where each picture had been taken and asked whether the class could use copies.

Itsuki recognised the small playground near the shopping street.

“That one hasn't changed much,” he said.

Ayane followed his gaze.

“No. The swings are newer.”

“The fence is the same.”

“I think so.”

They stood beside the display board while other students arranged the photographs.

“Do you want to help choose the captions?” she asked.

“Sure.”

They worked through the folder. Ayane wrote the first draft of each caption. Itsuki checked dates and spelling. They disagreed about whether one photograph showed the old station entrance or the bus terminal. A student from another class settled the question by recognising the sign in the background.

It was not a particularly meaningful afternoon.

That was why it went well.

By the time they finished, the display board had begun to look like something people might actually stop to read. Ayane thanked him, then asked whether he would be at school early the next morning.

“Probably.”

“Could you help me carry the remaining boards?”

“Sure.”

She smiled.

“Thanks.”

Riko appeared at his elbow.

“Look at you. Becoming indispensable.”

“I carried cardboard.”

“Every great career begins somewhere.”

Itsuki took the schedule from her.

“Your decorations are missing a materials list.”

Riko blinked.

Then she snatched the paper back.

“That's classified.”

“It's blank.”

“Classified blank.”

The preparations continued.

By the end of May, the class had chosen its colour scheme, submitted the activity plan, and received approval from the teacher in charge. The café would use a simple menu, the display would feature photographs of Hoshimiya, and the game corner would have a ring toss and a small prize table.

Riko's cardboard mascot had been rejected.

She claimed this was censorship.

Itsuki considered it an improvement.

One afternoon, as they were packing the display materials, Emi asked Itsuki to take a folder to the committee office.

“It's just the updated schedule,” she said. “Could you hand it to Fujisaki from 3-A? She's helping coordinate the shared equipment list.”

Itsuki looked at the folder.

“Why me?”

“Because you're going that way.”

He was not.

But the committee office was on the same floor as the library, and arguing would take longer than walking.

He took the folder.

The office door was open. Akari stood beside a table covered with forms and supply lists. Two other students were comparing the number of extension cords needed for the classrooms.

“Excuse me,” Itsuki said.

Akari turned.

“Oh, hello.”

He held out the folder.

“From 3-B. The updated schedule.”

“Thank you.”

She accepted it and opened the cover.

“Emi sent this?”

“Yeah.”

Akari checked the first page, then nodded.

“That's much clearer. The previous version had two classes listed for the same storage room.”

“That's probably why she changed it.”

“Could you tell her thanks?”

“Sure.”

Akari looked up.

“Kurose, right?”

He nodded.

“Thanks for bringing it over.”

“No problem.”

He left.

It was the second time they had spoken for more than a few words. Neither of them seemed to think that made it important.

Outside the office, the corridor was crowded with students carrying supplies. Itsuki moved aside to let a group pass.

A familiar voice called his name.

“Hey.”

Mio Kanzaki stood near the library entrance, holding two books against her chest. Her long midnight-blue hair fell over one shoulder. She gave a small nod.

Itsuki nodded back.

“Hi.”

She walked past him toward the office.

That was all.

The school year continued.

# Chapter Ten — Before the Doors Open

The final week of June arrived with a heat that seemed to have been waiting behind the calendar.

By Thursday, the classrooms were warm before the first lesson. Students opened the windows, then complained when the wind carried in dust from the sports field. The school had placed extra water dispensers near the gym, and teachers reminded everyone to take breaks during festival preparation.

Itsuki's class had been assigned the old home-economics room for storage. The room contained folding tables, spare chairs, and a collection of items that nobody had used in years. A faded poster about food hygiene still hung beside the sink.

Riko stood in the doorway, looking around.

“It's perfect.”

“It's dusty.”

“Perfectly dusty.”

“That's not a feature.”

She walked toward a stack of cardboard boxes.

“Where do you want these?”

“Not there.”

“They seem fine.”

They had spent the morning preparing the class display. Ayane was checking the photograph captions. Emi was updating the equipment schedule. Riko was decorating the café signs with coloured paper. Itsuki had been asked to make a list of supplies and confirm that the class had enough extension cords, tape, and tablecloths.

“These photographs look nice.”

Ayane smiled.

“Thanks. They're from around Hoshimiya.”

“It's strange how familiar places look different in photographs.”

Akari smiled.

“That's a good way to put it.”

“She's nice.”

“I know.”

“Understood.”

“Are you excited?”

“It's a school event.”

“That's not an answer.”

“You really don't know how to enjoy anything in advance.”

“That's a terrible attitude.”

A message from Nanami.

*Are you going to the festival tomorrow?*

He typed back.

*I have to help with the class booth.*

*Then I'll come.*

*You have your own school.*

*It's Saturday.*

*You still have things to do.*

*I can do them later.*

*I'll bring Yui if she's free.*

Itsuki looked at the screen.

*Don't make me responsible for your friends.*

*You're already responsible for me.*

That was not a legally recognised arrangement.

At home, he set his alarm for the next morning and placed his festival shirt over the back of a chair. He checked the schedule one last time, then left it on the table.

The day ahead was likely to be loud.

There would be visitors, questions, misplaced supplies, and Riko.

He turned off the light.

For once, he hoped the weather would stay clear.

# Chapter Twelve — The Corridor Between Crowds

Akari had not planned to leave her class.

She had planned to take the replacement menu board to the entrance, check that the sign stand was stable, and return before the next group of visitors arrived.

The plan had been simple.

Then the stand's lower support slipped.

The board tilted, struck the edge of a nearby table, and slid sideways. Akari caught it before it fell, but the movement sent a stack of printed menus onto the floor.

“Are you okay?” one of her classmates asked.

“I'm fine. The board's fine.”

The board was not fine. One corner had bent slightly, and the printed menu had come loose from its backing.

Akari looked toward the entrance.

The class had run out of spare stands. The committee room had another one, but the route was crowded. She asked a classmate to keep the counter running and carried the board toward the service corridor.

She did not want to ask the teacher for help. The problem was small, and the teacher was already dealing with a sound-system issue in the gym.

The corridor narrowed near the storage area.

A group of students carrying folding chairs came through from the opposite direction. Akari stepped aside to let them pass. Someone behind her tried to move around a trolley, and the crowd compressed.

The board caught against the wall.

“Sorry,” she said, turning sideways.

A student brushed past her shoulder. She shifted her grip, trying to keep the board from hitting anyone.

The trolley moved again.

One of its wheels caught against a raised strip of flooring. The person pushing it stopped suddenly. The students behind him did not.

Akari's heel slipped.

She caught the edge of the wall with her free hand. The board tilted, and a stack of menu sheets began sliding from beneath her arm.

A hand caught the top corner.

“Careful.”

The voice was calm.

Akari looked up.

Kurose Itsuki.

She recognised him from the other class. The quiet boy who had brought the updated schedule to the committee office. He was holding the board steady with one hand and gathering the loose sheets with the other.

“Thanks,” she said.

“Are you hurt?”

“No. I just lost my footing.”

The crowd moved around them.

Itsuki looked toward the service corridor.

“This way's blocked.”

“I noticed.”

“There should be another route through the side hall.”

“I was trying to get there.”

He nodded, as if that explained everything.

The board was awkward to carry alone. Akari adjusted her grip, but the bent corner caught against the wall again.

“I can take it,” Itsuki said.

“It's okay. I can manage.”

“You're holding the papers and the board.”

“I'm aware.”

He looked at the stack of menus, then at the corridor.

“Which one do you want to drop?”

Akari stared at him.

Then she laughed once, despite herself.

“The papers.”

“Good choice.”

He took the board.

It was wider than it looked. Itsuki turned it carefully, keeping the bent corner away from the wall. Akari gathered the loose sheets and followed him toward the side hall.

The corridor opened near the old music room. The crowd noise softened. A window stood open at the far end, letting in a warm breeze and the distant sound of the gym announcement.

Akari stopped to straighten the papers.

“Sorry,” she said. “I didn't mean to make you carry everything.”

“It's fine.”

“You were going somewhere.”

“Back to my class.”

“Then I interrupted you.”

“I was already walking.”

She looked at him, unsure whether he was being polite or simply stating a fact.

His expression gave her no help.

“Thanks,” she said again.

He nodded.

Akari checked the board. The corner had not bent further. The printed menu could be fixed with tape, and the stand could be replaced.

“Could you hold this for a second?” she asked, offering him the papers.

He took them.

She crouched to retrieve a small roll of tape that had fallen from her bag. When she stood, the hallway seemed to tilt slightly—not from injury, but from the sudden rush of moving through the crowd and then stopping.

Itsuki noticed.

“Take a moment.”

“I'm fine.”

“I didn't say you weren't.”

She looked at him.

He was not smiling. He was not making a joke. He simply stood there, holding the papers, waiting for her to finish.

Akari let out a breath.

“Sorry. It's been a busy morning.”

“Looks like it.”

She adjusted the strap of her bag.

“I'm Akari Fujisaki, by the way.”

“I know.”

“Right. Everyone knows everyone.”

“Not everyone.”

She smiled faintly.

“You said that before.”

“I did?”

“At the committee office? Maybe not. I don't know.”

Itsuki glanced at the board.

“Should we get this to the stand?”

“Yes. Thank you.”

They walked toward the service entrance.

At the far end of the corridor, two students from another class came around the corner. One was carrying a tray of paper cups. The other held a camera, apparently taking pictures for the school festival page.

They saw Akari and Itsuki approaching together.

The student with the tray slowed.

“Fujisaki?”

Akari turned.

“Oh—hi.”

The student looked at the board in Itsuki's hands, then at the papers Akari was carrying.

“Are you moving the display?”

“Just fixing the stand.”

“Okay.”

The camera lifted briefly.

Itsuki did not notice.

Akari did.

She lowered her eyes and continued walking.

The side entrance opened into a small covered passage between buildings. The replacement stand was stored against the wall. Itsuki set the board down while Akari checked the support.

“That's better,” she said.

“Good.”

She taped the loose menu sheets back into place.

“I'm sorry I dragged you into this.”

“You didn't.”

“You were helping.”

“That's usually how it works.”

Akari looked up.

Then she laughed.

It was not a loud laugh, but it was genuine.

“Fair enough.”

She picked up the board.

Itsuki handed over the papers.

“Do you need help carrying it back?”

“No. I can manage now.”

He nodded.

“Then I'll go.”

“Wait.”

He stopped.

Akari looked toward the main corridor, where the crowd was still moving.

“Could you keep this quiet?”

Itsuki glanced at her.

“Keep what quiet?”

“The whole thing. I don't want people making a story out of a broken sign and a crowded hallway.”

He considered that.

“Why would they?”

Akari gave him a look.

“You'd be surprised.”

“I suppose.”

“It's not a big deal. I just don't want it becoming one.”

“Okay.”

She seemed relieved.

“Thank you.”

Itsuki adjusted the strap of his bag.

“Good luck with the festival.”

“You too.”

He left through the side corridor.

Akari watched him go for a moment, then turned back toward her class.

The incident had taken less than ten minutes.

It should have been forgotten by the time the festival ended.

But the two students with the camera had already started comparing what they had seen.

One remembered Akari standing close to a boy from another class.

The other remembered the board in his hands.

Neither had seen the crowd, the slipping trolley, or the loose papers.

And the photograph, taken just as they stepped through the corridor, showed only the two of them walking side by side.

It was not a lie.

It was simply not the truth.
