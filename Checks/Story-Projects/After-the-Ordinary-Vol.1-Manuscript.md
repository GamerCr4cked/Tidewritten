# After the Ordinary — Volume 1
## The Rumour That Started It All

**Manuscript status:** In progress; publication prose is being written chapter by chapter.  
**Canonical edition:** English  
**Series:** After the Ordinary  
**Volume:** 1 of 5  
**Target length:** Approximately 30,000–32,000 words of story prose, with the final length determined by the complete narrative rather than padding.

> **Continuity note for the author:** The Story Bible fixes Volume 1 to April–June, but also places the cultural festival in early autumn while requiring the festival rumour to end Volume 1. To preserve the five-volume calendar and the explicit Volume 1 ending, this manuscript currently places the festival on the final weekend of June. This is a transparent working resolution of that internal conflict, not a silent change to the Story Bible. The owner can revise it later if desired.

## Volume 1 — Chapter plan

This is a full-volume structure, not a short opening sample. The eighteen chapters carry the main narrative from April 1 through the final week of June. Additional scenes are placed between chapters where they deepen a character or relationship without interrupting the central progression.

1. **Prologue — The Last Day of Nothing** — Nanami's sixteenth birthday, Itsuki's apartment, and the siblings' ordinary rhythm.
2. **Chapter One — A Perfectly Ordinary Start** — Itsuki turns eighteen, begins his final school year, and meets the familiar faces in his new class.
3. **Chapter Two — The People Who Don't Need Invitations** — Riko's established friendship with Itsuki and their teasing dynamic.
4. **Chapter Three — A Familiar Name on the Attendance Sheet** — Ayane and Itsuki reconnect through school and a family gathering.
5. **Chapter Four — The Girl Everyone Seems to Know** — Akari's social presence and the distance between being recognised and being known.
6. **Chapter Five — A Quiet Seat by the Window** — Mio appears through ordinary library routines; no instant bond is forced.
7. **Chapter Six — A City Made of Small Errands** — Hoshimiya, Itsuki's apartment, Nanami's visits, and the small routines that make a life.
8. **Chapter Seven — The Things We Don't Ask Twice** — Itsuki and Ayane revisit a familiar route and acknowledge their gradual drift.
9. **Chapter Eight — A Test with Too Many Questions** — Academic pressure, Akari's effort, and Itsuki's ability to explain without being a prodigy.
10. **Chapter Nine — The Committee Nobody Wanted** — Class responsibilities and the first practical preparations for the festival.
11. **Chapter Ten — Before the Doors Open** — Final preparations, shared spaces, and the ordinary problems before the event.
12. **Chapter Eleven — The Morning Everyone Was Busy** — Festival day, the class booth, Nanami's visit, and the school at its busiest.
13. **Chapter Twelve — The Corridor Between Crowds** — The incident with Akari and Itsuki, shown as a brief act of practical help.
14. **Chapter Thirteen — Everyone Saw Something** — Partial witnesses and the photograph begin to turn fragments into a rumour.
15. **Chapter Fourteen — The Simplest Explanation** — Denials, speculation, and the first consequences of the rumour.
16. **Chapter Fifteen — The Account That Doesn't Match** — Mio asks Akari directly and learns that the rumour's versions do not fit.
17. **Chapter Sixteen — A Question Worth Answering** — Mio and Itsuki compare accounts; he begins to see that ignoring the rumour will not make it disappear.
18. **Chapter Seventeen — The Angle of a Photograph** — Mio speaks to the photographer and another witness, discovering how little anyone actually saw.
19. **Chapter Eighteen — The Last Week of June** — The school returns to routine; the rumour loses some attention, while the new connections remain.
20. **Epilogue — The First Version** — Mio chooses to continue the investigation, setting up Volume 2 without resolving the series arc.

### Additional character scenes

- **A Different School Bell** — Nanami's school life, friendship with Yui, and library-exchange responsibility.
- **The Version Akari Knows** — Akari's friendships, study effort, and relationship with public attention.
- **The Joke That Lands** — Riko's home expectations and her own effort with schoolwork.
- **A Page Out of Order** — Mio's quiet hobby of observing small mysteries, including the limits of her assumptions.
- Itsuki-centred side scenes deepen the origin of his friendship with Riko, ordinary school sports, his relationship with Nanami's father, the family home he no longer lives in, his study-support role, and his friendships with Riko and Ayane.

# Prologue — The Last Day of Nothing

April 1 was the kind of day that made people suspicious of calendars.

The city had not quite decided whether spring had arrived. Cherry blossoms lined the road beside Hoshimiya Station, but the wind still carried a chill from the previous week. A few petals had collected in the gutter. Others drifted across the pavement and caught against the wheels of bicycles parked outside the convenience store.

Itsuki Kurose watched one land on the toe of his shoe.

He waited for it to move.

It did not.

“Are you planning to negotiate with that?”

Itsuki looked up.

Nanami stood in the doorway of his apartment with a paper shopping bag in one hand and a small cake box balanced on top of it. Her long warm-brown hair fell over the shoulders of her pale cardigan, and the ends moved slightly in the breeze. Her amber eyes were fixed on the petal near his shoe.

“I was waiting for it to leave.”

“It's a flower.”

“I noticed.”

“Then perhaps you should be the one to move.”

“I'm comfortable where I am.”

Nanami stepped around him and entered without waiting for an invitation. She had a spare key. The fact that she used it with the confidence of someone who paid rent was one of the more persistent mysteries of Itsuki's life.

“Shoes,” he said.

“I know.”

“You're still wearing them.”

“I was getting to that.”

“You've been getting to that for three years.”

Nanami placed the bag on the small kitchen counter, slipped off her shoes, and lined them beside his. She did it neatly enough to undermine her own argument.

Itsuki closed the door.

The apartment was modest: a narrow entrance, a compact kitchen, a living space barely large enough for a low table and two people to sit comfortably, and a separate sleeping area divided by a sliding partition. It was clean because Itsuki disliked searching for things more than he disliked cleaning. A drying rack stood near the balcony door. Two mugs were upside down beside the sink. A school timetable was pinned to the refrigerator with a magnet shaped like a blue fish.

Nanami glanced around.

“You cleaned.”

“I live here.”

“That wasn't my question.”

“Then ask a better one.”

She opened the shopping bag. “I brought dinner.”

“That's not dinner.”

“It's part of dinner.”

The cake box made a soft cardboard sound as she moved it onto the counter.

Itsuki looked at it, then at her.

“Happy birthday.”

Nanami paused.

For a moment, her expression lost its usual quickness. Then she looked away and began taking things from the bag.

“You remembered.”

“It's on the calendar.”

“Your calendar has school deadlines, garbage collection, and the day the water company is supposed to inspect the meter.”

“It's a useful calendar.”

“And my birthday.”

“An important administrative event.”

She gave him a flat look. “You're terrible at this.”

“I bought the cake.”

“You didn't buy the cake. I bought the cake.”

“I allowed you to.”

“That's not how gifts work.”

“I'll remember that next year.”

“You'd better.”

Nanami's sixteenth birthday had arrived on a Tuesday, at the beginning of spring break. Their mother had sent a message that morning. Their father—Nanami's father, though Itsuki had never found the distinction particularly useful in daily conversation—had called the previous evening because he had an early business meeting today. Nanami had spent most of the afternoon at her own school helping a friend prepare for the new term.

Now she was here, in Itsuki's apartment, as if the arrangement required no explanation.

It usually didn't.

She unpacked a container of simmered vegetables, a packet of fried chicken, and a small bag of strawberries. The cake was from the bakery near the station. Itsuki recognised the box.

“You went to the one by the east exit?”

“It's your favourite.”

“It's a bakery.”

“You always buy the strawberry shortcake there.”

“Because it's close.”

“Of course.”

He opened the refrigerator and moved a bottle of tea to make room for the food. Nanami watched him.

“You're doing that thing again.”

“What thing?”

“Pretending not to be happy.”

“I haven't said anything.”

“Exactly.”

Itsuki shut the refrigerator.

Nanami smiled, satisfied with herself.

They ate at the low table. The television stayed off. Outside, a bicycle bell rang twice, followed by the distant announcement of a train arriving at the station. Nanami talked about a classmate who had managed to lose her indoor shoes before the new term had even started. Itsuki listened, occasionally asking a question when the story became difficult to follow.

“Anyway,” Nanami said, “she blamed the cleaning committee.”

“Reasonable.”

“She was the cleaning committee.”

“Less reasonable.”

“She said she had been betrayed by the system.”

“An unfortunate system.”

Nanami pointed her chopsticks at him. “You would have liked her.”

“I doubt that.”

“You both think being quiet is a personality.”

“I don't think that.”

“You just demonstrated it.”

Itsuki took another piece of chicken.

Nanami was good at filling a room without making it feel crowded. She could talk for ten minutes about something that had happened at school, then sit quietly for another ten without seeming to need a reason. When she visited, she sometimes rearranged the cushions, complained about his lack of snacks, and left a hair tie on the table. Itsuki had stopped returning the hair ties. They appeared again the next week anyway.

After dinner, she brought out the cake.

It was small, with strawberries arranged around the top and a thin chocolate plate that read *Happy Birthday, Nanami*. The writing leaned slightly to the left.

“Sixteen,” Itsuki said.

“Don't say it like that.”

“Like what?”

“Like you're announcing a weather report.”

“Sixteen degrees. Light rain.”

She laughed, then shook her head.

He found two plates. Nanami cut the cake with more care than the occasion required, making sure each slice had a strawberry. She gave him the slightly larger one.

“You're the birthday person,” he said.

“I know. That's why I'm allowed to decide.”

“Convenient.”

“Very.”

They ate slowly.

When the plates were empty, Nanami leaned back against the sofa. Her gaze moved to the refrigerator, where the blue fish magnet held Itsuki's timetable in place.

“Your school starts tomorrow, right?”

“Orientation. Classes begin next week.”

“Third year.”

“Unfortunately.”

“Graduation year.”

“That's what the timetable says.”

“You really haven't thought about what comes after?”

“Not today.”

“That's not an answer.”

“It's the answer I have.”

Nanami considered him. She had inherited their father's habit of looking at a person as if she could hear the thought they had decided not to say. It was a habit Itsuki disliked because it occasionally worked.

“I'm not asking you to decide tonight,” she said. “Just don't let the year finish before you start thinking.”

“I'll put it on the calendar.”

“Don't.”

“Too late. April first: think about the future.”

She threw a cushion at him.

He caught it.

For a while, the apartment settled into the comfortable quiet that followed a meal. Nanami checked her phone. Itsuki washed the plates. The ordinary tasks made a small rhythm: water, ceramic, the cupboard door closing, a message notification from somewhere in the room.

His phone vibrated on the counter.

A message from their father.

*Happy birthday to Nanami. I hope she had a good day. Tell her I will call after work tomorrow. And Itsuki—your school year starts soon. Take care of yourself.*

Itsuki read it twice, then placed the phone face down.

Nanami had seen the name.

“Dad says he'll call tomorrow?”

“He says he'll call after work.”

“That's what I said.”

“You asked a question. I answered it.”

She stood and came to the kitchen. “Did you tell him I was here?”

“He knows.”

“Of course he does.”

“He asked whether you were staying for dinner.”

“And you told him?”

“That you were bringing dinner.”

Nanami gave him a suspicious look. “You two talk about me like I'm a delivery service.”

“You did bring the food.”

“I'm never bringing you anything again.”

“Understood.”

She took the dish towel from his hand and dried the last plate.

Itsuki let her.

There were things about their family that had become easier to live around than to explain. Nanami's father had once been the person who came home to the same house as both of them. Their mother had later left that marriage and built another household. Itsuki had not followed her into it. Nanami moved between places more often, trying to make each arrangement feel normal without pretending they were the same.

No one had asked Itsuki to stop caring about the man who had raised him for years. No one had asked Nanami to choose one home over another. That did not mean the arrangements were simple.

But tonight was Nanami's birthday.

There was no reason to turn a cake into a family meeting.

When the kitchen was clean, Nanami retrieved her cardigan from the chair.

“I should go.”

“It's getting cold.”

“I have a coat.”

“You left it in the entrance.”

“Then the problem is solved.”

She put it on, checked her bag, and reached for the door. Before opening it, she turned back.

“Tomorrow is your birthday.”

“I know.”

“Eighteen.”

“Apparently.”

“Do you want anything?”

“Peace and quiet.”

“You already have those.”

“I'd like to keep them.”

Nanami smiled. “I'll see what I can do.”

“That sounds threatening.”

“It is.”

She opened the door, then leaned back into the apartment.

“Happy early birthday, Onii-chan.”

“It's not early if it's tomorrow.”

“Then I'll say it again tomorrow.”

“You don't have to.”

“I know.”

She left before he could answer.

Itsuki locked the door and stood in the entrance for a moment. The apartment returned to its usual quiet. On the table, two plates waited to be put away. A few crumbs remained beside the cake box. The blue fish magnet held tomorrow's timetable against the refrigerator.

Nothing had changed.

That was usually what he wanted.

Still, when he looked at the calendar, he found himself noticing the date.

April 1.

The last day before his final year began.

He turned off the kitchen light.

# Chapter One — A Perfectly Ordinary Start

Itsuki turned eighteen at 12:00 a.m.

Nothing happened.

No sudden wisdom arrived. No hidden talent unlocked itself. The ceiling remained the same shade of off-white, the refrigerator continued its low mechanical hum, and the neighbour upstairs dropped something heavy at 12:07.

Itsuki lay on his back and considered whether adulthood was supposed to be louder.

At 6:40, his alarm answered with a cheerful electronic melody that sounded increasingly unreasonable.

He turned it off.

The first day of April was cold enough that he wore a light jacket over his shirt. He made toast, boiled water for tea, and checked his school bag. His timetable was still pinned to the refrigerator, though the date had changed.

A message waited on his phone.

*Happy birthday, Itsuki. Eighteen already. I hope this year is kind to you. Please eat properly. — Mom*

He read it, then placed the phone beside the kettle.

A second message arrived before he finished breakfast.

*Happy birthday. You're officially an adult, so I expect you to become more responsible. — Nanami*

Itsuki typed back.

*I'll try to become less responsible instead.*

Her reply came almost immediately.

*I knew you would disappoint me.*

He put the phone away.

The school stood on a gentle slope beyond the station district. Hoshimiya Municipal High School was not especially old or especially modern. Its main building had been repainted a few years earlier, but the older gymnasium still had faded court markings and windows that rattled when the wind came from the north. The grounds were wide enough for a proper sports field and a row of cherry trees, though the bicycle parking area always seemed to be full.

Itsuki arrived ten minutes before the orientation assembly.

Students gathered in the courtyard in loose groups. Some had already found their friends. Others stood beside the class lists, scanning names and pointing at the paper as if the arrangement might change if they looked long enough.

Itsuki checked the board.

Class 3-B.

He found his name halfway down the list.

There were a few familiar names. One belonged to a boy from his second-year English class. Another belonged to a girl who had once borrowed his notes and returned them with a packet of biscuits.

He did not see anyone he particularly needed to find.

That was fine.

“Morning.”

A voice came from behind him.

Itsuki turned.

Riko Tachibana stood with one hand raised in greeting. Her short caramel-brown hair was tucked behind one ear, and her golden-brown eyes carried the bright, amused look of someone who had already decided the day would be entertaining.

“Morning,” he said.

She leaned sideways to read the class list over his shoulder.

“Three-B. Same class.”

“I noticed.”

“You don't look happy.”

“I was trying not to look anything.”

“That's your natural expression.”

“Then it's working.”

Riko laughed. She had been doing this for long enough that Itsuki no longer remembered which of them had first decided they were friends. He suspected she had simply started talking to him one day and refused to stop. She was one of the few people who could turn his silence into a conversation without making him feel obliged to fill it.

“Did you do anything for your birthday?” she asked.

“It's today.”

“I know.”

“Then why are you asking?”

“To see if you had a party.”

“I ate toast.”

Riko stared at him.

“That's tragic.”

“It was good toast.”

“I'm sure the bread was honoured.”

“Thank you.”

She narrowed her eyes. “You're getting sarcastic earlier than usual.”

“It's a new-year improvement.”

“Maybe eighteen suits you.”

“It's been less than nine hours.”

“Still.”

A group of students passed behind them. Riko moved aside to let them through, then glanced at the class list again.

“Do you know anyone else in our class?”

“Some people.”

“That's not useful.”

“It's accurate.”

“Fine. I'll do the social work.”

“You enjoy it.”

“I enjoy making people regret underestimating me.”

“That's different.”

“Not really.”

The assembly bell rang.

They walked toward the gym with the crowd. Riko talked about a teacher who had apparently been assigned to their year again. Itsuki listened and occasionally gave a short response. He had no strong opinion about the teacher. He had no strong opinion about most things before breakfast.

The gym smelled faintly of floor polish and old wood. Students sat by class while teachers moved along the aisles, checking attendance. Itsuki took a place near the end of the third-year row. Riko sat two spaces away and immediately began whispering commentary about the principal's microphone.

The principal spoke for several minutes about responsibility, opportunity, and the importance of making the most of one's final year.

Itsuki looked at the stage clock.

He had always respected clocks. They did not ask him to make the most of anything.

After the assembly, Class 3-B gathered in their assigned room. The windows faced the sports field, where a groundskeeper was collecting fallen branches. A faint smell of chalk lingered near the blackboard.

Their homeroom teacher, Mr. Hasegawa, introduced himself. He was in his early forties, with a neat haircut and the tired but patient expression of someone who had spent enough years teaching teenagers to recognise the difference between a quiet student and a sleeping one.

He read out the class responsibilities, explained the schedule, and handed out forms.

“Choose your committee preferences by the end of the week,” he said. “You don't need to volunteer for everything. But do choose something. This is your final year, and the school still needs people to keep things running.”

Riko raised her hand.

“Does choosing nothing count as a choice?”

“It counts as a conversation with me after class.”

“Understood.”

Itsuki kept his eyes on the form.

There were several committee options. Library, health, class events, student council support, festival preparation. He marked library as his first preference, mostly because it seemed unlikely to involve a microphone.

Riko leaned over.

“Library?”

“It's quiet.”

“That's your entire reason?”

“It's a good reason.”

“You should choose festival preparation. It would be funny.”

“For whom?”

“For me.”

“Then I see the problem.”

She grinned and marked something on her own form.

A few minutes later, Mr. Hasegawa began collecting the papers. He stopped beside Itsuki's desk.

“Kurose.”

Itsuki looked up.

“Your English test from last term was strong. Not the highest score in the year, but your written explanations were clear.”

“Thank you.”

“Would you be willing to help with the class study-support board this term? Nothing complicated. Just examples of how to approach the reading questions.”

Itsuki glanced at the form.

“I'm not sure I'm the best person for that.”

“Why not?”

“Someone who scores higher might be better.”

“Someone who scores higher might also explain less clearly.”

That was a reasonable point. Itsuki disliked reasonable points because they were difficult to argue with.

“I'll think about it,” he said.

“That's all I'm asking.”

Mr. Hasegawa moved on.

Riko waited until he was out of earshot.

“Study-support board?”

“Apparently.”

“Look at you. A public service.”

“I'm considering a career change.”

“To what?”

“Unemployment.”

“Stable hours.”

“Exactly.”

The morning passed in forms, schedules, and introductions. Itsuki learned that the boy sitting in front of him was named Sota Miyake and that he played basketball. The girl by the window, Emi Naruse, had transferred from another class and was already organising a list of people willing to help with the classroom display.

He also noticed a familiar name on the seating chart.

Ayane Fujimoto.

She was in the same class.

Itsuki found her near the window after lunch. She was speaking with two girls, her shoulder-length black hair tucked behind one ear. When she turned and noticed him, her expression brightened with recognition.

“It'suki.”

He stopped.

“Fujimoto.”

The girls beside her exchanged a quick glance and moved away with the tact of people who knew they had arrived in the middle of something.

Ayane smiled, but there was a small hesitation beneath it.

“It's been a while.”

“We saw each other at New Year's.”

“That's three months ago.”

“That's a while.”

She laughed softly. “I suppose it is.”

They had known each other since they were small enough to sit under the same dining table during family gatherings. Their parents had been close for years. At one point, Itsuki had spent so many afternoons at Ayane's house that he knew which floorboard creaked outside the kitchen.

That had been a long time ago.

They still spoke when their families met. They still knew each other's names, birthdays, and general habits. But there were gaps now—ordinary gaps, made from school schedules and different friends and the assumption that the other person was busy.

“How was spring break?” Ayane asked.

“Quiet.”

“That's good.”

“How was yours?”

“Busy. My mother had me help with the neighbourhood association, and I had to organise some things for school.”

“Sounds busy.”

“It was.”

A pause settled between them.

Neither seemed to know what to do with it.

Ayane glanced at his school bag. “You're still carrying the same one.”

“It's still a bag.”

“I meant you haven't changed it.”

“It's not broken.”

She smiled again. “Right.”

Itsuki realised he had made the conversation more difficult than it needed to be.

“Your hair's a little shorter,” he said.

Ayane touched the ends of it. “I had it trimmed last month.”

“It suits you.”

Her eyes widened slightly, then softened.

“Thank you.”

He looked toward the window.

The compliment had been a normal thing to say. He was not sure why it felt like he had stepped onto an unfamiliar staircase.

Before either of them could continue, the classroom door opened and Mr. Hasegawa called for everyone to return to their seats.

Ayane gave him a small nod.

“See you later, Itsuki.”

“Yeah.”

He returned to his desk.

Riko was waiting with her chin resting on one hand.

“You talked to Fujimoto.”

“She talked to me.”

“Important distinction.”

“Very.”

“Childhood friend?”

“Family friend.”

“That's a different answer.”

“It's the accurate one.”

Riko studied him for a moment, then smiled in a way that suggested she had stored the conversation for future use.

Itsuki looked down at his timetable.

The first day of his final year had been uneventful.

He had met his teacher, confirmed his class, received several forms, and spoken to two people he already knew. He had also turned eighteen, though the event had failed to produce any noticeable improvement in his circumstances.

A perfectly ordinary start.

He decided to count that as a success.

When he returned to the apartment that evening, a small paper bag was hanging from the doorknob.

Itsuki stopped in the corridor.

There was no name on it, but the handwriting on the folded note was familiar.

*You said you didn't want anything. I decided not to listen. Happy birthday again.*

Inside the bag was a mechanical pencil, a packet of refills, and a small pudding from the convenience store near Nanami's school. The pencil was plain black, with a comfortable grip and no unnecessary decoration.

He turned it over once.

His phone rang.

“Hello?”

“Happy birthday, Itsuki.”

The voice was calm and familiar. Itsuki leaned one shoulder against the wall.

“Thanks.”

“Eighteen.”

“Apparently.”

A quiet laugh came through the speaker.

“Your mother told me you had orientation today. How was it?”

“Normal.”

“That's good.”

There was a pause. Neither of them was particularly skilled at making phone calls last longer than necessary, but the silence between them was not uncomfortable.

“Nanami said she was going to see you yesterday,” the man said.

“She brought dinner.”

“Did you have enough?”

“More than enough.”

“Good. She's been busy with the new school year.”

“She told me about the library project.”

“She sounded excited about it.”

“She made a schedule.”

“Then it must be serious.”

Itsuki looked at the mechanical pencil in his hand.

“Thanks for calling.”

“Of course. If you want to come over this weekend, I'll be home on Sunday.”

“I'll see.”

“That's fine. No pressure.”

The call ended a minute later.

Itsuki put the pencil on the table beside his English workbook. He opened the pudding, ate it standing at the kitchen counter, and washed the spoon immediately.

The day had not changed much.

He was still in the same apartment. The same timetable was pinned to the refrigerator. The same school bag sat beside the chair.

But there was a new pencil on the table, and two people had remembered the date without needing to be reminded.

For an eighteenth birthday, it was enough.

# Chapter Two — The People Who Don't Need Invitations

Riko had a talent for appearing in places where she had not been invited.

It was not that she ignored invitations. She simply treated their absence as a technicality.

At 3:18 p.m., Itsuki discovered her sitting on the low wall beside the school bicycle parking area, eating a melon bread she had apparently acquired from somewhere.

He stopped.

She looked up.

“Good afternoon.”

“School ended twenty minutes ago.”

“Then you're late.”

“For what?”

“Your appointment.”

“I don't have one.”

“That's why you're late.”

Itsuki considered walking away. Riko lifted the second melon bread from the paper bag and held it out.

He stopped considering.

“Bribery,” he said.

“Hospitality.”

“There's a difference.”

“Not when it works.”

He accepted the bread.

The afternoon sun had warmed the concrete wall. Students moved past them in small groups, their voices drifting toward the main gate. A baseball club member jogged across the field with a bag over one shoulder. Somewhere behind the gym, someone was practising the same brass passage repeatedly, each attempt ending at the same uncertain note.

Riko swung one leg.

“Are you going straight home?”

“Yes.”

“That's boring.”

“It's a destination, not a performance.”

“Come to the shopping street with me.”

“Why?”

“I need to buy something.”

“You can buy it without me.”

“I could.”

“Then I don't see the difficulty.”

She looked at him as if he had disappointed her personally.

“It's a heavy thing.”

“What is it?”

“I don't know yet.”

“That sounds difficult.”

“Exactly. I need a second opinion.”

Itsuki took a bite of the bread. It was sweet enough to qualify as a small argument against his usual preference for plain food.

“What are you buying?”

“Something for the classroom.”

“You don't have a classroom assignment.”

“I might.”

“That's not how assignments work.”

“Not with that attitude.”

He should have gone home. There were dishes in the sink from breakfast, laundry to bring in, and an English workbook he had promised himself he would finish before the weekend. None of those tasks was urgent.

That was how Riko won most of their arguments. She did not need a convincing reason. She only needed the alternative to be slightly less interesting.

“Fine,” he said.

Riko hopped down from the wall.

“Excellent. You're learning to live.”

“I was doing that before I met you.”

“Debatable.”

They walked toward the station district.

The shopping street was narrow, with low buildings and shopfronts that had accumulated their own histories. A stationery store displayed notebooks in the window. The florist had buckets of pale flowers arranged outside. A small café had placed two chairs beneath its awning, though no one was sitting in them. At the far end, the road widened near a crossing where the traffic light took an unreasonable amount of time to change.

Riko stopped outside the stationery store.

“Pens,” she announced.

“You brought me here for pens?”

“I said I needed to buy something.”

“You said it was heavy.”

“The decision is heavy.”

Itsuki looked at the store window. A display of coloured markers stood beside a stack of poster paper.

“Get black.”

“Why?”

“Because you use black.”

“That's a very narrow view of the world.”

“It's a pen.”

Riko opened the door.

A small bell rang above them. Inside, the shop smelled of paper and plastic packaging. The owner greeted them from behind the counter. Riko answered brightly, then wandered toward the display shelves.

She picked up a set of pastel markers, put them down, chose a pack of black pens, then returned to the markers.

Itsuki stood near the notebooks.

“You're not helping,” she said.

“You haven't asked for help.”

“I'm asking now.”

“Buy the pens.”

“What if I regret it?”

“Then you'll have several pens to regret.”

She laughed, but she bought the markers.

At the counter, she also picked up a pack of plain sticky notes. Itsuki noticed the corner of a folded worksheet sticking out of her bag.

“Homework?” he asked.

“Evidence of an unfortunate system.”

“Math?”

“Don't say it like that.”

“Like what?”

“Like you know.”

He looked at the worksheet. There were several problems marked in red, not all of them wrong.

“You missed the negative sign here.”

Riko followed his finger.

“That's a decorative mark.”

“It's part of the equation.”

“It's ruining the composition.”

“It's a minus sign.”

“Exactly. Very negative.”

He returned the worksheet.

Riko paid, thanked the shop owner, and stepped outside. She walked a little more slowly than before, turning the packet of markers over in her hands.

“You could ask Hasegawa-sensei for help,” Itsuki said.

“I could.”

“You don't have to pretend the work is impossible.”

“I don't.”

“You just called the minus sign decorative.”

“That's because it is.”

He did not press the point. Riko was not stupid. She could understand the material when she wanted to, but she often lost patience halfway through a problem and decided the problem had personally offended her.

They stopped at a vending machine near the corner. Riko bought a canned peach drink. Itsuki chose tea.

“Are you going to help with the study board?” she asked.

“Maybe.”

“You should.”

“Why?”

“Because you're good at explaining things.”

“I've explained one minus sign.”

“And I understood it.”

“You've rejected it.”

“Understanding and acceptance are different.”

Itsuki opened his tea.

Riko looked down the street. A group of younger students passed, their bags bouncing against their backs. One of them called to a friend across the pavement. The friend waved both arms.

“Do you ever think about what you'll do after graduation?” Riko asked.

“Not often.”

“Why not?”

“Because it's April.”

“That's not an answer.”

“It's the current month.”

She rolled her eyes.

“You're impossible.”

“I've been told.”

“By me.”

“Frequently.”

Riko took a sip from her drink. Her expression had gone thoughtful, though she did not seem unhappy.

“My parents already have a plan for me,” she said.

Itsuki glanced at her.

“School?”

“Everything. School, grades, what I should do after. They have a list.”

“Do you agree with it?”

“Sometimes.”

“And when you don't?”

She smiled.

“Then I come to school and become a public nuisance.”

“That seems unrelated.”

“It's very related.”

She said it lightly. Itsuki did not ask what she meant. There were parts of Riko's life he knew only in outline. She was lively at school, quick with a joke, and almost impossible to embarrass. Her family was strict, she had once mentioned. That was all.

A phone vibrated in her pocket.

Riko checked the screen. Her expression changed—not dramatically, but enough for Itsuki to notice. The smile settled into something more polite.

“It's my mother,” she said.

She answered.

“Hello, Mom. Yes, I'm on my way. I bought the things for school. No, I haven't forgotten the workbook. I'll be home before dinner.”

Her voice was softer than the one she used with him. She listened, gave a few short replies, and promised to send a message when she reached the station.

When the call ended, she returned the phone to her pocket.

“Everything all right?” Itsuki asked.

“Of course.”

“You sound different.”

“Do I?”

“A little.”

Riko shrugged.

“That's because I have a phone voice.”

“An entire voice?”

“Several. You're only familiar with the one I use to bother you.”

“I suspected there were others.”

She nudged his shoulder with hers.

“Don't sound so disappointed.”

They continued toward the station. At the crossing, Riko waited beside him while the signal stayed red.

“Thanks for coming,” she said.

“You bought pens.”

“And you provided emotional support.”

“I corrected a minus sign.”

“Exactly. You were there for me.”

The light changed.

Riko crossed first, waving without looking back. Itsuki followed at a more ordinary pace.

At the station entrance, they separated. She went toward the bus stop; he headed for the local train platform.

His phone buzzed before he reached the ticket gate.

A message from Riko.

*Don't forget your birthday cake tomorrow. You owe me a slice.*

Itsuki typed back.

*You already ate the cake.*

*That was Nanami's cake. I'm talking about yours.*

*I don't have one.*

*Then buy one.*

He put the phone away.

It was possible, he thought, that the final year would be less quiet than he had planned.

He decided not to mention that to anyone.

# Interlude VIII — The Answer in the Margin

Riko arrived at the library with a workbook and an expression of deep suspicion.

Itsuki looked up from his notes.

“What's wrong?”

“Everything.”

“Specific.”

She dropped into the chair opposite him and placed the workbook on the table.

“I need help.”

Itsuki looked at the cover.

“Mathematics?”

“Don't say it like that.”

“Like what?”

“Like you're announcing a diagnosis.”

He closed his notebook.

“Which question?”

Riko opened the workbook to a page marked with two sticky notes. Several problems had been attempted. Some had been crossed out. One had a small drawing of a skull beside it.

“Question seven.”

He read it.

“It's not difficult.”

Riko leaned back.

“That's a cruel thing to say to someone asking for help.”

“I didn't say it was easy.”

“You said it wasn't difficult.”

“Those are different.”

“Not to me.”

Itsuki turned the workbook toward himself.

“First, what do you know?”

“Very little.”

“That's not useful.”

“Then ask a better question.”

He pointed to the first line.

“What's the value they're asking you to find?”

Riko read it.

“The missing angle.”

“Good. What information do they give you?”

She scanned the diagram.

“Two angles.”

“And?”

“A triangle.”

“That's a start.”

“It's a very triangular triangle.”

He waited.

Riko sighed and looked at the diagram again.

“The angles add up to one hundred and eighty degrees.”

“Right. So what do you do?”

“Subtract the two we know.”

“Exactly.”

She wrote the equation.

For a few minutes, the table was quiet. Riko worked through the numbers, stopping twice to check the diagram. Her first answer was wrong because she had copied one value incorrectly. The second was right.

She looked at the page.

“That's it?”

“That's it.”

“That's annoyingly simple.”

“Most of the question is noticing what information matters.”

Riko tapped the pencil against the workbook.

“I hate when the answer is obvious after someone explains it.”

“Then remember the explanation.”

“That's even worse.”

Itsuki returned the workbook.

She did not close it.

Instead, she turned to the next question.

“Can you check this one too?”

He nodded.

They worked through three more problems. Riko made two mistakes, corrected one without help, and asked him to explain the other. Itsuki showed her where the diagram gave the necessary information.

He did not solve every problem for her.

That seemed to annoy her.

It also seemed to help.

When they finished, Riko closed the workbook.

“I'm going to become a mathematician.”

“That's unlikely.”

“I'm going to become a mathematician who hates mathematics.”

“More believable.”

She smiled.

“Thanks.”

“Sure.”

Riko packed her things, then paused.

“Do you ever get tired of explaining things?”

“Sometimes.”

“Then why do it?”

He considered the question.

“Because you asked.”

She looked at him for a moment.

Then her usual grin returned.

“That's almost nice.”

“Don't tell anyone.”

“Your secret is safe.”

She stood and returned the workbook to her bag.

At the door, she turned.

“Same time next week?”

“If I have time.”

“Good. I'll assume yes.”

“That's not what I said.”

“It's close enough.”

She left before he could correct her.

Itsuki looked at the empty chair.

He had planned to finish two pages of his own work.

He had finished one.

It was not an efficient afternoon.

Still, Riko had left with a workbook she could actually use.

He decided that was probably worth it.

# Side Scene — The First Time Riko Sat Down

Itsuki did not remember deciding to become friends with Riko Tachibana.

He remembered the first time she spoke to him.

It had been late in the previous school year, on a day when the classroom was too warm and the afternoon lesson had been cancelled because a teacher was absent. Most students had gone to the courtyard. A few stayed behind to finish assignments.

Itsuki had been sitting near the window, reading.

He preferred the classroom when it was almost empty. The silence was not complete, but it was predictable. A chair moved. Someone turned a page. A door opened somewhere down the corridor. None of it required a response.

Then someone pulled out the chair opposite him.

“Is this seat taken?”

Itsuki looked up.

A girl with short brown hair stood beside the desk. She had a bright, curious expression and a notebook tucked beneath one arm.

“It's a classroom,” he said.

She blinked.

“That's not an answer.”

“Then no.”

She sat down.

He returned to his book.

For a minute, she said nothing.

Then she leaned forward.

“What are you reading?”

He showed her the cover.

She tilted her head.

“Is it interesting?”

“Sometimes.”

“That's another terrible answer.”

“It's a collection of essays.”

“I know what it is. I asked if it was interesting.”

“Some of them.”

She stared at him.

Then she laughed.

“Do you always talk like that?”

“Like what?”

“Like every sentence costs money.”

Itsuki turned a page.

“Only when I'm saving.”

She laughed again.

He looked at her over the top of the book.

“What?”

“Nothing. You're funny.”

“I wasn't trying to be.”

“That's why it's funny.”

She introduced herself as Riko Tachibana. She was in another class, though he had seen her around school. She had a reputation for making teachers sigh and classmates laugh. Itsuki knew that much.

He did not know why she had chosen his table.

“Are you always alone?” she asked.

“Usually.”

“Do you like it?”

“Yes.”

“Then why are you talking to me?”

He considered that.

“You sat down.”

Riko smiled.

“Fair.”

She opened her notebook and began drawing something in the margin. Itsuki returned to his reading.

After a few minutes, she pushed the notebook toward him.

It was a drawing of a small fish wearing glasses.

Itsuki looked at it.

“That's you,” she said.

“I don't look like a fish.”

“You have the same expression.”

“Fish don't wear glasses.”

“This one does.”

He pushed the notebook back.

“It's inaccurate.”

“Then I need a better reference.”

She stayed until the bell rang.

The next day, she sat at the same table.

The day after that, she did it again.

Eventually, Itsuki stopped asking why.

He had not invited her.

She had not asked him to become her friend.

It had happened through repeated small decisions: a chair pulled out, a question asked, a joke that did not need an answer, a conversation that continued because neither of them had a reason to leave.

He still did not know exactly when it had become friendship.

Riko had never explained it.

He had stopped trying to find out.

# Side Scene — The Last Game Before Rain

Physical education was scheduled for the final period on Friday.

The sky had been clear in the morning, but by the time Class 3-B reached the sports field, clouds had gathered over the western side of Hoshimiya. Mr. Hasegawa had checked the weather app twice and decided they had enough time for one short activity.

“Badminton,” he announced.

Riko looked at the rackets.

“Excellent.”

Itsuki looked at the sky.

“It's going to rain.”

“That's not the spirit.”

“It's a weather forecast.”

The class divided into pairs. Itsuki ended up with Miyake, who played for the school team and had volunteered to help keep the matches moving. Riko paired with Emi.

The first few minutes were uneventful.

Itsuki returned the shuttlecock when it came within reach. He was not especially good, but he understood where he was supposed to stand and did not swing at empty air. Miyake did most of the work.

“Nice,” Miyake said after Itsuki returned a shot over the net.

“Thanks.”

“You play sometimes?”

“Not really.”

“You're better than you think.”

“That's probably because you keep sending it to me.”

Miyake laughed.

On the next court, Riko had decided that the rules were flexible.

She served too high, missed the return, and then claimed the wind had interfered with the shuttlecock's intentions.

Emi looked at her.

“It's indoors.”

“Air conditioning.”

“It's off.”

“Then it's a personal problem.”

The teacher blew the whistle.

“Tachibana. Focus.”

“I am focused.”

“On the game.”

“Of course.”

Riko served again.

The shuttlecock hit the net and fell back onto her side.

She stared at it.

“Betrayal.”

Itsuki looked away before he laughed.

The first drops of rain began during the second round. The teacher called the class back toward the building before the field became slippery. Students collected the rackets and carried them to the equipment room.

Riko walked beside Itsuki.

“You laughed.”

“No.”

“I heard it.”

“You heard the rain.”

“The rain doesn't sound like that.”

“Maybe it was laughing too.”

She nudged his arm.

“You're getting worse.”

“I'm improving.”

They returned the equipment and went back to the classroom to collect their bags. The rain grew heavier against the windows.

Miyake stopped beside Itsuki's desk.

“You should join the badminton club.”

“No.”

“You'd get better.”

“I'd have to attend practice.”

“That's usually how clubs work.”

“Then it's not for me.”

Miyake laughed and left.

Riko leaned against the desk.

“You're allergic to commitments.”

“Only unnecessary ones.”

“That's a dangerous philosophy.”

“It's kept me alive.”

She grinned.

The final bell rang.

Outside, the rain had settled into a steady shower. Itsuki took out his umbrella. Riko checked her bag and frowned.

“Forgot yours?” he asked.

“Again.”

“That's twice.”

“Don't count.”

He opened the umbrella.

Riko stepped under it.

“You're very kind.”

“I'm preventing you from getting sick and missing class.”

“Still kind.”

“Don't tell anyone.”

She smiled.

They walked toward the station, arguing about whether badminton counted as a sport if one of the players was actively negotiating with the shuttlecock.

By the time they reached the crossing, the rain had begun to ease.

Itsuki looked at the sky.

Riko looked at him.

“Don't say I told you so.”

“I wasn't going to.”

“You were thinking it.”

“Maybe.”

She laughed.

The light changed.

They crossed together.

# Chapter Three — A Familiar Name on the Attendance Sheet

The first family gathering of the new school year was not meant to be a celebration.

Itsuki knew this because his mother had said so three times.

“It's just dinner,” she had told him over the phone. “Ayane's parents are coming, and I thought it would be nice if you joined us.”

“Why?”

“Because we haven't all eaten together in a while.”

“We ate together at New Year's.”

“That was three months ago.”

“Nanami used the same argument yesterday.”

His mother had gone quiet for half a second.

“Then perhaps she's right.”

Itsuki had considered refusing. He did not dislike Ayane's parents. He had known them for most of his life, and they had always treated him kindly. The difficulty was not the people. It was the place.

His mother's current home was a house he had never chosen to live in, shared with a man he had never chosen to call family. The third husband was polite. He asked about school, offered food, and made an effort to include Itsuki in conversations. Itsuki understood that those were decent things to do.

He also understood that politeness did not create closeness by itself.

His mother had eventually suggested that dinner could be held at the Fujimotos' house instead. Itsuki agreed.

So on Saturday evening, he took the train two stops north and walked through a residential neighbourhood where the houses stood close together behind low fences. The air smelled faintly of cut grass and cooking oil. A child was riding a small bicycle in circles at the end of the street.

The Fujimoto house looked much as he remembered it. The front garden had changed; a row of potted herbs now stood beside the steps. The wind chime near the entrance was the same one that had been there when he was in primary school.

Ayane opened the door.

“Itsuki.”

“Evening.”

She wore a soft yellow cardigan over a simple dress. Her hair was tucked behind one ear, and she looked as if she had been about to go back to the kitchen.

“You came.”

“Your mother invited me.”

Ayane smiled. “I know. I just wasn't sure if you would.”

There was no accusation in it. That made it harder to answer.

“Your house is close,” he said.

“That's true.”

She stepped aside to let him in.

The familiar smell of the entranceway reached him first: wood polish, laundry detergent, and something simmering in the kitchen. His shoes found their old place beside the cabinet without him having to think about it.

Ayane noticed.

“You still remember.”

“It's the only place the shoes fit.”

She laughed.

The living room was already occupied. Ayane's father stood near the table, talking with Itsuki's mother. Her mother was bringing dishes from the kitchen. The third husband had not come; Itsuki's mother had said he had a work commitment. Itsuki was relieved, though he did not say so.

His mother turned when he entered.

“Itsuki. Thank you for coming.”

“Hello.”

She looked at him for a moment, as if checking whether he seemed well. Then she reached up and adjusted the collar of his jacket.

“You've grown your hair out.”

“It's the same length.”

“It looks longer.”

“Maybe the collar is different.”

She lowered her hand.

“Have you been eating properly?”

“Yes.”

“Really?”

“Most days.”

His mother sighed. Ayane's father laughed from the table.

“He's always been like that. If you ask him whether he's eaten, he'll tell you the number of meals rather than what they were.”

“That's useful information,” Itsuki said.

“It isn't,” his mother replied.

Dinner was warm and ordinary. Ayane's mother had made grilled fish, rice, miso soup, and a small dish of vegetables. They talked about the new school year, the neighbourhood, and a relative who had recently moved closer to the station. Itsuki answered when asked and otherwise listened.

Ayane sat across from him.

She had become more comfortable speaking with the adults than he remembered. She helped her mother serve food, asked about Itsuki's school, and laughed at her father's jokes. When she spoke to Itsuki directly, however, she still paused a little before each question.

“Are you in the same class as Riko?” she asked.

“Yeah.”

“She's the one who always teases you, right?”

“That's one description.”

“I remember her from the sports festival last year.”

“She was probably causing trouble.”

Ayane smiled. “Probably.”

“She's not as bad as people say,” Itsuki added.

He was not sure why he said it. Riko was capable of defending herself. She would probably have found it funny that he was doing so.

Ayane looked at him with a small expression of surprise.

“You sound close.”

“We're friends.”

“That's good.”

The conversation moved on.

After dinner, Ayane's mother asked them to bring a box of old photo albums from the storage room. Itsuki followed Ayane down the hall.

The storage room was narrow and cool. A cardboard box sat on the lower shelf, marked with a faded label. Ayane crouched and pulled it forward.

“Careful,” Itsuki said. “It's heavier than it looks.”

“I know. We put the albums in last year.”

“You could have asked your father.”

“He's talking to your mother.”

“Then you could have asked me.”

She glanced at him.

“I did.”

He looked at the box.

“Fair.”

They lifted it together and carried it to the living room. Ayane set it on the low table and opened the lid.

Inside were several albums, envelopes of printed photographs, and a small stack of school drawings. Ayane picked up the top album.

“This one is from primary school.”

Itsuki sat beside the table.

The first few pages showed a class trip, a sports day, and a summer festival. There were photographs of children in hats, children holding paper fans, children standing in rows with expressions of forced patience.

Then Ayane turned a page.

Itsuki was there.

He was younger, his hair shorter, his expression less carefully blank. He stood beside Ayane beneath a cherry tree. Both of them held paper cups. Ayane was laughing at something outside the frame.

“I remember that day,” she said.

“I don't.”

“You spilled juice on your shirt.”

“That sounds familiar.”

“You said the stain looked like a map.”

“I might have been right.”

“You were very proud of it.”

“I was seven.”

Ayane laughed.

The next photograph showed them sitting on the floor of the Fujimoto living room, surrounded by building blocks. Itsuki had one hand on a half-finished tower. Ayane was leaning toward him, holding a piece that did not seem to fit anywhere.

“We used to play here all the time,” she said.

“Your house had more space.”

“Your apartment was smaller.”

“We didn't live in the apartment then.”

“I know.”

She turned another page.

There were more photographs. A school trip. A birthday party. A rainy afternoon with several children gathered around a board game. Their families appeared in the background, smiling at the camera.

Itsuki recognised the people, but the moments came back only in fragments.

A smell. A voice. A colour.

He remembered being close to Ayane. He did not remember the exact day they had stopped being close.

Perhaps there had not been one.

“Do you ever miss it?” Ayane asked.

He looked at her.

“The old days?”

She nodded.

“Sometimes.”

“Me too.”

She closed the album carefully.

“I thought maybe we could talk more this year,” she said. “Since we're in the same class.”

“That's fine.”

Ayane's expression softened, but she did not look entirely satisfied.

“Not because we have to,” she added. “I just thought it might be nice.”

Itsuki understood the difference. He was not sure he knew how to respond to it.

“Yeah,” he said. “It might.”

It was not a promise. It was not a rejection.

For now, it was honest.

They carried the albums back to the living room. Their parents had moved to the kitchen, where Itsuki's mother was helping clear the dishes. Ayane returned the box to the table.

When Itsuki left later that evening, Ayane walked him to the front door.

“See you at school,” she said.

“See you.”

She hesitated, then smiled.

“And happy birthday, by the way. I didn't get to say it on Wednesday.”

“Thanks.”

“I hope it was good.”

“It was ordinary.”

“That's not necessarily bad.”

“No,” he said. “I suppose not.”

He stepped outside.

The wind chime moved once behind him, making a small glass sound.

On the walk back to the station, he thought about the photographs. The children in them seemed familiar, but not quite like people he knew now. He wondered whether Ayane felt the same way.

He did not have an answer.

The train arrived before he reached one.

# Interlude V — The House He Doesn't Live In

The envelope arrived on a Thursday.

Itsuki found it in the mailbox downstairs, tucked between a supermarket flyer and a notice about the building's water inspection. His name was written on the front in his mother's handwriting.

Inside was a school document he had forgotten to ask her to sign.

There was also a note.

*If you have time, come by this weekend. I made too much curry.*

Itsuki read it twice.

He could have signed the document himself. The form required a guardian's confirmation, not a special ceremony. He could also have asked his mother to meet him at the station.

Instead, he sent a message.

*I'll come by Saturday to pick it up.*

Her reply arrived a few minutes later.

*I'll be here.*

The house looked the same when he arrived.

The entryway was clean. A pair of shoes stood beside the cabinet, aligned with the wall. The living room curtains were open, and a small plant sat near the window. Itsuki could hear the television from the kitchen.

His mother opened the door.

“Thank you for coming.”

“I needed the form.”

“I know. Come in.”

He removed his shoes and placed them beside the cabinet.

His stepfather appeared from the living room.

“Itsuki. Good to see you.”

“Hello.”

The man wore a pale shirt with the sleeves rolled to his elbows. He had been working at home, judging by the laptop on the table and the documents stacked beside it.

“Would you like something to drink?”

“No, thank you.”

“There's tea.”

“I'm fine.”

The man nodded. He did not insist.

His mother brought the envelope from the kitchen and handed it over.

“I signed it. You only need to return it to school.”

“Thanks.”

“Have you eaten?”

“Not yet.”

“Then stay for lunch.”

Itsuki glanced toward the kitchen.

“I have food at home.”

“You can eat here too.”

“I don't want to trouble you.”

His mother looked as if she wanted to say something else. The third husband spoke first.

“There's enough for everyone. But if you have plans, that's fine.”

Itsuki nodded.

“Thank you.”

He did not stay.

At the door, his mother touched his sleeve.

“You can come over whenever you want.”

“I know.”

“I mean it.”

“I know.”

She let go.

The third husband stood a little behind her.

“Take care on the way back.”

“I will.”

Outside, the air was cool. Itsuki walked toward the station with the signed form inside his bag.

He did not dislike the house because it was unpleasant.

That was the difficult part.

The third husband was polite. He made an effort. He did not shout or demand that Itsuki call him anything. He simply kept trying to make the place feel like a home they all shared.

Itsuki did not want that.

He could be civil without pretending otherwise.

At the crossing, his phone vibrated.

A message from his mother.

*I'm glad you came.*

Itsuki looked at the screen for a moment.

Then he typed:

*Thanks for signing the form.*

He put the phone away and waited for the light to change.

# Chapter Four — The Girl Everyone Seems to Know

Akari Fujisaki arrived at school with three people talking to her.

This was not unusual.

Itsuki had seen her in the corridor before, usually surrounded by classmates or being stopped by someone who wanted to ask a question. She was easy to recognise even from a distance: long dark reddish-brown hair, a friendly smile, and the sort of relaxed posture that made it look as if she had never once worried about where to put her hands.

He knew her name because everyone knew her name.

That was not the same as knowing her.

On Thursday morning, she stood near the shoe lockers, listening to a girl from another class explain something with great urgency. Akari nodded, asked a question, and laughed at the answer. When another student approached, she turned to include him in the conversation without making the first girl feel dismissed.

It looked effortless.

Itsuki suspected it wasn't.

He changed his indoor shoes and headed upstairs.

“Morning, Kurose.”

The voice belonged to Miyake, who was carrying a sports bag over one shoulder.

“Morning.”

“You know Fujisaki?”

Itsuki followed his glance toward the shoe lockers.

“Not really.”

“She's in 3-A, right? I heard she's helping with the welcome event.”

“Maybe.”

Miyake looked at him.

“You don't know much.”

“That's why I said maybe.”

Miyake laughed and continued toward the stairs.

Itsuki entered Class 3-B. Riko was already at her desk, drawing something in the margin of a worksheet.

“Is that a cat?” he asked.

“It's a teacher.”

“Which one?”

“I'm still deciding.”

The drawing had pointed ears and a very displeased expression.

Itsuki sat down.

“Ambitious.”

“Thank you.”

The morning lessons passed without incident. During the break before lunch, the class representative announced that the student committee would be collecting suggestions for the spring welcome event. The event was a small school tradition for new first-year students: club displays, a guided tour, and a short performance in the gym.

Riko raised her hand.

“Can we suggest cancelling it?”

“No,” the representative said.

“Can we suggest cancelling the suggestion box?”

“No.”

“It's a hostile system.”

Itsuki continued writing the date at the top of his notes.

Across the courtyard, the welcome-event committee had gathered near the main entrance. Akari stood with two other students, holding a clipboard. She listened while one of them pointed toward the gym, then checked a list and made a note.

A first-year student approached them, looking uncertain. Akari stepped forward and spoke to her. The girl relaxed almost immediately.

Itsuki looked away.

There was nothing particularly interesting about someone being kind to a nervous student. It was simply the sort of thing that happened at school.

At lunch, Riko sat on the edge of his desk.

“Do you have plans after school?”

“Homework.”

“That's not a plan.”

“It's a scheduled activity.”

“Come help me carry something.”

“What?”

“Something.”

“Is it heavy?”

“Probably.”

“Then ask someone stronger.”

“I'm asking someone more available.”

“I'm not available.”

Riko looked at his empty lunch box.

“You're finished eating.”

“That's unrelated.”

She slid off the desk.

“Fine. I'll ask Miyake.”

“Good.”

She walked away, then turned around.

“Unless you're scared.”

Itsuki looked at her.

“Of carrying something?”

“Of being useful.”

“That's a serious accusation.”

“I know.”

He went after her.

They ended up helping the class representative move a box of event leaflets from the staff room to the student committee office. It was not heavy. Riko had exaggerated, which was a common feature of her requests.

The corridor outside the office was busy. Students passed carrying poster boards, stacks of paper, and a bundle of extension cords. A teacher asked them to keep the walkway clear.

Akari came out of the office with a clipboard tucked under one arm.

“Sorry—could you let me through?”

Riko stepped aside.

“Of course.”

Akari smiled at her. “Thanks.”

Her eyes moved briefly to Itsuki.

“Thank you.”

He nodded.

That was all.

She continued down the corridor with another committee member. Riko watched her go.

“She's nice,” she said.

“Seems so.”

“Everyone likes her.”

“Probably.”

“Do you think she knows everyone?”

“Not everyone.”

“Maybe she has a list.”

“Maybe.”

Riko turned to him. “You really have no curiosity.”

“I have some.”

“About?”

“Whether the box is going to be put down.”

Riko laughed and took the other side of it.

They carried the leaflets into the office.

Later that afternoon, Itsuki saw Akari again near the courtyard. She was speaking with a teacher, pointing to a page on her clipboard. The teacher nodded, then gave her another sheet. Akari looked at it, asked something, and wrote down the answer.

She seemed busy.

That was the only impression Itsuki took away.

He did not know that she had spent the previous evening reviewing a set of English questions until the words began to blur together. He did not know that she had asked her older cousin for advice about a future she was not sure she wanted. He did not know that being liked by so many people could make it difficult to say no to any of them.

Those things belonged to Akari's day, not his.

For now, they were students in the same school who happened to pass each other in the corridor.

That was enough.

# Interlude II — The Version Akari Knows

Akari Fujisaki liked the sound of a busy classroom.

It meant people were talking. It meant someone had brought something to share, someone else had a story, and there was always a chance that a quiet corner would turn into a conversation if she crossed the room at the right moment.

She liked being known.

There was no secret resentment beneath it. She enjoyed meeting people, remembering names, and finding out what other classes were doing. She liked the way a school event could bring students together who might otherwise never speak.

What she did not enjoy was being expected to know what everyone wanted.

That was a different kind of attention.

“Akari, can you check the menu again?”

She looked up from her notebook.

Saki stood beside her desk, holding a printed sheet.

“Sure.”

“Do you think the prices are okay?”

Akari read through the list.

“Tea is fine. The juice might be a little expensive.”

“That's what I thought.”

“We can lower it by ten yen.”

Saki nodded and made a note.

“Thanks.”

Another classmate approached before Saki had taken two steps.

“Akari, do you know who has the spare tablecloths?”

“I think they're in the committee room. I'll ask.”

“Could you?”

“Of course.”

The classmate thanked her and hurried away.

Akari closed her notebook.

She had not been elected to anything. She had simply offered to help when the class started planning its festival activity. People tended to ask her questions because she usually knew who to ask next.

She liked that.

She also sometimes wished someone else would make the decision.

After school, she went to the library.

The English passage she had been working on was still open in her notebook. She had copied three sentences from the textbook and written their meanings underneath. The first two made sense. The third seemed to change direction halfway through.

She read it again.

Then again.

The words were familiar. She knew what each one meant. Together, they refused to become a sentence she could understand.

Akari pressed her pencil against the page.

“Why?”

The librarian looked up.

“Everything all right?”

“Sorry. Yes.”

She lowered her voice.

The problem was not that she disliked studying. She wanted to improve. She had a good memory for conversations, names, and small details. She could remember what a classmate had said weeks earlier and notice when a friend was having a difficult day.

But written passages did not always stay in her head.

She could read a paragraph and understand it, then reach the questions and forget which sentence had supported which answer. When she tried to memorise the structure, it became worse.

She did not want to ask her friends for help again.

They were kind, but they had already spent time explaining the same things. Akari disliked feeling as if she were taking more than she gave.

Her phone vibrated.

A message from her mother.

*Your father and I will be home late. There is food in the refrigerator. Don't wait for us.*

Akari replied with a small heart.

She packed her books and left the library.

At home, the entrance hall was quiet. Her family's house was large enough that one person could move through it without seeing anyone else. The kitchen was clean. A vase of flowers stood on the dining table, replaced every few days by someone who knew what colours her mother liked.

Akari changed out of her uniform and returned to her room.

On her desk was a brochure her parents had given her earlier in the month. It listed several universities and possible programmes. Her family had not demanded that she choose one immediately. They had asked her to think about the options and tell them what interested her.

They wanted her to have choices.

Sometimes, that made it harder to admit that she did not know which one she wanted.

She placed the brochure beneath her notebook.

Then she opened the English passage again.

She would understand it eventually.

She always did, if she kept trying.

# Chapter Five — A Quiet Seat by the Window

The library was the quietest place in the school, provided nobody had decided to whisper.

Itsuki preferred it to the classroom after lessons. The room was on the second floor of the old building, with tall windows facing the residential streets beyond the sports field. The shelves were arranged in a way that suggested several generations of librarians had disagreed about where books belonged. A small sign near the entrance asked students to return borrowed materials to the desk rather than reshelve them.

Itsuki approved of the sign.

He returned a book on modern Japanese history and checked the English section for a collection of short essays. He had not planned to borrow anything. He simply liked having something to read that did not come with a test date.

A chair moved softly near the windows.

He glanced over.

A girl sat at the far table, one hand resting beside an open notebook. Her hair was long and dark enough to appear almost blue where the afternoon light touched it. She wore a simple cardigan over her uniform and had arranged her books in a neat line.

Mio Kanzaki.

Itsuki knew the name from the class list. She was in 3-C. He had seen her in the library once or twice, usually alone.

She was reading.

That was not remarkable. It was a library.

He selected a book and moved toward the desk.

As he passed, he noticed that her notebook contained a small diagram. Several names were written in the margin, connected by lines. It looked like a family tree, or perhaps an outline for a history assignment.

Mio turned a page.

Itsuki kept walking.

At the desk, the librarian stamped the date inside the book.

“Back in two weeks,” she said.

“Thank you.”

He left the library.

The corridor outside was warmer than the room. Students were leaving in groups, and the noise from the courtyard had grown louder. Itsuki adjusted the strap of his bag and headed downstairs.

He did not think about Mio again until the following Tuesday.

He had stayed after class to finish a worksheet. When he reached the library, the same seat by the window was occupied.

Mio was not reading this time. She was looking out at the sports field, where a group of students were practising badminton under the late-afternoon sun. Her notebook lay open beside her, but she did not appear to be writing.

Itsuki chose a seat two tables away.

He worked through the first page of his English assignment. The passage was about urban planning, a subject he found more interesting than the questions made it seem. He underlined a sentence, wrote a short explanation, and checked the next paragraph.

A chair scraped softly.

He looked up.

Mio had stood and was returning a book to the shelf. She moved carefully, making sure the spine lined up with the others. Then she took another book from the shelf and returned to her seat.

No conversation.

No greeting.

A perfectly ordinary library afternoon.

When Itsuki finished, he closed his notebook and packed his things. As he passed the window table, Mio's pencil rolled off the edge.

It stopped near his shoe.

He picked it up and placed it on the corner of her desk.

“Thanks,” she said.

Her voice was quiet, but clear.

“Sure.”

That was the entirety of their conversation.

He left.

Outside, the sky had begun to turn pale gold. A train passed somewhere beyond the school buildings, its sound briefly rising above the traffic.

Itsuki walked toward the station.

He had learned one thing about Kanzaki Mio.

She used a mechanical pencil with a blue grip.

It was not a particularly useful fact.

He suspected he would forget it by tomorrow.

# Interlude IV — A Page Out of Order

Mio Kanzaki liked small mysteries.

Not the kind that required a detective agency or a dramatic confession. She preferred the ordinary ones: a book returned to the wrong shelf, a classroom window left open when everyone remembered closing it, a familiar umbrella that appeared in the lost-property box twice in one week.

Most of the time, the answer was simple.

That was part of the appeal.

On a Wednesday afternoon, she found a library book on the return cart with a bookmark sticking out from the middle.

The bookmark was a narrow strip of pale green paper. Someone had written a date on it.

April 18.

Mio checked the borrowing label.

The book had been returned two days late.

She looked toward the library desk.

“Did someone leave this?”

The librarian glanced over.

“Probably. You can put it in the lost-property tray.”

Mio took the bookmark out and examined it.

There was no name.

She placed it beside the book and returned both to the desk.

The next afternoon, the bookmark was gone.

The book was still on the shelf.

Mio noticed because she remembered the green paper.

She checked the return cart. Nothing.

Then she looked at the desk, where a small envelope had appeared beside the librarian's stamp.

The envelope was pale green.

Mio stared at it for a moment.

She had an idea.

She asked the librarian whether anyone had come looking for a lost bookmark.

“A student from 2-A, I think,” the librarian said. “She left it in a book and came back yesterday.”

Mio nodded.

The mystery was solved.

It was also less interesting than she had hoped.

She returned to her seat near the window.

Her phone vibrated.

A message from her mother.

*Your father and I will be late again. Dinner is in the refrigerator. There's fruit on the counter. Please remember to lock the balcony door.*

Mio replied:

*Okay. Have a good evening.*

A second message arrived.

*How was school?*

Mio looked at the empty page in her notebook.

*Normal. I found a lost bookmark.*

Her mother sent a smiling face.

*Good detective work.*

Mio smiled faintly and put the phone away.

Her parents were often busy. Their work schedules did not always match, and there were weeks when dinner was a matter of leaving food in the refrigerator and sending a message. They still asked about her day. They remembered her favourite tea. Her father had recently repaired the loose hinge on her desk drawer without being asked.

She did not think of them as uncaring.

She simply had a lot of time to herself.

Sometimes that time became reading.

Sometimes it became observation.

Sometimes it became a puzzle.

She opened her notebook and wrote:

*The person who left the bookmark returned for it.*

Then she crossed out the sentence.

It was not a useful mystery.

Outside, the badminton club was packing away its equipment. A student stood near the gate, looking through her bag. Another approached and held out a folded umbrella.

Mio watched them.

She thought the first student had forgotten the umbrella.

Then the second student said something, and the first shook her head. They both laughed.

Mio looked away.

She had been wrong.

It happened more often than people thought.

That was why she liked asking questions.

# Chapter Six — A City Made of Small Errands

Itsuki's apartment was close enough to school that he could walk if he felt like it and far enough away that he usually took the train.

The distinction mattered mostly in bad weather.

On Saturday morning, he walked to the shopping street with a list in his pocket. Rice, detergent, eggs, dish soap. He had written the list the previous night and folded it twice. The handwriting was neat. The order was not.

He bought rice first because it was heavy, then detergent because the store had a discount, then eggs because he had nearly forgotten them. At the small vegetable shop, the owner asked whether he was cooking for one.

“Usually.”

“You should eat more vegetables.”

“I do.”

The owner looked at the instant curry packet in his basket.

Itsuki looked at it too.

“Sometimes,” he amended.

The owner added two carrots to the bag.

He paid for them.

On the way home, he stopped at the convenience store near the station. A new poster advertised a spring campaign. Someone had drawn a small smile on the corner of the display card. It was probably a staff member. It was probably against the rules.

Itsuki bought tea and a rice ball.

Outside, he found a message from Nanami.

*Are you home?*

He typed back.

*Almost.*

*Good. I'm coming over.*

*You could have asked first.*

*I did. You answered.*

He looked at the message.

It was technically true.

By the time he reached the apartment, Nanami was waiting outside the entrance with a tote bag over one shoulder. She wore her school cardigan, though it was Saturday, and had tied her long hair loosely behind her neck.

“Hello,” she said.

“You're early.”

“You're slow.”

“I carried groceries.”

“That's a weak excuse.”

She took the lighter bag from his hand and followed him inside.

The first thing she did was open the refrigerator.

“Carrots.”

“Observant.”

“Eggs.”

“Very observant.”

“Rice.”

“I'm impressed.”

She turned to him. “You're eating properly.”

“Apparently.”

“Who threatened you?”

“The vegetable shop owner.”

Nanami laughed.

She unpacked her tote bag. Inside were two notebooks, a small pencil case, and a folder with the Hoshimiya East High School emblem printed on the front.

“School work?” Itsuki asked.

“Some of it. We have an inter-school activity next month.”

“What kind?”

“Library exchange. We're helping sort donated books and prepare a reading corner for the younger students.”

“Sounds useful.”

“It is. Also, my friend Yui volunteered me for the display team.”

“Did you want to do that?”

“I don't mind. She's good at making things, and I'm better at keeping the list organised.”

Nanami took a notebook from the folder and opened it to a page covered in small, tidy handwriting. There were columns for tasks, names, and dates.

Itsuki glanced at it.

“You made a schedule.”

“Someone has to.”

“I'm proud.”

“You sound surprised.”

“I've seen your room.”

“My room is organised.”

“Your desk is a different country.”

“It's a creative territory.”

He put the groceries away while she continued explaining the activity. Yui had suggested a theme based on local stories and old picture books. Another student wanted to include a small display about the history of the neighbourhood. Their teacher had approved both ideas, which meant Nanami now had to make sure the two groups did not order the same supplies.

“Do you like it?” he asked.

“It's fine.”

“That's not an answer.”

She gave him a look.

“You've been spending too much time with me.”

“It's possible.”

Nanami closed the notebook.

“I like having something to organise. And Yui gets excited about things. It's difficult to say no when she's already made a drawing.”

“Then don't say no.”

“That's not how it works.”

“Why not?”

“Because she looks at me like I've betrayed the future of literature.”

Itsuki considered that.

“Serious responsibility.”

“Exactly.”

They made lunch together. Nanami chopped the carrots while Itsuki prepared rice and warmed the curry. She complained that he cut the onions too thick. He told her that the onions had not filed a complaint.

They ate at the low table.

After lunch, Nanami spread her school notes across the floor. Itsuki sat at the other end with his English workbook. For a while, the only sounds were turning pages and the occasional scratch of pencil against paper.

“Do you remember the old library near the east station?” Nanami asked.

“The one with the blue sign?”

“Yeah. Our school is borrowing some of their picture books for the activity.”

“I remember it.”

“Dad used to take me there.”

Itsuki looked up.

Nanami kept her eyes on her notebook.

“He said he might help us get a few of the older books if the library approves. He knows someone on the committee.”

“That sounds like him.”

“He asked if you wanted to come too.”

Itsuki turned a page.

“When?”

“He didn't say. He just asked.”

“I'll see.”

Nanami nodded. She did not press him.

That was one of the things Itsuki appreciated about her. She could be persistent about small matters, but she usually knew when a question was not ready for an answer.

A little later, she stood and walked to the window.

“It's warmer today.”

“Not by much.”

“Still.”

She watched a bicycle pass below. Itsuki returned to his workbook.

“Are you going to stay for dinner?” he asked.

Nanami looked over her shoulder.

“Do you want me to?”

“There's enough curry.”

“That's not what I asked.”

He considered it.

“Sure.”

She smiled and returned to her notes.

The afternoon passed without anything important happening. Nanami worked on her schedule. Itsuki finished his English exercises. They argued about whether a certain sentence in the textbook was badly written. Nanami won by finding a more confusing sentence on the next page.

When evening came, she helped wash the dishes and folded the tea towels. Before leaving, she checked that she had packed every notebook.

At the door, she paused.

“Dad said he'll call tomorrow.”

“I know.”

“You can answer if you want.”

“I know.”

“Okay.”

She slipped on her shoes.

“See you Tuesday?”

“Probably.”

“That's a terrible way to say yes.”

“It's a realistic way.”

Nanami shook her head and left.

Itsuki locked the door.

The apartment was quiet again, but the afternoon had left small signs of another person: a pencil mark on the corner of the table, a notebook indentation on the cushion, and one carrot peel he had missed near the sink.

He cleaned the carrot peel.

He left the pencil mark.

# Interlude I — A Different School Bell

Hoshimiya East High School had a different morning rhythm.

The main building stood closer to the river than Hoshimiya Municipal High, and the path from the station passed a row of small shops that opened early. Students arrived in clusters, some carrying club equipment, others stopping at the bakery near the crossing. The bell rang five minutes earlier than the one at Itsuki's school.

Nanami knew this because she had once been late by exactly four minutes.

She had not repeated the mistake.

“Nanami!”

Yui waved from the bicycle racks.

Nanami hurried over, holding the strap of her bag.

“Morning.”

“You're early.”

“I'm on time.”

“That's early for you.”

“I was always on time.”

“You were late once.”

“One time.”

“It was memorable.”

“It was raining.”

“You blamed the rain.”

“It was raining.”

Yui laughed and fell into step beside her.

They were in different classes but had joined the library exchange committee together. Yui liked drawing and making displays. Nanami liked lists, schedules, and making sure the people who liked drawing remembered that glue had to dry before anyone moved the paper.

Their teacher had given them a small project: prepare a reading corner for a group of younger students who would visit the school library during the following month. They would borrow picture books from the old east-station library, arrange a few tables, and make a display about stories set in familiar places.

Yui had already drawn a sketch of the display.

It included a river, a train, a row of houses, and a cat sitting on a wall.

Nanami had asked why the cat was there.

“Because every town needs a cat,” Yui had said.

Nanami had written *cat decoration — optional* on the supply list.

Yui had crossed out *optional*.

Now they walked toward the classroom, discussing whether the display should use blue paper or pale green.

“Blue,” Yui said.

“Green.”

“Blue looks like the river.”

“Green looks like the park.”

“We can draw the river and the park.”

“That means two colours.”

“Exactly.”

Nanami looked at her.

“You're making the budget complicated.”

“I'm making the display beautiful.”

“Those are not the same thing.”

Yui smiled.

“You always say that.”

“Because it's true.”

They reached the classroom before the bell. Nanami placed her bag beside her desk and took out the folder. The supply list had grown to two pages. She checked the names of the students who had agreed to help and marked the tasks that still needed someone.

Her friend leaned over.

“You really like organising things.”

“I like knowing what's supposed to happen.”

“Same thing.”

“Not exactly.”

Yui looked at the page.

“You put yourself down for the book return.”

“Someone has to carry the boxes.”

“I can do that.”

“You're making the display.”

“I can do both.”

“You'll forget one.”

Yui placed a hand over her chest.

“That's hurtful.”

“It's accurate.”

They laughed.

During lunch, Nanami received a message from her father.

*How is the library project going? I spoke to the committee member at the east-station branch. They can lend you the picture books if your teacher sends the request this week.*

Nanami typed back.

*We have a list ready. I'll ask the teacher to send it.*

A moment later, another message appeared.

*Good. Are you coming home this weekend?*

She looked at the screen.

Her father's house was still her home. She knew that. Her room was there, her school books were there, and her father still remembered which foods she disliked. But the house had changed. Sometimes, when he had plans with his girlfriend, Nanami felt as if she were visiting a place where everyone else already knew what the evening would look like.

It was not anyone's fault.

That did not make it comfortable every time.

She replied:

*Maybe Saturday. I'll let you know.*

Her father sent a simple thumbs-up.

Nanami put the phone away.

Yui returned with two cartons of milk.

“Everything okay?”

“Yeah.”

“You look like you're doing maths.”

“I'm thinking.”

“That's worse.”

Nanami smiled.

“It's fine.”

Yui did not ask again. She sat beside her and began drawing the cat for the reading-corner display.

Nanami watched the pencil move.

The cat was wearing a tiny school cap.

“That's not necessary,” she said.

“It is now.”

Nanami opened her notebook and added one more line to the supply list.

*Small paper cat — one.*

She did not know whether the project would be good.

But she was looking forward to it.

# Interlude VI — A Book for the Other School

The old library near the east station had a blue sign and a narrow entrance.

Nanami remembered the place as larger than it looked now. When she was younger, she had spent entire afternoons there with her father, choosing picture books and sitting at a low table near the window. The children's section had been moved to the back, but the shelves were still arranged in the same order.

Itsuki arrived a few minutes after she did.

“You're late,” Nanami said.

“I'm on time.”

“Dad's been here for ten minutes.”

“Then I'm ten minutes late for Dad.”

Her father stood beside the entrance desk, holding a paper cup of coffee. He wore a dark jacket and carried a slim work bag. He looked composed, as usual, though his tie was loosened slightly.

“It'suki,” he said.

“Hello.”

“Thank you for coming.”

Nanami held up the folder.

“We brought the request.”

“Good. The librarian has already checked the list.”

They spoke with the librarian for a few minutes. The school wanted to borrow a small collection of picture books and local-history books for the reading corner. The library could lend them, provided the school completed the forms and returned everything by the end of the term.

Nanami asked several questions about the dates.

Itsuki listened while their father checked the paperwork.

“You've made a careful list,” he said to Nanami.

“I had to. Yui keeps adding things.”

“Is that the friend who draws?”

“That's her.”

“Then the display will probably look good.”

Nanami smiled.

After the paperwork was settled, the librarian led them to the children's section. Nanami moved along the shelves, checking titles against her list. Itsuki carried the empty crate. Their father helped the librarian identify which books were suitable for the younger students.

At one point, Nanami found an old picture book she remembered.

“This one.”

Itsuki looked at the cover.

A small illustration showed a train crossing a bridge beside a river.

“You used to borrow that.”

“I know.”

“You made me read it.”

“You liked it.”

“I tolerated it.”

“You asked for it twice.”

“I was being polite.”

Their father smiled.

“I remember that. You both wanted the same book.”

Nanami turned toward him.

“See?”

Itsuki lifted the crate.

“Are we finished?”

“Almost,” their father said.

They checked out the books and carried them to the car. The crate was heavier than it looked. Itsuki took one side, their father the other.

“You don't have to carry that,” the older man said.

“It's fine.”

“I know. I was only offering.”

Itsuki adjusted his grip.

They placed the crate in the back seat.

Their father looked at him.

“How is school?”

“Normal.”

“Are the classes difficult?”

“Not particularly.”

“Have you thought about what you might do after graduation?”

“Not yet.”

His father nodded.

“You don't need to decide everything immediately. But it may help to look at a few options before the year gets busy.”

“I know.”

“Good.”

There was no lecture. No comparison with other students. No suggestion that Itsuki owed him a plan.

Nanami came out of the library carrying the folder.

“Are you two finished having a serious conversation?”

“We weren't having one,” Itsuki said.

“That's what makes it serious.”

Their father laughed.

They went to a small restaurant near the station for lunch. Nanami ordered a set meal. Itsuki chose soba. Their father checked his phone once, replied to a message, and put it away.

For most of the meal, they talked about Nanami's project.

She explained the reading-corner layout and Yui's insistence on including a paper cat. Their father asked whether the school needed any additional materials. Nanami said they had enough.

When lunch was over, he paid.

“You don't have to,” Itsuki said.

“I invited you.”

“I can pay for mine.”

“I know.”

Their father looked at him calmly.

“Let me this time.”

Itsuki did not argue.

At the station, Nanami hugged their father goodbye. Itsuki bowed his head.

“Take care,” the older man said.

“You too.”

“I'll call next week.”

“Okay.”

Their father watched them walk toward the ticket gates.

Nanami was quiet until they reached the platform.

“He still worries about you.”

“I know.”

“He asks about you when I'm there.”

“I know.”

She looked at him.

“You could visit more.”

Itsuki watched the train display.

“I'll see.”

Nanami did not push.

The train arrived.

They got on together.

# Chapter Seven — The Things We Don't Ask Twice

Rain began during the last period on Monday.

It tapped against the classroom windows while Mr. Hasegawa explained the week's schedule. By the time the final bell rang, the sports field had darkened and the path beside the gym was shining with water.

Itsuki had brought an umbrella.

Riko had not.

She stood near the shoe lockers, looking outside with an expression of deep personal betrayal.

“Forgot yours?” Itsuki asked.

“I was optimistic.”

“About the weather?”

“About the world.”

He opened his umbrella.

Riko looked at it.

“Are you offering?”

“No.”

“Good. I wouldn't want to accept.”

She stepped under it.

Itsuki glanced at her.

“You just accepted.”

“I changed my mind.”

They walked toward the station together. Riko lived in the opposite direction from the bus stop, but she had decided that the first part of the route was shared territory. She talked about a teacher who had assigned an essay over the weekend and a classmate who had written the title at the bottom of the page.

At the crossing, they separated.

“Don't catch a cold,” Riko said.

“Try not to lose your homework.”

“No promises.”

She ran toward the bus stop.

Itsuki continued along the pavement.

Near the entrance to the shopping street, he saw Ayane standing beneath the awning of a closed flower shop. She held a transparent umbrella in one hand and a paper bag in the other. A few drops of rain had caught in her hair.

“Itsuki?”

He stopped.

“Fujimoto.”

“Are you going to the station?”

“Yeah.”

“Me too.”

They started walking in the same direction.

Ayane held her umbrella slightly higher to avoid brushing his. They had walked this route together many times as children. Back then, they had usually been carrying something from one house to the other: a lunch container, a forgotten textbook, a toy, a bag of sweets. They had rarely needed to decide who walked beside whom.

Now they were careful not to step too close.

“Do you still take the train from here?” she asked.

“Most days.”

“I thought you might walk.”

“Only when the weather is good.”

She looked at the rain.

“Not today, then.”

“Probably not.”

They passed the florist. A row of buckets stood inside the window, their flowers bright against the grey afternoon.

Ayane glanced at the paper bag she was carrying.

“My mother asked me to bring this to your mother.”

“What is it?”

“Some food containers. She borrowed them last week.”

“Right.”

“She said she'd return them at the next family gathering, but then she remembered your mother might need them.”

“That's how containers work.”

Ayane laughed.

The conversation loosened a little.

They reached the narrow lane that connected the shopping street to the station road. A small playground stood behind a low fence. The swing chains moved slightly in the wind, though no one was there.

Ayane slowed.

“Do you remember this place?”

Itsuki looked through the fence.

“Yeah.”

“We used to come here after school.”

“Your mother used to bring snacks.”

“And you always wanted the same one.”

“Which one?”

“Those little fish-shaped biscuits.”

“Taiyaki?”

“No. The small ones in the packet.”

“That's not helpful.”

“You called them tiny fish.”

“That seems accurate.”

Ayane smiled.

“I remember you used to line them up before eating them.”

Itsuki looked at her.

“I did?”

“Every time.”

“I don't remember that.”

“You were very particular.”

“Maybe I was hungry.”

“Maybe.”

They stood beneath their umbrellas for a moment, watching the rain collect in the shallow dips of the playground path.

“I used to think we'd always come here,” Ayane said.

Itsuki followed her gaze.

“Kids think things like that.”

“Do they?”

“Probably.”

She nodded, but did not seem convinced.

“I don't think we stopped being friends because anything happened,” she said. “We just got busy. Then it became normal not to call.”

Itsuki adjusted the umbrella handle.

“Yeah.”

“I wasn't sure if you wanted me to.”

He looked at her.

“Call?”

“Or talk more. I didn't know if you preferred being left alone.”

“I usually do.”

Ayane smiled faintly.

“I know.”

“Not always.”

She looked up.

Itsuki realised he had said more than he intended. He could have left it there, but the rain made the silence feel too noticeable.

“I don't mind talking,” he said. “I just don't always know what to say.”

Ayane's expression softened.

“That's okay.”

A train announcement sounded from the station.

They continued walking.

At the entrance, Ayane stopped beside the ticket machines.

“Thanks for walking with me.”

“We were going the same way.”

“Still.”

She shifted the paper bag to her other hand.

“Maybe we could have lunch together sometime. At school.”

“Sure.”

“Really?”

“It's lunch. I usually eat.”

Ayane laughed.

“Right. I'll ask you later.”

She went through the gate.

Itsuki watched her disappear into the crowd, then tapped his travel card against the reader.

The train was three minutes away.

He stood on the platform and listened to the rain against the roof.

He had not promised anything significant. Lunch was lunch. A conversation was a conversation. There was no reason to treat either as a turning point.

Still, he found himself hoping he would remember to say yes when she asked.

# Interlude IX — Lunch on the Second Floor

Ayane asked him on a Wednesday.

“Would you like to have lunch together?”

Itsuki looked up from his desk.

Riko, who was sitting on the chair beside his, immediately became interested.

“Together?” she repeated.

Ayane glanced at her.

“If that's okay.”

“Of course,” Riko said. “I'm very supportive.”

Itsuki looked at her.

“You don't have to come.”

“I wasn't invited.”

“Then why are you answering?”

“Because I'm supportive.”

Ayane laughed.

“Maybe another time, Riko.”

Riko placed a hand over her heart.

“Rejected by the committee.”

Itsuki packed his lunch.

Ayane waited near the classroom door. They went to the second-floor terrace, where a few students were eating beneath the shade of the overhang. The school had placed several benches there, but most were occupied.

They found a quiet corner near the railing.

The view was mostly rooftops and the sports field. A line of trees marked the edge of the school grounds. Beyond them, the station district rose in low buildings and signs.

Ayane opened her lunch box.

“I made too much rice.”

“That's a common problem.”

“Would you like some?”

“I'm fine.”

She smiled.

“Still the same.”

Itsuki looked at her.

“Do you remember when we used to trade lunch?”

“Sometimes.”

“You always wanted the fried egg.”

“You always wanted the pickled vegetables.”

“Because you didn't like them.”

“I didn't dislike them.”

“You gave them to me every time.”

“That was generosity.”

Ayane laughed.

The conversation moved more easily than it had during their first few days in the same class. They talked about teachers, classmates, and the new schedule. Ayane told him about a neighbourhood event her family helped organise. Itsuki told her about the library committee.

“You chose the library?” she asked.

“Yeah.”

“That makes sense.”

“Why?”

“You always liked quiet places.”

“I still do.”

She nodded.

“I thought so.”

They ate for a while.

A breeze moved across the terrace, lifting the corner of a napkin. Itsuki caught it before it fell.

Ayane watched him.

“You still notice small things.”

“It's a napkin.”

“You used to notice when I forgot things.”

“You forgot things often.”

“That's true.”

She looked out toward the field.

“I used to think you remembered everything.”

“I don't.”

“I know that now.”

There was a small pause.

Ayane closed her lunch box.

“I don't want us to only talk about the past,” she said. “I like remembering it. But I don't want to make you feel like you have to be the person you were when we were children.”

Itsuki looked at her.

“I'm not.”

“I know.”

She smiled.

“That's why I wanted to have lunch.”

He considered that.

“Then we can do it again.”

Ayane's expression brightened.

“Really?”

“Unless you change your mind.”

“I won't.”

The bell rang.

They gathered their things and returned to class.

At the door, Ayane said, “Thanks for lunch.”

“You brought your own.”

“I meant the company.”

Itsuki nodded.

“Sure.”

She went to her seat.

Riko leaned over the back of her chair as he passed.

“Supportive committee approves.”

“Go to class.”

“I'm already in class.”

“Then be quiet.”

Riko grinned.

The afternoon lesson began.

Itsuki opened his notebook.

He had not expected lunch to feel different.

It had not been especially exciting. They had eaten, talked, and returned to class.

But the next time Ayane asked, he knew he would say yes without needing to think about it.

# Chapter Eight — A Test with Too Many Questions

The first English assessment of the year was scheduled for Friday.

It was not a major exam. Mr. Hasegawa called it a diagnostic test, which meant the school wanted to know what students remembered before the real work began.

Itsuki finished the reading section with twelve minutes left.

He checked his answers once, corrected a careless article, and turned the page over.

Across the room, Riko was staring at question fourteen.

She had been staring at question fourteen for some time.

When the bell rang, she put her pencil down with the careful movement of someone leaving a dangerous object behind.

“How was it?” Itsuki asked as they left the classroom.

“Unfair.”

“Which part?”

“All of it.”

“That's broad.”

“It's a broad injustice.”

He glanced at the worksheet in her hand.

“You missed the second passage?”

“I read it.”

“Then?”

“I disagreed with it.”

“It's a reading test.”

“Exactly. They should test whether the passage is worth reading.”

He did not answer.

Riko folded the paper and slipped it into her bag.

“Don't look at me like that.”

“Like what?”

“Like you're about to explain the answer.”

“I wasn't.”

“You were thinking about it.”

“Maybe.”

“Keep your thoughts to yourself.”

They reached the corridor outside the staff room. A few students were waiting to ask about their scores. Mr. Hasegawa stood near the door, speaking with another teacher.

“Ah, Kurose,” he called when he saw Itsuki. “A moment?”

Itsuki stopped.

Riko leaned toward him.

“Good luck with your promotion.”

“Go to class.”

“Cruel.”

She walked away.

Mr. Hasegawa held out a paper.

“You did well. Your answers are accurate, and your explanation for the final question is particularly clear.”

Itsuki looked at the page. A red mark circled the paragraph he had written.

“Thank you.”

“Would you mind if I used this as an example for the class? I'll remove your name.”

“That's fine.”

“Good. It may help some students see how to support an answer with the passage rather than guessing from the wording.”

Itsuki nodded.

Mr. Hasegawa placed the paper on his folder.

“By the way, Fujisaki from 3-A asked whether the school has any peer-support sessions for English. I told her we'd look into it.”

Itsuki did not know why the teacher had mentioned that.

“Okay.”

“She seems to be putting in effort. Some students have a good ear for language but struggle with reading structure. It can be frustrating.”

“Yeah.”

Mr. Hasegawa smiled.

“Enjoy the rest of your day.”

Itsuki returned to class.

He did not think much about the conversation.

At lunch, the class representative announced that the diagnostic results would be returned the following week. Riko asked whether the school would provide emotional compensation for low scores. The representative said no.

After lessons, Itsuki went to the library to return his essay collection.

Akari was there.

She stood near the reference shelves with a notebook open in one hand. Her expression was focused, and the usual group around her was absent. She read a paragraph, frowned, then tried again.

Itsuki approached the desk.

The librarian was sorting returned books. He placed his book on the counter and waited.

Akari looked up at the sound.

“Sorry,” she said, stepping aside.

“It's fine.”

She glanced at the book in his hand.

“Are you returning that?”

“Yeah.”

“Was it good?”

“Some of it.”

“That's a very strong review.”

“It's a collection.”

She smiled.

“Fair enough.”

The librarian stamped the return date. Itsuki moved away from the desk.

As he passed the reference shelves, he noticed that Akari's notebook contained a list of vocabulary words. Several had been underlined twice. She was not wasting time.

“Do you know what 'subsequently' means?” she asked suddenly.

Itsuki stopped.

“After that. Something happens later as a result of what came before.”

“That's what I thought.”

She looked at the sentence in the book.

“Then why does it sound wrong here?”

He read the paragraph from a respectful distance.

“The sentence is using it to connect two events, but the second one isn't really a consequence. It's just the next event.”

Akari looked at the page again.

“So it's not the word. It's the relationship between the sentences.”

“Right.”

She considered that, then made a small note.

“Thanks.”

“Sure.”

There was no dramatic change in her expression. She did not suddenly look at him as if she had discovered a hidden genius. She simply understood the sentence better than she had a minute earlier.

“Are you in 3-B?” she asked.

“Yeah.”

“I thought so. I'm Akari Fujisaki.”

“I know.”

Her smile turned a little amused.

“Of course. Everyone knows everyone here.”

“Not everyone.”

“Maybe not.”

She closed her notebook.

“Thanks again, Kurose.”

He nodded and left.

Outside the library, the late-afternoon light fell across the corridor floor. Itsuki adjusted his glasses and headed for the stairs.

He had answered a question.

That was all.

He did not know that Akari would remember the explanation later, when she was trying to understand why a paragraph refused to make sense. He did not know that she would begin to notice how patiently he listened when someone asked him something. And he certainly did not know that, a few weeks from now, she would consider asking him for help again.

For now, she was simply a classmate from 3-A.

And he had somewhere to be.

# Interlude VII — The Shape of an Explanation

The study-support board was placed beside the classroom door.

Itsuki had expected it to be a single sheet of paper.

Instead, Mr. Hasegawa had given him a small section of the notice board and asked him to prepare an example that would help students understand how to answer reading questions.

“You don't have to write a lesson,” the teacher had said. “Just show the steps you use.”

Itsuki had spent twenty minutes deciding what those steps were.

He eventually wrote four lines:

1. Read the question first.
2. Find the sentence that answers it.
3. Check the sentence before and after.
4. Explain the connection in your own words.

Underneath, he added a short example from a passage about public transport.

The board was not particularly attractive.

Riko examined it during the break.

“Very exciting.”

“Thank you.”

“Where's the mascot?”

“Rejected.”

“Oppression.”

Emi stopped beside them.

“I think it's helpful,” she said.

“That's because you read it,” Riko replied.

Emi ignored her and looked at the example.

“I always forget to check the sentence before.”

“That's where the context usually is,” Itsuki said.

“Could you explain this part?”

He pointed to the paragraph.

“The question asks why the city changed the bus route. The answer isn't in one sentence. The first sentence gives the problem, and the next one explains the change. You need both.”

Emi read it again.

“Oh. I see.”

Riko leaned over.

“So the answer is that the bus was lonely?”

“No,” Itsuki said.

“That's what I got.”

“Then you should read it again.”

She made a face.

Emi laughed.

A few minutes later, Mr. Hasegawa came by to check the board.

“Good work, Kurose.”

“It's simple.”

“Simple is useful.”

The teacher looked at Emi.

“Did it help?”

“Yes. I understood the example.”

“Good. That's what matters.”

He moved on.

Itsuki returned to his seat.

Riko tapped the corner of the board.

“You know, if you keep doing things like this, people might start asking you for help.”

“That would be unfortunate.”

“You could charge.”

“Then nobody would ask.”

“Exactly. A business plan.”

Itsuki looked at the board.

He had not thought of it as anything worth noticing. He had simply written down the steps he used when a passage became confusing.

But if it helped someone else, that was fine.

He put the marker back in the tray.

# Interlude III — The Joke That Lands

Riko Tachibana was a well-behaved daughter.

At home, she put her shoes away properly, greeted her parents, washed her hands before dinner, and answered questions without making a performance out of it.

Her school friends would have found the information difficult to believe.

She arrived home at 6:12 p.m. and placed her bag beside the cabinet.

“I'm home.”

“Welcome back,” her mother called from the kitchen. “Dinner will be ready soon.”

Riko changed into house clothes and washed her hands. When she returned, her father was at the dining table reading through a document. He looked up.

“How was school?”

“Fine.”

“Any tests?”

“English and mathematics.”

“How did they go?”

“English was fine.”

“And mathematics?”

Riko took a glass from the cupboard.

“Mathematics happened.”

Her father lowered the document.

“That doesn't tell me anything.”

“It tells you the subject was present.”

Her mother glanced toward them from the kitchen.

“Riko.”

“Sorry.”

She sat down.

Her father did not sound angry. He rarely raised his voice. He simply wanted to know what she was doing, whether she was keeping up, and whether she had thought about the next step after graduation. The questions were reasonable.

They were also frequent.

Riko took out the test paper from her bag.

Her score was not disastrous. It was not good, either. She had understood most of the questions, but had lost marks through careless mistakes and two problems she had abandoned halfway through.

Her father read it.

“You can do better than this.”

“I know.”

“Then you need to spend more time on it.”

“I will.”

He returned the paper.

“Let's make a study schedule for next week.”

Riko nodded.

Her mother set dinner on the table. The conversation moved to groceries, a relative's visit, and whether the family needed to replace the rice cooker. Riko listened and answered when asked. She helped clear the dishes after dinner and wiped the table.

Later, she sat at her desk.

Her room was neat. The textbooks were arranged by subject. A small calendar hung beside the desk, with school deadlines marked in different colours. She opened the mathematics workbook and began with the first question.

The first few went well.

Then she reached a problem that required three steps.

She read it twice.

The answer did not come.

Riko tapped her pencil against the page.

At school, she could make a joke when she did not understand something. She could turn a wrong answer into a performance, make the people around her laugh, and move on before anyone had time to look too closely.

At home, she could not do that.

She had to sit with the problem.

She tried again.

The second attempt was wrong.

She erased it.

The third attempt was closer.

Her phone lit up beside the workbook.

A message from Itsuki.

*You left your worksheet in the classroom.*

Riko looked at the screen.

She typed:

*Keep it. It's a historical document.*

His reply came a minute later.

*It's folded in half.*

*Preservation damage.*

*I'll put it on your desk tomorrow.*

She smiled.

*Thanks.*

Then, after a moment, she added:

*Did you understand question fourteen?*

His answer arrived quickly.

*Yes.*

She stared at the word.

Then she typed:

*I hate you.*

*That's not a question.*

Riko laughed quietly.

Her mother called from the hallway.

“Everything okay?”

“Yes.”

“Don't stay up too late.”

“I won't.”

She returned to the workbook.

The problem still looked unpleasant.

But she tried it once more.

***

At school the next morning, Riko arrived with the worksheet completed.

Itsuki looked at it when she placed it on his desk.

“You did it.”

“Unfortunately.”

“Question fourteen?”

“Especially question fourteen.”

He checked the working.

“You used the wrong sign here.”

Riko leaned over.

“It's decorative.”

“Again?”

“It's a recurring design choice.”

He pointed to the next line.

“You got the answer right anyway.”

“See? Artistic freedom.”

“Don't tell the teacher.”

She grinned.

Itsuki returned the paper.

Riko took it and slid into her seat.

The joke had landed.

The work was still hers.

# Chapter Nine — The Committee Nobody Wanted

By the middle of May, the school had developed a new sound.

It was the sound of people asking where the forms were.

The first-year welcome event had passed, the class photographs had been taken, and the teachers had begun using the phrase “the first term is moving quickly” with increasing confidence. The school corridors were no longer full of students who looked lost. Everyone had found a route between classrooms, a preferred vending machine, and at least one place where they could sit without being asked to move.

Itsuki had found a place near the library windows.

Riko had found three places where she was not supposed to sit.

The school festival was still more than a month away, but preparation had begun in the form of a committee notice pinned beside the staff room. Each class would choose an activity, submit a plan, and assign students to the work. The notice included a list of approved options and a reminder that food preparation required supervision.

Riko read it over Itsuki's shoulder.

“Let's do a haunted house.”

“No.”

“You haven't heard the proposal.”

“I heard the first three words.”

“That's enough to make an informed decision.”

“Then we're done.”

She leaned closer to the paper.

“What about a café?”

“Someone will spill something.”

“That's why we have towels.”

“What about a quiz room?”

“People will complain.”

“A game booth?”

“People will cheat.”

Riko turned to him.

“You're very good at finding problems.”

“Thank you.”

“It's not a compliment.”

“I'll accept it anyway.”

Their class representative, Emi Naruse, approached with a clipboard.

“Have either of you decided what you'd like to help with?”

Riko pointed at Itsuki.

“He's our risk-assessment department.”

“I didn't agree to that.”

“You're doing a good job.”

Emi looked between them, then wrote something on her clipboard.

“I'll put you down for general preparation.”

Itsuki watched the pen move.

“That's not a role.”

“It is now,” Riko said.

Emi smiled apologetically. “We need people who can help move things, check supplies, and keep track of the schedule. You don't have to decide the actual activity yet.”

Itsuki considered asking to be removed.

Then he saw the stack of forms in Emi's arms and the way she was trying to keep them from sliding onto the floor.

“Fine,” he said. “I can help with the schedule.”

“Thank you.”

Riko gave him a pleased look.

“You're growing.”

“I'm being exploited.”

“Those are not mutually exclusive.”

The class eventually voted for a small café with a display of local photographs and a corner for simple games. It was not the most original idea, but it was manageable. Emi would coordinate the plan. Riko volunteered to handle decorations, which worried Itsuki more than the budget.

“What?” she said when he mentioned it.

“Nothing.”

“Your face says something.”

“My face is neutral.”

“Your face is filing a complaint.”

She had already begun sketching ideas in the corner of her notebook. Most involved paper lanterns, coloured signs, or something that looked suspiciously like a cardboard monster.

Itsuki pointed at the monster.

“What is that?”

“A mascot.”

“For a café?”

“It's a hungry customer.”

“That's not reassuring.”

“People like honesty.”

Across the room, Ayane was discussing the class activity with Emi. She had been assigned to help organise the display board and had already made a list of students who could bring photographs of the neighbourhood. When she noticed Itsuki looking, she gave him a small wave.

He returned it.

Riko noticed.

“Fujimoto's helping with the display.”

“I can see that.”

“You could help her.”

“I am helping the class.”

“That's not what I meant.”

Itsuki looked at her.

Riko smiled innocently.

He returned to the schedule.

The following week, the class began collecting ideas for the display. Ayane brought a folder of photographs from the local community centre. Most showed familiar streets: the station before its renovation, the shopping street during a summer event, the river path in winter. She explained where each picture had been taken and asked whether the class could use copies.

Itsuki recognised the small playground near the shopping street.

“That one hasn't changed much,” he said.

Ayane followed his gaze.

“No. The swings are newer.”

“The fence is the same.”

“I think so.”

They stood beside the display board while other students arranged the photographs.

“Do you want to help choose the captions?” she asked.

“Sure.”

They worked through the folder. Ayane wrote the first draft of each caption. Itsuki checked dates and spelling. They disagreed about whether one photograph showed the old station entrance or the bus terminal. A student from another class settled the question by recognising the sign in the background.

It was not a particularly meaningful afternoon.

That was why it went well.

By the time they finished, the display board had begun to look like something people might actually stop to read. Ayane thanked him, then asked whether he would be at school early the next morning.

“Probably.”

“Could you help me carry the remaining boards?”

“Sure.”

She smiled.

“Thanks.”

Riko appeared at his elbow.

“Look at you. Becoming indispensable.”

“I carried cardboard.”

“Every great career begins somewhere.”

Itsuki took the schedule from her.

“Your decorations are missing a materials list.”

Riko blinked.

Then she snatched the paper back.

“That's classified.”

“It's blank.”

“Classified blank.”

The preparations continued.

By the end of May, the class had chosen its colour scheme, submitted the activity plan, and received approval from the teacher in charge. The café would use a simple menu, the display would feature photographs of Hoshimiya, and the game corner would have a ring toss and a small prize table.

Riko's cardboard mascot had been rejected.

She claimed this was censorship.

Itsuki considered it an improvement.

One afternoon, as they were packing the display materials, Emi asked Itsuki to take a folder to the committee office.

“It's just the updated schedule,” she said. “Could you hand it to Fujisaki from 3-A? She's helping coordinate the shared equipment list.”

Itsuki looked at the folder.

“Why me?”

“Because you're going that way.”

He was not.

But the committee office was on the same floor as the library, and arguing would take longer than walking.

He took the folder.

The office door was open. Akari stood beside a table covered with forms and supply lists. Two other students were comparing the number of extension cords needed for the classrooms.

“Excuse me,” Itsuki said.

Akari turned.

“Oh, hello.”

He held out the folder.

“From 3-B. The updated schedule.”

“Thank you.”

She accepted it and opened the cover.

“Emi sent this?”

“Yeah.”

Akari checked the first page, then nodded.

“That's much clearer. The previous version had two classes listed for the same storage room.”

“That's probably why she changed it.”

“Could you tell her thanks?”

“Sure.”

Akari looked up.

“Kurose, right?”

He nodded.

“Thanks for bringing it over.”

“No problem.”

He left.

It was the second time they had spoken for more than a few words. Neither of them seemed to think that made it important.

Outside the office, the corridor was crowded with students carrying supplies. Itsuki moved aside to let a group pass.

A familiar voice called his name.

“Hey.”

Mio Kanzaki stood near the library entrance, holding two books against her chest. Her long midnight-blue hair fell over one shoulder. She gave a small nod.

Itsuki nodded back.

“Hi.”

She walked past him toward the office.

That was all.

The school year continued.

# Chapter Ten — Before the Doors Open

The final week of June arrived with a heat that seemed to have been waiting behind the calendar.

By Thursday, the classrooms were warm before the first lesson. Students opened the windows, then complained when the wind carried in dust from the sports field. The school had placed extra water dispensers near the gym, and teachers reminded everyone to take breaks during festival preparation.

Itsuki's class had been assigned the old home-economics room for storage. The room contained folding tables, spare chairs, and a collection of items that nobody had used in years. A faded poster about food hygiene still hung beside the sink.

Riko stood in the doorway, looking around.

“It's perfect.”

“It's dusty.”

“Perfectly dusty.”

“That's not a feature.”

She walked toward a stack of cardboard boxes.

“Where do you want these?”

“Not there.”

“Helpful.”

“Near the wall. The shelves are unstable.”

Riko looked at the shelves.

“They seem fine.”

“Last year, one of them tilted.”

“Did it fall?”

“No.”

“Then it was successful.”

Itsuki moved the boxes himself.

They had spent the morning preparing the class display. Ayane was checking the photograph captions. Emi was updating the equipment schedule. Riko was decorating the café signs with coloured paper. Itsuki had been asked to make a list of supplies and confirm that the class had enough extension cords, tape, and tablecloths.

It was not difficult work.

It was, however, the kind of work that became more complicated when several people tried to do it at once.

“Where's the blue tape?” Emi asked.

“On the table,” Itsuki said.

“That's green.”

“Then the blue tape is not on the table.”

Riko held up a roll.

“Found it.”

“Where was it?”

“Under the table.”

Itsuki looked at her.

She smiled.

He returned to the list.

At lunch, Ayane brought over a tray of sandwiches from the staff room. The teacher supervising the festival had asked the class to eat before the afternoon preparation period began.

“There's enough for everyone,” she said.

Riko took one.

“Fujimoto, you're saving our lives.”

“It's just lunch.”

“Exactly. The foundation of civilisation.”

Itsuki took a sandwich.

Ayane sat beside Emi and began talking about the display. She had found a photograph of the river path taken in early spring. The image showed the water reflecting the pale sky, with a row of bicycles parked along the embankment.

“Maybe we should put this near the entrance,” she said. “It's one of the places people recognise.”

Emi nodded.

“Good idea.”

Riko leaned over the photograph.

“Can we put a monster in the river?”

“No,” Itsuki said.

“You haven't heard the concept.”

“I've heard enough.”

Ayane laughed.

The afternoon was busier. Students from other classes moved through the corridor carrying decorations and equipment. The festival committee had placed signs near the stairs to direct people toward the correct rooms. A teacher reminded everyone not to block the emergency exits.

Akari appeared in the doorway shortly after two.

She was carrying a clipboard and speaking to another student. Her class was preparing a café as well, though with a different theme. The two classes shared a storage area for some of the tables and display boards.

“Sorry to interrupt,” she said. “Has anyone seen the spare folding sign stands?”

Itsuki checked the supply list.

“We have two in the storage room.”

“Are they available?”

“I think so. They're not assigned to our class.”

Akari looked relieved.

“Could I borrow them for a few hours? We need to put the menu boards near the entrance.”

“Ask Emi,” Itsuki said. “She's handling the schedule.”

“Right. Thank you.”

Emi came over and confirmed that the stands were free. Akari thanked her, then asked two students to help carry them.

Before leaving, she glanced at the class display.

“These photographs look nice.”

Ayane smiled.

“Thanks. They're from around Hoshimiya.”

“I like the river one.”

“That's my favourite too.”

Akari leaned closer to look at the caption.

“It's strange how familiar places look different in photographs.”

Ayane nodded.

“Sometimes you notice things you usually walk past.”

Akari smiled.

“That's a good way to put it.”

She left with the sign stands.

Riko watched her go.

“She's nice.”

“You said that already,” Itsuki replied.

“I can say it twice.”

“You can say anything twice.”

“That's the spirit.”

The final preparations continued into the evening. The class tested the lights, arranged the tables, and placed the display board near the entrance. Itsuki checked the extension cords and marked their locations on the schedule. Riko taped a paper lantern to the wall, stepped back, and decided it was crooked.

“It is crooked,” Itsuki said.

“I know.”

“Then fix it.”

“I'm evaluating.”

“From that angle?”

“Art requires perspective.”

He reached up and straightened it.

Riko looked offended.

“You've ruined the process.”

“The lantern is straight.”

“That's not the point.”

By the time they finished, the classroom looked different. The desks had been moved to the sides. The café counter stood near the back wall, and the local photographs formed a line along the entrance. The game corner occupied the far side, with a ring toss and a small table of prizes.

The cardboard mascot had not returned.

Riko had placed a tiny paper version beside the prize table.

Itsuki decided not to comment.

At the end of the day, the teacher in charge reminded them to arrive early the next morning. The festival would open at ten. Students were to wear their class shirts, keep the corridors clear, and report any problems to the committee.

“Get enough sleep,” she said. “Tomorrow will be busy.”

Riko raised a hand.

“What if we get too much sleep and miss it?”

“Then I will call your parents.”

Riko lowered her hand.

“Understood.”

Itsuki packed his bag.

Outside, the sky had turned a deep blue. The air smelled of warm pavement and the faint sweetness of the trees along the road. Students were leaving in groups, talking about their plans for the next day.

Riko walked beside him to the gate.

“Are you excited?”

“About what?”

“The festival.”

“It's a school event.”

“That's not an answer.”

“It's the answer I have.”

She smiled.

“You really don't know how to enjoy anything in advance.”

“I'll enjoy it when it's over.”

“That's a terrible attitude.”

“It's efficient.”

Riko shook her head.

At the gate, they separated. She headed toward the bus stop, and Itsuki walked to the station.

His phone buzzed.

A message from Nanami.

*Are you going to the festival tomorrow?*

He typed back.

*I have to help with the class booth.*

*Then I'll come.*

*You have your own school.*

*It's Saturday.*

*You still have things to do.*

*I can do them later.*

He considered replying, then put the phone away.

A second message arrived.

*I'll bring Yui if she's free.*

Itsuki looked at the screen.

*Don't make me responsible for your friends.*

*You're already responsible for me.*

He sighed.

That was not a legally recognised arrangement.

At home, he set his alarm for the next morning and placed his festival shirt over the back of a chair. He checked the schedule one last time, then left it on the table.

The day ahead was likely to be loud.

There would be visitors, questions, misplaced supplies, and Riko.

He turned off the light.

For once, he hoped the weather would stay clear.

# Chapter Eleven — The Morning Everyone Was Busy

The festival opened at ten.

At 9:42, the class had already misplaced the price list.

At 9:45, Riko found it beneath the prize table.

At 9:47, Emi discovered that the printed menu had the wrong price for tea.

At 9:50, Itsuki was asked to find another roll of tape.

He found two.

At 9:53, the teacher in charge inspected the room and reminded them that the emergency exit must remain clear.

At 9:56, Riko moved the cardboard mascot three centimetres to the left.

At 9:58, Itsuki moved it back.

“Why?” she demanded.

“Because it was blocking the sign.”

“It's a mascot. It needs visibility.”

“The sign needs visibility.”

“It's a very competitive world.”

At 10:00, the doors opened.

Students and visitors began moving through the school. The first wave was mostly parents and younger siblings, followed by groups of students from nearby schools. The corridors filled with voices, footsteps, and the occasional announcement from the gymnasium.

Itsuki took his place near the counter.

His assigned task was to keep track of the supply box and answer questions about the display. He was not required to sell food, which suited him. Riko had volunteered for the counter because she liked talking to people. Emi managed the cash box. Ayane guided visitors through the photographs and explained where each image had been taken.

For the first half hour, everything worked.

The café served tea, juice, and a small selection of packaged snacks. The game corner attracted more people than expected, mostly because Riko had decided that the ring toss needed a dramatic commentary.

“Three attempts,” she told a first-year student. “One chance to become a legend.”

“It's a ring toss,” Itsuki said.

“Don't undermine the legend.”

The student laughed and tried again.

A little after eleven, Itsuki saw Nanami near the entrance.

She was accompanied by a girl with short hair and a bright yellow tote bag. Nanami wore a light blouse and a dark skirt, her warm-brown hair falling neatly over her shoulders. She looked around the room, found Itsuki, and raised a hand.

He went to meet her.

“You came.”

“I said I would.”

“People say things.”

“People who don't keep their promises are unreliable.”

The girl beside her bowed slightly.

“Hello. I'm Yui.”

“Itsuki.”

“Nanami's told me about you.”

Itsuki looked at his sister.

Nanami smiled with suspicious innocence.

“Only good things.”

“That's unlikely.”

Yui laughed.

Nanami glanced around the room.

“This is your class?”

“Yeah.”

“It's nice.”

“Thanks.”

“Where's the food?”

“Counter.”

“Then we're going there.”

She walked off before he could answer.

Yui gave him an apologetic smile and followed.

At the counter, Riko greeted them with the enthusiasm of someone who had been waiting for new victims.

“Welcome! What can I get you?”

“Tea,” Nanami said.

“Two,” Yui added.

Riko looked at Itsuki.

“Your sister?”

“Unfortunately.”

Nanami accepted the cups.

“I'm his favourite person.”

“That's not what he said.”

“He doesn't say things.”

Riko laughed.

“Then I'll believe you.”

Itsuki returned to the display.

Nanami and Yui spent some time looking at the photographs. Ayane explained the river path and the old station entrance. Yui seemed especially interested in the picture of the shopping street during a summer event.

“Is this the street near the east station?” she asked.

“I go there sometimes. There's a bakery I like.”

“The one with the strawberry cakes.”

“That's his favourite.”

Itsuki looked at her.

“Why are you telling people that?”

“Because it's true.”

Yui smiled. “I'll remember that.”

“Please don't,” Itsuki said.

The girls moved toward the game corner.

For a while, the day was exactly what it was supposed to be: noisy, slightly disorganised, and full of small tasks. Itsuki restocked cups, answered questions, and helped a parent find the nearest restroom. Riko made a child laugh by pretending the ring toss had rejected his first attempt on artistic grounds. Ayane kept the display moving, making sure visitors did not crowd the entrance.

Akari's class was on the same floor.

Itsuki saw her several times through the open doorway of 3-A. She moved between the counter and the entrance, greeting visitors and helping her classmates. Her class had chosen a café theme as well, but theirs had a more elaborate menu and a set of decorative panels. Akari seemed comfortable in the middle of it all.

At one point, she passed the 3-B doorway with a stack of paper cups.

“Busy?” she asked.

“Not too bad,” Itsuki said.

“That's good. We're already out of the small plates.”

“Good luck.”

She smiled and continued down the corridor.

“Your class is popular,” Nanami said.

“It's a café.”

“That's not an answer.”

“It's the answer I have.”

“Your sister's funny.”

“She's aware.”

Nanami took another cup of tea.

“Are you coming to see our school activity next month?”

“Maybe.”

“That's not a yes.”

“It's a realistic answer.”

She narrowed her eyes.

“I'm going to tell Dad you refused.”

“He'll ask whether I have time.”

“And what will you say?”

“That I'll see.”

Nanami sighed.

“Both of you are impossible.”

Yui laughed into her tea.

They left after another few minutes. Nanami promised to send a message when they reached the station.

Itsuki returned to his task.

The afternoon grew busier. More visitors arrived, and the corridor outside the classrooms became crowded. The festival committee announced that the gym performance would begin in twenty minutes. Several classes started moving equipment at the same time.

Emi asked Itsuki to take a spare extension cord to the committee room near the old home-economics area.

“Can you bring this to the storage desk?” she said. “They've run out.”

“Sure.”

“Don't use the main stairs. They're busy.”

He took the cord and left.

The corridor near the storage rooms was quieter, though not empty. Students passed carrying signs, folded chairs, and boxes of supplies. A teacher stood near the corner, directing traffic toward the gym.

Itsuki delivered the extension cord, signed the equipment sheet, and turned back.

He had been away for less than ten minutes.

The noise from the main hall grew louder.

He adjusted his glasses and headed toward the stairs.

He did not know that, a few metres away, Akari Fujisaki had just discovered that her class's replacement display board was missing.

# Chapter Twelve — The Corridor Between Crowds

Akari had not planned to leave her class.

She had planned to take the replacement menu board to the entrance, check that the sign stand was stable, and return before the next group of visitors arrived.

The plan had been simple.

Then the stand's lower support slipped.

The board tilted, struck the edge of a nearby table, and slid sideways. Akari caught it before it fell, but the movement sent a stack of printed menus onto the floor.

“Are you okay?” one of her classmates asked.

“I'm fine. The board's fine.”

The board was not fine. One corner had bent slightly, and the printed menu had come loose from its backing.

Akari looked toward the entrance.

The class had run out of spare stands. The committee room had another one, but the route was crowded. She asked a classmate to keep the counter running and carried the board toward the service corridor.

She did not want to ask the teacher for help. The problem was small, and the teacher was already dealing with a sound-system issue in the gym.

The corridor narrowed near the storage area.

A group of students carrying folding chairs came through from the opposite direction. Akari stepped aside to let them pass. Someone behind her tried to move around a trolley, and the crowd compressed.

The board caught against the wall.

“Sorry,” she said, turning sideways.

A student brushed past her shoulder. She shifted her grip, trying to keep the board from hitting anyone.

The trolley moved again.

One of its wheels caught against a raised strip of flooring. The person pushing it stopped suddenly. The students behind him did not.

Akari's heel slipped.

She caught the edge of the wall with her free hand. The board tilted, and a stack of menu sheets began sliding from beneath her arm.

A hand caught the top corner.

“Careful.”

The voice was calm.

Akari looked up.

Kurose Itsuki.

She recognised him from the other class. The quiet boy who had brought the updated schedule to the committee office. He was holding the board steady with one hand and gathering the loose sheets with the other.

“Thanks,” she said.

“Are you hurt?”

“No. I just lost my footing.”

The crowd moved around them.

Itsuki looked toward the service corridor.

“This way's blocked.”

“I noticed.”

“There should be another route through the side hall.”

“I was trying to get there.”

He nodded, as if that explained everything.

The board was awkward to carry alone. Akari adjusted her grip, but the bent corner caught against the wall again.

“I can take it,” Itsuki said.

“It's okay. I can manage.”

“You're holding the papers and the board.”

“I'm aware.”

He looked at the stack of menus, then at the corridor.

“Which one do you want to drop?”

Akari stared at him.

Then she laughed once, despite herself.

“The papers.”

“Good choice.”

He took the board.

It was wider than it looked. Itsuki turned it carefully, keeping the bent corner away from the wall. Akari gathered the loose sheets and followed him toward the side hall.

The corridor opened near the old music room. The crowd noise softened. A window stood open at the far end, letting in a warm breeze and the distant sound of the gym announcement.

Akari stopped to straighten the papers.

“Sorry,” she said. “I didn't mean to make you carry everything.”

“It's fine.”

“You were going somewhere.”

“Back to my class.”

“Then I interrupted you.”

“I was already walking.”

She looked at him, unsure whether he was being polite or simply stating a fact.

His expression gave her no help.

“Thanks,” she said again.

He nodded.

Akari checked the board. The corner had not bent further. The printed menu could be fixed with tape, and the stand could be replaced.

“Could you hold this for a second?” she asked, offering him the papers.

He took them.

She crouched to retrieve a small roll of tape that had fallen from her bag. When she stood, the hallway seemed to tilt slightly—not from injury, but from the sudden rush of moving through the crowd and then stopping.

Itsuki noticed.

“Take a moment.”

“I'm fine.”

“I didn't say you weren't.”

She looked at him.

He was not smiling. He was not making a joke. He simply stood there, holding the papers, waiting for her to finish.

Akari let out a breath.

“Sorry. It's been a busy morning.”

“Looks like it.”

She adjusted the strap of her bag.

“I'm Akari Fujisaki, by the way.”

“I know.”

“Right. Everyone knows everyone.”

“Not everyone.”

She smiled faintly.

“You said that before.”

“I did?”

“At the committee office? Maybe not. I don't know.”

Itsuki glanced at the board.

“Should we get this to the stand?”

“Yes. Thank you.”

They walked toward the service entrance.

At the far end of the corridor, two students from another class came around the corner. One was carrying a tray of paper cups. The other held a camera, apparently taking pictures for the school festival page.

They saw Akari and Itsuki approaching together.

The student with the tray slowed.

“Fujisaki?”

Akari turned.

“Oh—hi.”

The student looked at the board in Itsuki's hands, then at the papers Akari was carrying.

“Are you moving the display?”

“Just fixing the stand.”

“Okay.”

The camera lifted briefly.

Itsuki did not notice.

Akari did.

She lowered her eyes and continued walking.

The side entrance opened into a small covered passage between buildings. The replacement stand was stored against the wall. Itsuki set the board down while Akari checked the support.

“That's better,” she said.

“Good.”

She taped the loose menu sheets back into place.

“I'm sorry I dragged you into this.”

“You didn't.”

“You were helping.”

“That's usually how it works.”

Akari looked up.

Then she laughed.

It was not a loud laugh, but it was genuine.

“Fair enough.”

She picked up the board.

Itsuki handed over the papers.

“Do you need help carrying it back?”

“No. I can manage now.”

He nodded.

“Then I'll go.”

“Wait.”

He stopped.

Akari looked toward the main corridor, where the crowd was still moving.

“Could you keep this quiet?”

Itsuki glanced at her.

“Keep what quiet?”

“The whole thing. I don't want people making a story out of a broken sign and a crowded hallway.”

He considered that.

“Why would they?”

Akari gave him a look.

“You'd be surprised.”

“I suppose.”

“It's not a big deal. I just don't want it becoming one.”

“Okay.”

She seemed relieved.

“Thank you.”

Itsuki adjusted the strap of his bag.

“Good luck with the festival.”

“You too.”

He left through the side corridor.

Akari watched him go for a moment, then turned back toward her class.

The incident had taken less than ten minutes.

It should have been forgotten by the time the festival ended.

But the two students with the camera had already started comparing what they had seen.

One remembered Akari standing close to a boy from another class.

The other remembered the board in his hands.

Neither had seen the crowd, the slipping trolley, or the loose papers.

And the photograph, taken just as they stepped through the corridor, showed only the two of them walking side by side.

It was not a lie.

It was simply not the truth.

# Chapter Thirteen — Everyone Saw Something

The first version of the story began before the festival ended.

It started near the side entrance, where two students were comparing photographs on a phone.

“Isn't that Fujisaki?”

“Yeah.”

“Who's the guy?”

“I don't know. Maybe from 3-B?”

The photograph showed Akari walking beside a boy with dark hair and glasses. He was carrying a large display board. She was holding a stack of papers. They were close enough that the corridor seemed narrow around them.

The image had been taken at the exact moment the two stepped out of the service passage.

It did not show what had happened before.

One student zoomed in.

“Looks like they're together.”

“They're just carrying stuff.”

“Maybe.”

The phone changed hands.

By the time the two students reached the gym, the photograph had been shown to three more people.

None of them had seen the trolley.

None of them had seen Akari lose her footing.

None of them knew that the board belonged to a café display.

They had seen a photograph.

That was enough to begin.

***

Itsuki did not hear about it until the following morning.

He arrived at school at his usual time. The weather had cooled overnight, and the air carried the smell of wet pavement. The festival banners still hung along the entrance, though a few had begun to curl at the edges.

He changed his shoes and walked toward the stairs.

Two students near the notice board stopped talking when he passed.

He noticed.

He did not care.

At least, that was what he told himself.

In Class 3-B, Riko was already seated. She had a paper cup of tea and a packet of biscuits on her desk.

“Morning,” Itsuki said.

“Good morning.”

She sounded unusually polite.

He stopped.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“You're smiling.”

“Am I not allowed?”

“You're usually smiling when you're about to say something.”

Riko leaned back.

“Did you have a good festival?”

“It was fine.”

“Anything interesting happen?”

“No.”

She tilted her head.

“Nothing at all?”

“I carried a board.”

Riko's eyes brightened.

Itsuki looked at her.

“Why?”

She took a biscuit.

“Just asking.”

He sat down.

A few minutes later, Miyake came over.

“Kurose.”

“Yeah?”

“Were you with Fujisaki yesterday?”

Itsuki looked up from his notebook.

“For a few minutes.”

Miyake grinned.

“Really?”

“I helped her carry a display board.”

“That's what I heard.”

“Then why are you asking?”

“People are talking.”

“About a board?”

Miyake laughed.

“Not exactly.”

Riko made a small sound beside him.

Itsuki turned.

She was looking at the window.

“What's funny?” he asked.

“Nothing.”

Miyake pulled out his phone.

“Someone took a picture.”

He showed it to Itsuki.

The photograph was familiar. It showed the side passage, Akari beside him, and the display board in his hands. The angle made the corridor look narrower than it was. Akari's head was turned toward him. Itsuki's face was partly hidden by the board.

It looked like a moment that belonged to a different conversation.

“That's the picture?” Itsuki asked.

“Yeah.”

“It's a picture of us carrying something.”

Miyake shrugged.

“People are saying you two are dating.”

Itsuki stared at him.

Then he looked at the picture again.

“No.”

Miyake laughed.

“That's your answer?”

“It's the correct one.”

“Maybe you should tell them.”

“Who?”

“Everyone.”

Itsuki returned the phone.

“Everyone can read the picture.”

Miyake looked unconvinced.

Riko finally turned toward them.

“Maybe they can't.”

Itsuki looked at her.

She smiled.

He decided not to ask.

***

Akari heard about the rumour during the first break.

A girl from her class approached her with a phone in hand.

“Akari, is this true?”

Akari looked at the screen.

The photograph.

Her stomach tightened—not because of the boy in it, but because she recognised exactly how the image would look to someone who had not been there.

“It's not what it looks like,” she said.

The girl smiled.

“So there is something to explain?”

“No. I mean, there is an explanation. He helped me carry a display board.”

“Who is he?”

“Kurose. From 3-B.”

“Are you close?”

“No.”

The answer came too quickly.

Her friend raised an eyebrow.

Akari realised how it sounded.

“We're not dating,” she said. “We barely know each other.”

“Okay, okay.”

The girl lifted both hands.

“I was just asking.”

Akari smiled, but the conversation had already begun to feel like a room with too many people in it.

She spent the rest of the break answering similar questions.

Some were harmless. Some were teasing. One student asked whether the two had been meeting secretly before the festival.

Akari said no.

The student asked why she looked so serious.

Akari did not know how to answer that without making it worse.

***

The rumour changed shape during lunch.

In one class, someone said Akari had been seen leaving the service corridor with a boy.

In another, someone said the boy had carried her belongings.

A third version claimed that the photograph had been taken after the two had argued.

By the end of the day, a student from the first-year class had heard that they had been dating since the previous term.

No one could explain where that version had come from.

Itsuki heard three versions before the final bell.

The paper cat had been placed beside a picture book, wearing its tiny school cap. The river and park were drawn in blue and green. The display was neat, colourful, and slightly crowded.

*Finished,* she wrote.

Itsuki replied:

*Looks good.*

*Only good?*

*Very good.*

*Better.*

A second message arrived.

*Yui says the cat needs a name.*

*Cat.*

*We already discussed this.*

*Then the problem is solved.*

*You're impossible.*

He put the phone down.

A few minutes later, it rang.

Nanami.

“Hello?”

“Are you busy?”

“No.”

“Good. I wanted to tell you that Dad said we can borrow the old picture books until the end of term.”

“He helped?”

“He did. He also said you should come see the display.”

“I'll see.”

“That's not a yes.”

“It's not a no.”

Nanami sighed.

“Fine. I'll send you the date.”

“Okay.”

There was a pause.

Then she said, “Are you doing anything for the start of summer break?”

“Not yet.”

“Good. You can help me with the book return.”

“That's not a holiday plan.”

“It is now.”

He looked toward the calendar on the refrigerator.

The last week of June was almost over.

July was waiting on the other side.

“I'll think about it,” he said.

Nanami laughed.

“That's all I wanted.”

The call ended.

Itsuki returned to the kitchen and filled the kettle.

He had not made plans for summer.

He had not decided what he would do after graduation.

He had not found a way to stop people from talking about a photograph.

But there were things he could do tomorrow.

He could go to school.

He could help Nanami return the books.

He could have lunch with Ayane again.

He could answer Riko when she asked a question, even if the question was usually unreasonable.

And if Mio asked him something about the festival, he could answer that too.

It was not a plan for the future.

It was a start.

# Epilogue — The First Version

Mio waited until Itsuki had left before opening her notebook again.

He had not told her everything.

He had explained the crowd bottleneck, the fallen papers, and the display board. He had described the side corridor and the replacement stand. He had not seemed interested in the photograph itself, except to say that it showed only the last part of the incident.

That was useful.

It meant the photograph was not the beginning of the story.

Mio wrote down the sequence as he had described it.

A practical problem in Akari's class.

A damaged display stand.

A crowded corridor.

A minor loss of balance.

A student from another class helping carry the board.

A request to keep the incident quiet.

She drew a line beneath the notes.

The details were ordinary. That was what made the rumour strange. The story had grown larger without gaining anything reliable.

She turned to a clean page.

There were still questions.

Who had taken the photograph?

Who had seen the first part of the incident?

Why had the accounts changed?

And why had Akari been so quick to deny something that, according to Itsuki, had never been anything more than an awkward moment?

Mio tapped the pencil against the paper.

She could have asked Akari directly.

She probably should have.

But she wanted to understand the order in which the story had changed. If she asked too broadly, people would simply repeat what they had heard.

She needed the first version.

Outside, the last students were leaving the school. The light beyond the windows had turned soft and gold.

Mio closed her notebook.

The rumour itself was not important.

At least, that was what she told herself.

She was curious about the people inside it.

And there was one person she had not yet understood.

Kurose Itsuki.

For the first time, she wrote his name at the top of a new page.

**Investigation — Festival Rumour.**

Then, beneath it:

**Start with what actually happened.**
