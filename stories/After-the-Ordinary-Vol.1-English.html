Prologue — The Last Day of Nothing

The rain started at four in the afternoon, which was typical of April first. Itsuki Kurose stood in the chilled aisle of the Hoshimart by the station, holding a plastic tub of pudding and thinking about how much of his life consisted of standing in brightly lit rooms waiting for something small to be finished. The pudding was the expensive kind. Custard, caramel, a paper band around the lid with a cow on it and some copy about Hokkaido. He had bought the same one last year, and the year before that, and at some point it had stopped being a choice and become a ritual, which was a comfortable thing for it to become. His phone buzzed against his hip. **Nanami:** *im at the station. its raining. bring umbrella??* **Itsuki:** *You're at the station and you don't have an umbrella.* **Nanami:** *i have a bag* **Itsuki:** *That's not an umbrella.* **Nanami:** *bring one or ill get wet and die* He paid for the pudding and added a second one, the cheap kind, for himself, because two tubs looked less like a ritual and more like groceries. --- She was waiting under the station's concrete overhang with her school bag held over her head like a small, ineffective roof, her long brown hair already dark at the ends. When she saw him she lowered the bag and grinned with the total confidence of someone who had never once been left to drown. "You came." "You made it sound like a flood." "It *is* a flood. Look at it." She shook her sleeve out and sprayed him. "That's a flood." Itsuki opened the umbrella and held it over her, which required standing close enough that she could immediately duck under his arm and take the handle from him, which she did. "You're late," she said. "I've been standing here for *nine minutes*." "You texted me six minutes ago." "Nine minutes of my life, gone." They walked. The rain hammered the vinyl above them and the streetlights smeared orange down the wet pavement, and Nanami talked the whole way about a girl in her class called Kaede who had apparently bought the same sneakers as another girl and was now involved in what sounded like a low-grade diplomatic crisis. Itsuki made noises at the appropriate intervals. Nanami had never needed more than that. She filled silences the way other people filled forms — thoroughly, and with commentary. At the door of his building she collapsed the umbrella and shook it out with a snap that got most of the water on his trousers. "Sixteen," he said. "Sixteen," she agreed, and then, because she could not let a straight line stand, "Which means I'm basically an adult. I could get a mortgage." "You could get a part-time job." "One step at a time." The apartment was on the third floor. Two rooms, a kitchen unit along one wall, a bathroom with a mirror he had never bothered to defog properly, and a narrow balcony with a view of the next building's pipes. The lease was in his mother's name. The rent came out of her account on the twenty-seventh of every month, and Itsuki had learned the exact texture of the silence that followed if he forgot to send a one-line message saying it had arrived. He hung his blazer on the back of the chair, put the umbrella in the stand, and put the two puddings in the fridge. Nanami kicked her shoes off in the genkan, dropped her bag, and went straight to the cupboard where he kept the rice. "Don't cook," he said. "I'm not cooking. I'm inspecting." "Of what?" "Whether you're eating like a person." She pulled out a bag of rice, weighed it in her hand, and put it back with the satisfaction of an auditor. "You are. Barely." "You came all the way here in the rain to audit my rice." "I came all the way here because Dad has Akemi over." Akemi was the girlfriend. Nanami had nothing against her — she said so unprompted, more than once, which was how Itsuki knew the situation had edges. "Again?" "Third time this month." She pulled out a chair and sat down backwards, arms folded on the backrest, chin on her arms. "It's fine. She made curry. It was good curry. She's *nice*." "Nice curry." "Shut up." But she was smiling. "It's just — they laugh at things that aren't funny. Both of them. Together. In the living room. It's like being in a car with someone else's radio on." Itsuki filled the kettle. "Stay tonight," he said. "I was going to anyway. You don't have a futon, though." "You do." She had left one in his closet two years ago and never taken it back. It was folded on the top shelf behind the winter blankets, in the spot that had quietly stopped being storage and started being hers. --- They ate rice and fried eggs with the rain on the window and the television on low, and at ten to eleven Itsuki took the expensive pudding out of the fridge and stuck a match in it. Nanami's eyes went round. Then narrow. "That's not a candle." "It's a match." "It's *going out*." "Blow it out, then." She blew it out. The smoke curled. She looked at the pudding, and then at him, and her face did something complicated — pleased and embarrassed at once, the way she got when she was reminded of something she had decided not to expect. "Happy birthday," Itsuki said. "Sixteen," she said again, quieter. "Sixteen." She ate the whole thing. Then she said the caramel was too sweet and made him eat the last third, and then she did the washing up before he could, standing at the sink with her sleeves pushed to her elbows and her hair tied back in a knot, humming something tuneless. At 11:58 his phone lit up. **Nanami:** *happy birthday in advance old man* She was in the same room. Itsuki looked up. She had her head down, thumbs still on the screen, ears pink. He typed back: *You're two metres away.* **Nanami:** *im shy* --- At some point in the night the rain stopped, and the apartment got very quiet in the way it only did when there were two people in it and one of them was asleep. Itsuki lay on his back with his glasses off, staring at the ceiling, and did the arithmetic he did every April. Eighteen. Third year. One more year of classrooms and corridors and bells, and then the part of his life that nobody had described to him yet. He didn't have a plan. He had a routine, which he suspected was not the same thing, but which was at least the right shape — up at six-forty, breakfast, the 7:22, shoes in the locker, sit near the window, answer when called, go home, cook, sleep. A plan would have required a future, and the future had a way of turning into a list of things other people wanted to talk to him about. His last year of high school was going to be quiet. He had decided that the way other people decided to lose weight. From the other room, Nanami coughed in her sleep, muttered something that sounded like a complaint, and rolled over. Itsuki put his glasses back on and looked at the ceiling a while longer. ---

Chapter One A Perfectly Ordinary Start

The first day of the school year was bright and cold, because Japan liked to make a point. Itsuki got to the classroom eight minutes before the bell, which was the correct number of minutes: late enough that the room was already full and nobody would try to talk to him about it, early enough that he wasn't the person everyone watched walk in. Seat thirty-one. Second row from the back, third from the window. Last year he had been by the window, which had been ideal, and this year he was one seat further in, which meant he had to turn his head slightly to look outside and everyone to his left could see him do it. Small loss. Bearable. Third year, class 3-B. He pushed his glasses up and looked at the blackboard, where somebody had already written a joke about the homeroom teacher in chalk, and somebody else had already written *wrong* under it. "Kurose." The voice arrived with a body attached, swinging into the desk beside him with enough force to make the whole row shudder. Riko Tachibana had cut her hair over the break — shorter than before, cropped at the jaw, caramel-brown and sticking up in places it clearly hadn't been told about — and she was grinning at him with her chin propped on her fist like a magazine interviewer. "Third year," she said. "Same class. *Again.*" "Somebody at the top hates one of us." "Somebody at the top loves *me*." She flicked a piece of chalk dust off her sleeve. "You look taller." "I'm the same height." "See, this is why you're fun. You check." She spun a pen across her knuckles and dropped it. "So what did you do over the break? Reorganise your spice rack? Alphabetise your socks?" "Mostly stood in a convenience store." "God, don't tell me the good stuff all at once." The bell went. Riko straightened up for approximately four seconds, until Mr. Kudo came in and told everyone to sit down, and then she slouched with the specific slouch of a girl who had decided that her posture was a private matter. Kudo was in his fifties, built like a door, and had the flat delivery of a man who had run this exact homeroom thirty-one times. He read the class list aloud. When he got to Tachibana, Riko raised her hand and said "present," and Kudo said, without looking up, "We'll see." Itsuki's name came near the end. He said present. Kudo moved on. Afterwards there were notices and a timetable and a short speech about how third year was the year it counted, and then the morning was over and the corridors filled with the sound of six hundred people relocating at once. --- The shoe lockers were the usual crush. Itsuki changed into his indoor shoes and stood aside to let the current carry everyone else, and while he stood there he saw Akari Fujisaki come down the entrance hall. You saw her before you saw her, in a way. Not because of anything she did — she wasn't loud, and she didn't sweep through the crowd expecting it to part. She came down the middle of the hall with two friends, one on each side, saying something that made the shorter one laugh into her hand. Her hair was long and dark reddish-brown, the colour of old wood, and she had it tied half-up with a ribbon the exact shade of the school's trim. Someone said her name. She turned, and found the someone, and smiled, and asked a question, and listened to the answer. She did it in the time it took Itsuki to get his shoe locker shut. Then she was past him, up the stairs, and gone. That was the entire extent of their relationship. He knew her name. She had, on two occasions, said hello to him in corridors — once because she said hello to everyone in a five-metre radius, and once because he had been standing in front of the noticeboard and she needed to read it. He had thought, at the time, *that was friendly*, and then had gone to maths, and that was that. --- Third period was elective English Reading, which meant a mixed class and a walk to the far building, and Riko, who had chosen it because her parents had chosen it for her, walked beside him complaining the entire way. "I don't even like English." "You like the teacher." "Sakaguchi's all right. He's got that sad divorced thing going on." She kicked a bottle cap along the corridor. "What's the point? I'm not going to *England*." "You might." "Don't threaten me." The classroom had single desks and a window that faced the incinerator. Itsuki took a seat at the back. Riko took the one in front and turned hers around entirely, which Sakaguchi had given up objecting to sometime last November. The lesson was a passage about a man who built boats. Sakaguchi read a paragraph, asked a question, got silence, and then looked directly at Riko with the expression of a man choosing a volunteer. "Tachibana. Paragraph three. What does 'he had no ear for it' mean?" Riko's eyes went wide and then slid down and to the left, which was the signal. Itsuki wrote on the corner of his page: *He was bad at it. It's about music.* She read it upside down. Her shoulders relaxed a millimetre. "He was bad at it," she said. "Like, the music thing. He couldn't hear it properly." Sakaguchi nodded slowly, in the manner of a man who suspected a crime but could not prove one. "Correct. And why does the writer use an ear and not, say, a hand?" Silence. "Kurose." Itsuki looked up. "You wrote that down very fast for someone who wasn't asked." "Reflex." "Then answer." He turned his pen over once. "Because it's not about skill," he said. "If it were hands, it'd be about practice. Ears make it something you either have or don't. It's harsher." Sakaguchi looked at him for a second longer than was comfortable, then wrote *SENSORY METAPHOR — JUDGEMENT* on the board and moved on. "Show-off," Riko muttered, without turning around. "You asked." "I *looked*. There's a difference." --- When the bell went, the corridor outside filled up again, and Itsuki was putting his textbook away when a voice near the door said, "—so it's not about practice." He looked up. Akari Fujisaki was standing in the doorway with Serizawa from 3-D, who was holding a notebook and looking bored. "It's *not*," Serizawa said. "You were in that class, you heard him." "I heard him say it's about whether you have it or not, and then he said it was harsher." Akari's mouth was pulled to one side. "Which is true, but it's not the answer to *why the writer chose it*." Serizawa noticed Itsuki looking and nudged her. Akari glanced over. Her expression did the small recalibration of a person who had been discussing someone in front of them. "Ah — Kurose-kun." She said it easily. "You were right, by the way. About the ear thing. I just think the answer's about the writer being a snob." Then she was gone down the corridor, and Serizawa went with her, and Riko appeared at Itsuki's elbow and said, "Well. Look at *you*." "It's a grammar question." "It's Fujisaki Akari." --- He got home at five-forty, cooked rice and a pan of vegetables, ate standing up because sitting down alone felt like an event, and washed the pan. At seven the doorbell went. Nanami was on the step with her blazer over her arm and her tie loose, and she said, "Happy birthday, you're ancient," and pushed past him into the apartment before he could answer, and put a paper bag on the table. Inside was a slice of cake from the shop by the station, and a card, and on the card, in her round handwriting: *CONGRATULATIONS ON BEING 18. PLEASE LEARN TO USE A BANK APP.* Itsuki looked at it for a while. "What," she said, from the fridge. "Nothing." "You're doing the face." "I don't have a face." "You have the one where you're pleased and you won't say it." She came over and took the cake out of the bag herself. "Eat it before it gets warm. It's strawberry. I made them give me the one with the most strawberries." He sat down and ate it, and she sat across from him with her chin in her hand and watched him do it, and neither of them said anything for a while, which was, in this apartment, a form of conversation. ---

Chapter Two The Physics of Being Overlooked

There was a system to being overlooked, and Itsuki had refined it over two years. Sit near a wall. Answer questions in the fewest words that were still correct. Never be first to arrive or last to leave. Laugh at jokes aimed at the room rather than at a person, because a person who laughs at a joke aimed at someone else has to be looked at. Volunteer for the jobs nobody wanted, because the jobs nobody wanted came with no audience. By the second week of April he had settled into it. Third year had a smell to it — new paper, floor polish, and the faintly metallic pressure of people who had started saying *last year* about things that had happened eight months ago. Riko, of course, treated the system as a challenge. "Come to the conbini," she said on Thursday, appearing at his desk with her bag already on her shoulder. "I need a slushie." "You need one?" "I need one in the way that you need water." "That's a need." "See, you get it." The convenience store was two minutes from the school gate and permanently full of students, and Riko went through it like weather, knocking things off shelves with her bag and putting them back slightly wrong. She got a blue slushie. Itsuki got a coffee in a can and stood outside with it while she drank hers on the low wall and told him about a fight she'd had with her mother over the break about her mock-exam results. "She said the word 'potential,'" Riko said. "Twice. Like I'm a *building plot*." "Were you listening?" "I was in the room." Itsuki drank his coffee. "Say something clever," she said. "Start smaller. Pick one subject and get above fifty." "Ugh." She sucked the slushie. "That's so *sensible*. You're supposed to tell me my potential is a beautiful untapped thing." "Your potential is a beautiful untapped thing. Also, pick one subject." She laughed, which was the point, and then her phone rang and the laugh went away in a way that was almost visible. She looked at the screen. Her posture changed — spine straight, knees together, the slushie brought down to her lap. "Yeah. Hello." A pause. "No, I'm — I'm on my way home now. Yeah. I did it. Yeah." Another pause; her free hand picked at a seam on her skirt. "Okay. Bye." She hung up and let her back round over. "Home," she said. "Now?" "Apparently." They walked to the corner together and then split, and Itsuki watched her go up the hill in her tidy straight line, and thought that whoever had built Riko Tachibana had built two of her and put a hinge in the middle. --- The Fujimoto family lived four streets from the station, in a house with a persimmon tree in the front garden and a nameplate that had been replaced twice in ten years. Itsuki had been inside it more times than he could count, and he still rang the bell, because at some point it had stopped being his to walk into. Mrs. Fujimoto opened the door in an apron and said, "Itsuki-kun!" in the tone of a woman greeting a returning soldier, and took his coat before he had finished taking it off. "You're thin. Are you eating?" "Yes." "You're thin and you're lying." Ayane was in the living room, on the floor by the low table, folding napkins into a fan shape and not looking up. Her black hair was shoulder-length with the bangs swept to one side, and she was wearing a jumper the colour of weak tea, and when Itsuki came in she said, "Hi," and moved her knees so there was room at the table. "Hi." He sat. She kept folding. Mr. Fujimoto came out of the kitchen with a tray and said, "Kurose. Good. Sit up straight, both of you," and then talked for eleven minutes about a client at work who had sent a fax, which he considered a personal attack. Ayane's mother put down a dish of simmered pumpkin between them. Ayane reached for it at the same moment Itsuki did, and both of them stopped, and then both of them took some, and both of them said "go ahead," and it took four seconds for a plate of pumpkin to be served. That was the shape of it now. Four seconds of pumpkin. They had been five years old in this garden. They had dug a hole to Australia and been furious when it filled with rain. They had been eleven and walked to school together every day for three years, and she had talked the entire way, and he had listened the entire way, and neither of them had known that was unusual. Then middle school split their classes, and she got a phone that buzzed during dinner, and he got quieter, and each of them waited for the other to make the first move, and then waited long enough that making the first move became a *thing*, and then it was second year of high school and they were two people who said *hi* and *four seconds of pumpkin*. "Itsuki-kun," said Mrs. Fujimoto, "your mother called me last week." Ayane's head came up slightly. "She says you won't come for Obon." "I didn't say I wouldn't." "You said you'd see," said Mrs. Fujimoto, kindly, and put more rice in his bowl. "That's a no with extra steps." "It's a maybe." "It's a no with extra steps," said Ayane, without looking up. Itsuki glanced at her. She had a small smile, aimed at the napkin. The third husband's name was Kazuhiko. He was fifty-one and worked in procurement and had, on the two occasions Itsuki had met him, tried very hard. He had asked about his subjects and then about his hobbies and then, when the hobbies had run out, had suggested that Itsuki might like to come fishing. Itsuki did not like fishing. Itsuki had said so. Kazuhiko had said, "Then we'll find something you do like," which was a nice thing to say and had somehow made Itsuki want to leave the room. "It's one night," Mrs. Fujimoto said. "Two at most. Your mother's been asking." "I know." "I'm not nagging." "You're nagging beautifully," said Mr. Fujimoto. After dinner Ayane went out onto the veranda with a cup of tea, and Itsuki, after a moment in which he could have stayed where he was, followed. The night was mild. The persimmon tree was in leaf and the leaves moved when the wind came. She had her knees up and her tea balanced on them and she was looking at the fence. "Sorry about Mum," she said. "She's all right." "She's a menace." Ayane blew on her tea. "She thinks if she says your name enough times you'll get better." "Better at what?" "At whatever she's decided is wrong with you." Itsuki sat down on the edge of the veranda with his feet on the step. For a while there was nothing but the tree. It would have been easy to leave it — she had a way of leaving gaps that didn't demand filling, and he had a way of respecting them, and between the two habits they could sit like this for twenty minutes. "The festival's in June this year," Ayane said eventually. "They moved it up because of the gym roof work." "I heard." "Our class is doing a café." She turned her cup a quarter turn. "Nao's in charge, so it'll be a disaster." Serizawa. Itsuki had not known that Serizawa and Ayane were in the same class, and felt vaguely that this was information that should have reached him. "You'll be fine," he said. "See, you say that like a fact." "It's a café. You give people cake." "With Nao in charge." They both laughed, briefly, and then it stopped, and the gap came back, and this time neither of them could find the way into it. When he left, Mrs. Fujimoto pressed a container of leftovers on him at the door and Ayane stood behind her and said, "Bye. Get home safe," and he said, "Yeah," and the door shut. Halfway to the corner he realised he had not asked her a single question all evening. Not one. Not what she was doing, not how her classes were, not anything. He had answered and eaten and sat on a veranda. Four seconds of pumpkin, and he'd brought it on himself. ---

Chapter Three Committee of One

The gym roof had been half a scaffolding cage since March, and by the middle of May the sound of it had become the school's second bell. The festival had been moved to the last Saturday of June, which meant the whole of June was going to be preparation, which meant the whole of June was going to be a competition to see who could look busiest. Class 3-B, after ninety minutes of arguing, had settled on a paper-craft exhibit called *Hoshimiya, Remembered* — a room of paper buildings lit from inside, tracing the shopping street from the station to the park. It was a good idea. It was also the kind of idea that required an enormous amount of fiddly work by a small number of people, and by the third week the small number had quietly shrunk to eight. Itsuki had been assigned to structures, because he could measure. Riko had been assigned to advertising, because she could talk. She had made forty flyers with a marker pen and the word *WOW* on all of them and had been told to make them again. "You can't just write WOW." "You can if it's a good exhibit." "It's a room of paper houses." "*WOW,*" said Riko. On the Thursday she dragged him out to the shopping street to photograph the buildings they were meant to copy, and they got as far as the third one before she was recognised by a shopkeeper who knew her mother and had to spend six minutes being polite. "There's the arcade," Itsuki said, when they were free. "You could do that one from memory." "Memory," said Riko, "is exactly what got me into the redo." She took the picture anyway. Then she took eleven more, most of them crooked, one of them of her own thumb. They walked back along the covered section where the shutters were down and the overhead speakers played a song from nine years ago. Riko had her hands in her pockets and was quiet for longer than was normal. "Hey," she said. "Mm." "Do you think people are the same everywhere?" "Are you asking me something?" "I'm asking if you think —" She stopped. Restarted. "My cousin came over. She's at a private school in Nagoya, and she's *awful* there. Really. She's in the drama club and she wears lip gloss to class and she calls her teacher by his first name." "And?" "And she came here and was normal. She was funny and she helped with the dishes." Riko kicked at the base of a shutter. "So is she awful, or is that just — what she's like there?" Itsuki considered it. "You're asking if she's lying." "I'm asking which one's the real one." "I think," he said, "if she's funny at home and funny here and awful there, then she's funny, and she's also decided the awful thing works. That's still her. It's just not the part you like." Riko didn't answer for a while. "God, that's a sensible answer," she said at last. "You're no fun at all." But she said it quietly, and she walked closer to him for the rest of the way back. --- Akari Fujisaki was in 3-D, and 3-D was doing a café, and everyone knew about it because 3-D had put a sign on the stairwell in calligraphy. Itsuki's involvement with 3-D was limited to one thing: the equipment committee had borrowed 3-B's trolley, and it had not come back. "You go," said Hashimoto, who was class representative and had the permanent expression of a boy who had been elected by accident. "You're the least likely to start a thing." "Least likely to start a thing" was, Itsuki reflected, an accurate description of his life. The trolley was in 3-D's storeroom, under a pile of tablecloths, and getting it out required moving the tablecloths, and moving the tablecloths required someone to tell him where to put them, and the someone was Akari, who was in the middle of a conversation with three people and a clipboard. She saw him and came over without being asked. "Kurose-kun. Trolley?" "Yeah." "Under there." She pulled the tablecloths off herself, dumped them on a desk, and said, "Sorry. We took it and then everything got on top of it." "It's fine." "It's not fine, it's a trolley hostage situation." She crouched to help him work the wheels free. Up close, she had a smear of something white on her forearm and a pen behind her ear and she was visibly tired. "Are you doing structures for yours?" "Mm." "Lucky. I got rota, which means everyone hates me by Tuesday." "Rota's not so bad." "Rota's *entirely* bad. Two people have already asked to swap and one of them cried." She got the last wheel clear and stood up, and pushed her hair back with the back of her wrist. "There. Take it. Tell Hashimoto-san I'm sorry." "You can tell him." "He's scared of me." "He's scared of everyone." Akari laughed — a real one, short and surprised — and said, "That's true, actually," and then somebody called her name from the corridor and she was gone. Itsuki wheeled the trolley back up the stairs. Behind him, someone in 3-D said, "He's in 3-B," and someone else said, "So?" ---

Chapter Four The Night Nanami Cried

The rain came back in June, which was correct of it. It came on a Tuesday, and Nanami came with it, at half past nine, with no umbrella and a bag and the particular flatness around her eyes that Itsuki had learned to read before she said anything. "Hi," she said. "Hi." She came in, took her shoes off, and stood in the middle of the room with her arms at her sides. "I'm not staying," she said. "All right." "Dad's got Akemi over again and they're watching a film and it's the one with the — the one where the dog—" She stopped. "It's not about the dog." "Okay." "It's not." Her voice went thin. "It's just that they were on the sofa. Both of them. And there wasn't a — there wasn't a place. To sit. That's all. There wasn't a place to sit in my own house." Itsuki took the kettle down. "Nanami." "I'm *fine*." "You can be fine in the morning." She stood there another second, and then her face crumpled, and she crossed the room and put her forehead against his shoulder and cried. Itsuki put one hand on her back and the other on the back of her head, and held on, and said nothing. There was nothing to say. Every time he had tried to say something — *it's not your fault, it'll settle down, Akemi's all right* — it had come out sounding like a pamphlet. She cried for a while. He rubbed her back in slow circles and watched the rain on the window and the kettle steam and eventually click off. "I hate this," she said into his shirt. "Yeah." "I hate that I'm the one who has to be *gracious* about it. Like — he's allowed to be happy, and I'm supposed to be happy for him, and if I'm not then I'm a brat." "You're not a brat." "I was a brat on Sunday. I said Akemi's curry was salty." "Was it?" "It was *perfectly* salted." Nanami sniffed hard. "That's the worst part." Itsuki got her a tissue. She blew her nose and looked at the wet patch on his shoulder with professional interest. "Sorry about your shirt." "It's a shirt." "You've got five shirts, so." She sat down at the table and he put a cup of tea in front of her and sat down opposite, and she wrapped both hands round the cup and didn't drink it. "Can I stay," she said, without looking up. "Obviously." "I'll be out early." "You don't have to be." "I've got cleaning duty." She wiped her eye with the heel of her hand. "Don't tell Mum." "About what?" "About me coming here like this. She'll do the thing." The thing was their mother's particular brand of comfort, which was to call and be warm and ask twelve questions and then say *well, you know you're always welcome here*, in a way that made the offer sound like a verdict on somebody else's house. "I won't tell her," Itsuki said. They sat. Rain on glass. The refrigerator hummed and stopped. "Onii-chan," Nanami said, after a long time. "Mm." "Are you happy?" Nobody had asked him that in a while. He turned his cup a quarter turn, the way Ayane turned hers. "I'm all right," he said. "That's not the same." "No." She nodded slowly, as if he had confirmed something she'd suspected, and drank her tea, and at eleven she unrolled her futon in the other room and got into it and said, "Good night, old man," and Itsuki said, "Good night." He lay awake a while afterwards, listening to make sure she was asleep. ---

Chapter Five WOW

The last week before the festival had the texture of a held breath. The gym roof work stopped on the Wednesday. The scaffolding came down and left the building looking naked. Teachers patrolled the corridors with clipboards and said *walk* a lot. In 3-B the paper skyline grew along three tables, and Hashimoto developed a twitch, and Riko's flyers — the third batch — were finally approved by a margin of one vote. Itsuki cut struts and glued gussets and kept his head down. On Thursday he was sent to the equipment store behind the gym for a second trolley and a box of extension leads, and it took longer than it should have because the store had been reorganised by someone with a theory. When he came out with the box, Akari Fujisaki was standing at the foot of the outside stairwell, looking up at it. She had her hands full: a crate of paper cups stacked four high, her bag hanging off one wrist, and a coil of receipt paper under her arm. Her hair was coming out of its tie. "Kurose-kun." "Hi." "Don't say anything." She adjusted her grip on the crate. "I've been up and down these stairs four times and every time someone's put something at the top." "Want a hand?" "No, I've got a —" The top cup slid. She caught it with her chin. "I've got a system." Itsuki put the extension leads down. --- What happened next took about ninety seconds and would be described, over the following two weeks, in nine different ways by nine different people. The system failed at the third step. Akari's foot came down on a cup that had already made it to the ground, her ankle turned, and she went sideways into the handrail with a bang, and the crate went over. Itsuki got there in time to take her elbow and not in time to take the cups, which went down the stairs in a paper avalanche, bouncing and splitting and rolling into the drain grille. "Ow. *Ow.*" Akari hopped once, holding the rail. "Ow, that's — I'm fine. I'm fine." "You're not." "I'm *fine,*" she said, and then her face did something honest. "It hurts a bit." "Can you put weight on it?" She tried. Her mouth thinned. "Sort of." "Right." He looked at the cups, then at the stairwell. Behind the gym, the parade rehearsal had just started on the far side of the building, and the sound of a brass band tuning was coming round the corner in a wall. "You're not going up there. There's a crowd at the top of the stairs, you'll get knocked." "I have to get these to the café. We open at ten tomorrow, they'll—" "Then we go round." "Round where?" "Service corridor. Behind the gym, past the boiler room, comes out at the north exit by your building." Akari looked at him for a second, then at the stairwell, then down at the scattered cups. "Okay," she said. Itsuki gathered the cups into the crate — most were still usable, and he put the crushed ones aside without being asked. He picked up her bag, which had come off her wrist in the fall, and slung the strap over his own shoulder, because she was going to need both hands for the rail. The service corridor was concrete, dim, and smelled of drains and warm rubber. Their footsteps echoed. She limped along beside him with one hand on the wall, and the brass band receded, and after about twenty metres she started laughing. "I'm sorry," she said. "This is so stupid." "It's a corridor." "It's a *service* corridor. I've been at this school three years and I've never been down here." She looked up at the pipes. "It's like a submarine." "It's a corridor with a boiler." "You're not helping me romanticise it." At the north exit she stopped and took the crate from him and set it down, and then she stood there with her hand on the door and didn't push it. "Kurose-kun." "Mm." "You know how this works, right?" She said it plainly, without drama, which was somehow worse. "By Monday there'll be a story. If I go out there now, limping, with my bag on your shoulder, that's a story." Itsuki considered this. "It's a true story." "True stories are the worst kind. There's nothing to argue with." She shifted her weight and winced. "Can you — this is going to sound stupid. Can you not make it a thing? If anyone asks. Just be normal. Don't do a big explanation." "All right." "Really?" "Really." "Thank you." She looked relieved, briefly, and then she looked at her own ankle and the relief went. "I'm going to have to explain this to my mum." "Tell her you fell down the stairs." "I *did* fall down the stairs." "Then it's easy." She laughed again, once, and pushed the door open. Light came in. Somebody on the other side said, "*There* you are—" and Akari was gone into it, and Itsuki went back up the corridor the way he'd come, and the brass band was still playing. ---

Chapter Six Nine Versions

The first he heard of it was Monday morning, from Hashimoto, who said, "Kurose. Is it true?" "Is what true." "You and Fujisaki-san." Itsuki put his bag down. "Me and Fujisaki-san what." The whole row was listening. That was the first sign. "You know." Hashimoto made a vague hand gesture. "From the festival." Itsuki thought about it. "She fell over. I helped her round the back. It's not a thing." "That's not what people are saying." --- By second period it was a thing. By lunch it was a thing with a timeline. Someone in 3-A had it on good authority that they had been together since April. Someone in 3-E said April was rubbish, they'd been *caught* on Saturday, and *caught* was doing a lot of work in that sentence. A first-year on the stairwell claimed to have seen them holding hands. A boy in the photo club had a picture — taken from a second-floor window, at an angle, of two figures standing close in the gap between the gym and the boiler house, one of them carrying a bag. He showed it around like a trophy. The picture was not incriminating. That was what made it incriminating. Itsuki looked at it and understood, with a sinking feeling, that a photograph of two people standing a metre apart in a place they had no reason to be was worth more than any explanation. At lunch Riko arrived at his desk and sat on it rather than next to it. "Kurose." "Tachibana." "Fujisaki Akari." "Fujisaki Akari." She hit him with her bag. "You had *one job*, which was to be boring." "I was boring. I was extremely boring." "You were in an *alley* with her." "It's a corridor." "*With her.*" --- He was asked about it four times before the end of the day and answered differently each time, because each time he was asked a different question. *What were you doing?* — Getting cups. *Was she okay?* — She turned her ankle. *How long have you two been—* — I don't know. *Where were you, exactly?* — Behind the gym. That last one came from a boy in 3-C called Aida, who had a notebook and the manner of a detective in a television drama. "Behind the gym," Aida repeated, writing it down. "By the service corridor." "So the service corridor." "Behind the gym," said Itsuki, because he had said it once already and it was true. Aida wrote *behind the gym* and underlined it twice. --- The following morning the story had changed shape again, in the way a river changes shape around a rock. It was no longer a festival thing. It had gone back in time. Now it was *since the spring*, and the festival was simply where it had come out, and the evidence was that in April someone had seen them talking in the corridor outside the English room — which was true, she had said four sentences to him, and Serizawa had been standing right there, but Serizawa being there made it a smaller story, so Serizawa had been quietly removed from it. At the start of third period Akari walked past his desk on the way to the window, put a folded scrap of paper down without breaking stride, and went on. Itsuki unfolded it under the desk. Her handwriting was small and round and pressed hard into the paper. *Kurose-kun. Sorry. I said too much. Ignore whatever they're saying, it'll die down. Also — did you say behind the gym or the service corridor? Because I said the service corridor to Serizawa and now she thinks we've got our stories wrong. — F* Itsuki looked at the last line for a moment. He took out his pen. *Service corridor. Both true. The corridor is behind the gym. — K* He folded it and, on the way to the front to hand in his homework, dropped it on her desk. --- She read it during the break. He knew because she turned around in her chair and looked at him with an expression he could not read at all, and then turned back. At the end of the period she passed his desk again and said, quietly, "That's not going to help." "It's true." "I *know* it's true, that's what I'm—" She stopped, because two girls had slowed down in the aisle. "Never mind. Later." She went out. One of the girls said, "She talked to him," in a whisper that carried the length of the room. Riko, three desks over, put her chin in both hands and watched the door with deep interest. "So," she said. "The service corridor." "Don't." "I'm not doing anything." "You're doing the face." "I don't have a face," said Riko. ---

Chapter Seven Denial, Performed Publicly

Akari's plan, as far as Itsuki could reconstruct it, was to deny the rumour so thoroughly that it died. She began on Thursday at lunch, in the 3-D classroom, with the door open, to a group of six, in a voice pitched to carry. "It's not true. Nothing's going on. He helped me with some cups, that's all. It's genuinely that boring." Her audience made the sound an audience makes when it has decided not to believe you. "Then why were you in the corridor?" said someone. "Because of the cups!" "With him." "Because he was *there*!" By the end of the day this had become: *Fujisaki says nothing's going on, and she got quite insistent about it.* Insistent. Akari had spent three minutes insisting and had come out the other side looking like a girl with something to hide. On Friday she tried a different approach, which was to be breezy. "Honestly, if I were going to date someone, do you think it'd be Kurose-kun?" She said this in the shoe lockers, in front of eleven people, one of whom was Itsuki. "Hey," he said. "Present company excepted." "That's not what you said." "I said *if I were going to date someone*—" "So it's a downgrade." "I didn't say downgrade, I said—" She turned to the eleven. "See? This is what he's like. He does this." The eleven laughed. Then one of them said, "So you're not denying it," and Akari opened her mouth and closed it again, because she had walked into a doorframe of her own construction. --- Itsuki's own method was to answer accurately, which he discovered was the worst possible method. On Friday afternoon a girl from 3-A he had never spoken to stopped him by the stairs and said, "Kurose-kun. Is it true?" "Which part?" Her eyes lit up. "There are *parts*?" "No — I mean—" He adjusted his glasses. "It's a corridor." "What is?" "The thing." She went away looking delighted. --- The rumour reached Nanami on Saturday, which meant it reached her through East High, which meant it had crossed a road, two train stops, and a school boundary in under a week. She arrived at the apartment at eleven on Saturday morning, let herself in with the spare key, and announced from the genkan, "Fujisaki Akari." Itsuki was doing washing-up. "Good morning to you too." "Is she pretty?" "Yes." "Is she *nice*?" "...Yes." Nanami came into the kitchen and leaned against the counter with her arms folded, hair still damp from the walk, and looked at him with the flat suspicion of a customs officer. "Onii-chan. You've been at that school two and a half years and you've never had a *thing*." "I still don't have a thing." "Everyone at East knows about it. Everyone. A girl in my class has a cousin at your school." She took an apple out of the bowl and bit it, aggressively. "So what's she like." "She's a person." "Oh, *good*." "She turned her ankle. I carried some cups." He put a plate in the rack. "There's no plot." Nanami chewed. "You carried her bag, though." "Who told you I carried her bag?" "Everyone," said Nanami, which was not an answer, and took another bite. Then, after a moment, in a slightly different voice: "Is she going to be around?" "I don't know." "Hm." She turned the apple over. "I want to meet her." "You don't have to meet her." "I want to *meet* her," Nanami said, and went to sit down, which ended the matter. --- On Monday, Ayane found him at the bicycle racks after school. She had her bag on both shoulders and her hair tucked behind her ears and she said, "Hi," and then, "So. Fujisaki-san." "You too?" "I'm not asking properly. I just heard." She smiled, and it was the smile she used for her mother's jokes. "It's nice. She's nice." "I keep telling people it's nothing." "Yeah, but they're saying it anyway, so." Ayane hitched her bag up. "Are you coming for Obon or not? Mum's started buying things." "I said maybe." "Great. Maybe." She turned away, and then turned back, and there was something in her face that hadn't been there a second ago. "It's just — you'd tell me, wouldn't you? If there was something. Before it got all the way to the whole school." Itsuki looked at her. "Sure," he said. "Okay." She nodded twice. "Okay. Bye." He watched her go up the slope towards the gate and thought about the fact that he had just said *sure*, and that he had not, in fact, told her anything, and that there had not been anything to tell her until Thursday, and that in the eight days since Thursday he had not once thought to send her a message. ---

Chapter Eight The Girl Who Counts Details

The library was the only room in the school that was cold in June. Itsuki went there on Tuesday after class because it was the one place where nobody asked him anything. He took the corner seat behind the periodicals rack, opened a book he was not going to read, and put his head down. He had been there twelve minutes when a shadow fell across the table. "Kurose-kun." He looked up. He knew her face the way he knew most faces in the year group — a name attached to a shape, filed and never used. Mio Kanzaki. 3-C. Long midnight-blue hair, straight and slightly uneven at the ends, and a grey-blue gaze that came at you without any of the small social cushions most people put around their eyes. She had a notebook in her hand and her bag on one shoulder and she stood there as though waiting to be told to sit, but not as though she expected to be refused. "Can I?" she said, and sat down before he answered. Itsuki closed the book. "You're the one from the corridor," she said. "That's what they tell me." "Mm." She opened the notebook. It was not a fresh notebook. There were page markers in it. "I'm not going to ask if it's true. That's the uninteresting part." "Then what's the interesting part?" "That there are nine versions and none of them fit." She turned the notebook round and pushed it across the table. Itsuki looked at it. It was a page of notes in small, neat handwriting, and it was organised — that was the strange thing about it. Names of classes, dates, times. A list headed *ACCOUNTS*, with five entries, each one a single line. *Yoshida (3-B): saw him holding her bag by the boiler house, ~15:40.* *Mori (3-D): says Fujisaki left the café at 15:20 for cups, alone.* *Aida (3-C): told the corridor is "behind the gym," ~30m long, leads to north exit.* *1st-yr on stairs: heard her say "thank you" and "you didn't have to."* *Photo club (2nd-yr): shot from window, 15:52, figures ~1m apart, no contact.* Itsuki read it twice. "Thirty metres," Mio said. "That's how long that corridor is. It takes about forty seconds to walk it, so they were in there for at least a minute and a half, and in that minute and a half she turned her ankle, dropped a crate of cups, and got as far as the north exit." "Right." "Which is a lot to happen in a corridor in ninety seconds, if you're just walking through it." She turned the notebook back round. "So either the timeline's wrong, or something happened in there that nobody saw." "Something happened in there that nobody saw." Mio's pen stopped. "Which was?" she said. Itsuki leaned back. "She fell on the stairs. The cups went down. I picked them up. Her ankle hurt, so we went round the back instead of up through the crowd." He pushed his glasses up. "That's it. That's the ninety seconds." Mio looked at the page, then at him, and something in her expression shifted — not disappointment, exactly. Recalibration. "Then why does everyone have a different answer?" "Because everyone asked a different question." "That's not a satisfying answer." "It's the true one." "It can be both." She wrote something, short. "So the corridor isn't the event. It's where you went to get out of the crowd." "Yeah." "And she asked you to keep it quiet." Itsuki paused. "How do you know that?" "Because otherwise you'd have gone up the stairs, and there'd have been nothing to see." Mio capped her pen. "Also because you've been telling everyone it's nothing in a voice that sounds like you were told to." --- She sat back and looked at him properly for the first time, and Itsuki became aware that he had just been taken apart in a library by a girl he had never spoken to, and that he had, annoyingly, enjoyed it. "You're not going to write any of this down anywhere," he said. "I am, actually. That's what the notebook's for." She tapped it. "But not for anyone else. Nobody reads it." "Then why keep it?" "Because if I don't, the version in my head gets sloppy." She stood up, and gathered her bag, and did not leave immediately. "Kurose-kun. There's a hole in it." "I know." "You know?" "There's always a hole. It's a corridor behind a gym in a school with nine hundred people in it." He picked his book back up. "The hole's not the problem. The problem is that the hole's the only part anyone wants." Mio stood there a moment longer, and then she said, "Do you mind if I ask you more? Not today. Not in here." She glanced at the periodicals rack. "People come in here to be left alone. It's rude." "Ask me wherever." "All right." She shouldered her bag. "Then somewhere that isn't here. Thursday, after class. The bench by the incinerator — nobody uses it." "Nobody uses it because of the smell." "Yes," said Mio. "That's why nobody will hear us." She left. Itsuki sat looking at the door for a while, and then at the page of his book, which he still had not read. ---

Chapter Nine Something Like Progress

Thursday, the bench by the incinerator. It smelled, exactly as promised, of warm ash and old cardboard, and Mio was already there with two cans of coffee. She handed him one. "You didn't have to," Itsuki said. "It's from the machine. It's not a favour." She sat down at the far end of the bench, one knee drawn up. "I've got four more questions and then you can go." "Go ahead." "The photo's from 15:52. You said you left the café side at about half past three." "Twenty to four. Aida asked me at the lockers." "So the corridor's about ten minutes, and then the north exit, and then the photo's two minutes after that." Mio turned her can a quarter turn. "Except the photo's from the *second-floor window over the north exit*, and the north exit is at the end of the corridor, and it's got a door that opens outward." Itsuki waited. "If you'd gone out that door," she said, "the photo would show the door open. It doesn't. It shows the door shut, and it shows the two of you in the gap, and she's not carrying the crate." "The crate's on the ground." "On the ground *inside*?" "On the ground outside. She put it down to open the door." "Then the door would be open." "Then she put it down, opened the door, and stood in it for a second because someone on the other side said her name." Itsuki took the tab off his coffee. "Then she went out. The door shut behind her. Then the photo." Mio looked at him for a long moment. "You've thought about this." "Only since Saturday." "Hm." She wrote. "That's not normal, by the way. Most people wouldn't be able to place themselves to the minute." "Most people didn't get a photograph taken of them." "Fair." She turned the page. "Next question. Why did you say 'behind the gym' to Aida when the corridor is the thing that matters?" "Because Aida writes everything down and puts it on the 3-C board, and if I gave him the corridor he'd have drawn a *map*." Mio's mouth moved. It was the closest thing to a smile she'd shown him. "He did draw a map," she said. "It's wrong. He's put the north exit on the east side." "See." "So you were managing him." "I was being left alone. It's a different thing." "Is it?" Itsuki drank his coffee. Mio turned to a fresh page and was quiet for a while. The incinerator ticked as it cooled. "Kurose-kun," she said eventually. "Can I say the thing you won't like?" "Go on." "You're not actually trying to kill the rumour. You're waiting for it to die on its own." "That's the same thing." "It isn't." She turned the can in her hands. "If you were trying to kill it, you'd have told Aida the corridor and let him draw the map, because the map is boring and boring kills stories. She's doing it in public, loudly, to groups." "And?" "And the two of you are producing different amounts of effort in different places, and people can feel it. That's why it's getting worse." She paused. "It's also why she's getting the worse end of it." Itsuki thought about that. He thought about Akari at the shoe lockers, saying *if I were going to date someone*, and the eleven people laughing, and the fact that she had said it in front of him without warning him first. "Yeah," he said. "She is." Mio nodded slowly, and wrote something, and then she said, "I've got one more and it's the stupid one." "Go on." "Do you *like* her?" Itsuki looked out at the incinerator block. "I don't know," he said. "I've spoken to her about six times." "That's not the same as no." "No," he admitted. "It's not." Mio closed the notebook. "All right," she said. "I've got what I need for now. Not the answer. The shape of it." She stood and put her can in the bin, and then she stopped. "One thing." "Mm." "On Saturday, when the cups went down. Did she say anything? Before the corridor." Itsuki thought back. The stairwell, the crate, the way she'd said *don't say anything* while balancing four stacks of paper cups on her chin. "She said she had a system," he said. Mio stood very still. "A system," she repeated. "Yeah." "For carrying the crate." "Yeah." "She'd been up and down the stairs four times, and she had a system." "That's what she said." Mio looked at the incinerator, and then at the sky above the gym roof, and then she sat back down. "Then she'd done it four times already without help," she said. "So why did she let you take the cups?" Itsuki opened his mouth. Closed it. "Because the crate had already gone over," he said. "She didn't have a choice." "Mm. Maybe." Mio pulled the notebook back out. "Or maybe she was tired enough that being helped was better than the system, and she didn't want to say so, because she's the kind of person who says *I'm fine* while hopping on one foot." She clicked her pen. "That's not a rumour question. That's just a thing I noticed." She wrote for a moment, then glanced up. "You should tell her the map thing," she said. "The boring-answer thing. It might help her." "She's not going to want strategy from me." "Then don't call it strategy. Call it a suggestion and let her throw it away." Mio shut the notebook. "She's got more people telling her what to do than you have. That's the actual problem." --- Itsuki walked home that evening with the coffee can in his hand and no idea what to do with it. The rumour had been a week and a half old. It had already gone through three shapes and had reached another school. His mother had texted him *heard you've got a girlfriend, well done* with no question mark, and he had texted back *not really*, and she had sent a thumbs-up. Nanami was coming on Saturday and had already announced she wanted to meet her. Ayane had asked him to tell her things before the whole school knew. And now a girl he had spoken to twice had a notebook with his afternoon mapped out to the minute, and had told him, correctly, that he was not actually doing anything. Itsuki stopped at the corner by the station and looked at the traffic lights going through their cycle. For two years, being overlooked had worked. It had cost him nothing and returned him nothing and he had called that peace. Then someone had fallen down some stairs, and eleven days later he was standing at a crossing with a cold can in his hand, holding a strategy he hadn't asked for, and the thing he could not get out of his head was not the rumour and not the photograph and not even the corridor. It was that Akari had carried that crate up those stairs four times alone, and told everyone she had a system, and been believed. He threw the can in the bin. The light went green. He didn't move for a second, and then he did, and behind him the incinerator block at the school sat dark and cold and entirely full of nothing, which was, Itsuki thought, the only thing in Hoshimiya that was. ---

Epilogue — Thursday, Again

The bench by the incinerator became a Thursday thing without either of them deciding it. Mio brought the coffee the second time. Itsuki brought it the third, and got the wrong kind, and she drank it anyway without comment, which he understood to be a review. On the fourth Thursday she brought the notebook but didn't open it. "I've got a problem," she said. "With the timeline?" "With me." She pressed her thumbs into the rim of her can. "I've got everything except the part where she actually is. Fujisaki-san. I've got where she stood and when she left and how long the corridor is, and I don't know a single thing about her that isn't in a witness statement." She looked at him. "I've been treating her like a diagram." "That's not the worst thing to be treated like." "It is if you're a person." Mio's thumb worried the rim. "So I'm going to go and talk to her. Properly. Not to check anything, just to talk. And she's going to think it's about the rumour, because everything is about the rumour right now." "Probably." "And she'll be polite and give me the version she's been giving everyone, and I'll have to sit there and let her, and not correct her." Itsuki thought about Akari saying *it's genuinely that boring* to six people with the door open. "Let her," he said. "Then tell her one true thing about yourself that isn't useful." Mio looked at him. "That's a good idea," she said, in the tone of someone noting a fact rather than paying a compliment. "I have them occasionally." "On Thursdays." "Only on Thursdays." She almost smiled. Then she said, "Kurose-kun. This has stopped being a puzzle." Itsuki turned his can. "I know," he said. "Do you want it to stop?" He thought about the alternative — the bench empty, the coffee bought for one, the year going quiet again the way he'd said he wanted. "No," he said. Mio nodded, once, and opened her notebook, and started a new page with a date at the top, and the incinerator ticked, and somewhere across the yard the last of the afternoon light went off the gym roof. The rumour had eleven days left in it, though neither of them knew that yet. It had a hole in the middle of it and a photograph and nine versions and a girl who was tired of saying *I'm fine.* Itsuki had thought, at the start of April, that a quiet year meant a year in which nothing happened to him. He had been wrong in a way he couldn't yet describe, and right in a way he was going to have to work at, and it was only June. --- {% endcapture %} {{ story_markdown | markdownify }}
