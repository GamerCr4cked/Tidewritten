Chapter One The Girl at the Gate Again

A year passed. I finished junior high, survived the entrance exams, and moved into Seiran Academy’s high-school division. The classrooms were in another building, the assignments were harder, and everyone spoke about the future as though it were a train we had to catch before it left without us. I was busy learning formulas and pretending I understood chemistry. Still, whenever I took the narrow road beside the station, I remembered the girl who had once walked ahead of me with a schoolbag over one shoulder. Hina had moved away because of her father’s transfer. I had wished her well. I had meant it. That did not stop me from looking toward the school gate now and then. Then, on a humid August morning, I saw her. The courtyard below was crowded with students returning after summer break. Umbrellas dripped beside the entrance, cicadas buzzed in the trees, and a teacher tried to direct everyone toward homeroom. Between two younger students, a familiar figure adjusted the strap of a navy bag. Hina. Her hair was a little shorter. She looked a little taller. She was in her second year of junior high now, while I had become a first-year high-school student on the other side of the same campus. I nearly dropped my phone. *She came back.* For three days, I saw her from the upper corridor and did absolutely nothing about it. I told myself that the school buildings were different now. I told myself that I was older and should behave like it. I told myself several very convincing things while checking the courtyard far too often. On the fourth day, the cafeteria defeated all my excuses. I stood in line, scrolling through my phone, when a familiar voice said, “The line moves faster if you actually look at it.” I looked up. Hina stood beside me with her lunch box in hand. Her expression carried the same quiet mischief I remembered. “You…” I said brilliantly. “Me,” she replied. “Surprised?” “When did you come back?” “Last week. I thought you would notice sooner.” “I noticed on the first day.” “And it took you four days to say hello?” “The buildings are different now.” “The cafeteria isn't.” I had no answer to that. Her father’s transfer had been reversed, she explained. Her family had returned to the area, and she had re-enrolled at Seiran. She said it simply, as if she were telling me the weather. “I'm glad you're back,” I said before I could overthink it. She studied me for a moment. “For your sake or mine?” “Both, I hope.” That earned a real laugh. We ate near the cafeteria windows. She offered me a piece of tamagoyaki from her lunch, and I accepted it with more gratitude than a single piece of egg deserved. We talked about teachers, summer homework, and how strange it felt to return to a place that had continued without you. Nothing dramatic happened. But when we left the cafeteria, the campus felt familiar in a way it had not for a year. ---

Chapter Two A Routine We Never Planned

Our new routine formed without a meeting or a promise. At lunch, I went to the cafeteria. Hina did too. Sometimes we met in line; sometimes she waved me over from a table with her friends. If I arrived late, she made a point of telling me that my timing was terrible. If she arrived late, she claimed she had been delayed by “important junior-high business.” Her friend Yui noticed everything. “So that's your senior?” she asked one afternoon, looking at me as I approached. “Haruto,” Hina corrected. “And yes, he's a senior.” “Just a senior?” “A slightly strange senior.” Yui looked at me. “You must be special.” “I assure you, I'm mostly ordinary.” “That's exactly what a strange person would say,” Hina replied. I was beginning to suspect that returning to Seiran had made her even more dangerous. On a rainy Thursday, the cafeteria became crowded and noisy. I escaped to the covered walkway between the buildings, put in my earphones, and watched water gather along the edge of the roof. Someone tapped my shoulder. Hina stood there, holding her folded umbrella. “What are you listening to?” “Something that helps me ignore people.” “Then it must be very good.” She sat on the dry ledge a little way from me. We talked about the music, her new class, and the fact that I had chosen the science track even though I was much better at writing than explaining myself aloud. “You write well,” she said. I turned toward her. “How would you know?” “Your notebook. You were writing in it during lunch once.” “You read it?” “Only the line you left visible. I'm not a criminal.” I laughed, embarrassed. She looked pleased with herself. A gust of wind pushed rain beneath the roof. Hina shifted away from the spray, and her hand brushed mine. Neither of us moved for a moment. Then she drew back, glanced at our hands, and said, “Your fingers are cold.” “It's raining.” “Bring gloves.” “It's August.” “Then stop making excuses.” She looked back at the rain, but the tips of her ears had turned pink. I did not decide that it meant anything. I had learned how easily a hopeful mind could turn a small moment into a whole imagined future. Still, I remembered it all evening. ---

Chapter Three The Little Bird in Her Sketchbook

By September, our friendship had become ordinary enough that our friends could no longer pretend not to notice it. Daichi began asking why I vanished every lunch break. Yui asked Hina why she packed food if she was going to share half of it with me anyway. We both gave unconvincing answers. One day, I forgot my lunch. Hina split her sandwich in two without making a fuss. The next day, I brought an extra canned coffee for her. After that, sharing whatever we had became a rule no one had written down. A week later, we sat on a bench outside the cafeteria while Hina drew in a small sketchbook. She usually kept it angled away from me, so I was surprised when she turned it around. A bird filled the page: small, alert, perched on a branch. Underneath, in neat handwriting, were the words *Little Bird*. I stared at it. “You remember that nickname?” “Of course.” “I thought you might hate it.” “I did think it was strange.” She tilted her head. “But I never hated it.” “I only made it up because I didn't know your name at first.” “I know. Daichi is terrible at whispering.” I covered my face with one hand. “I am going to have a serious conversation with him.” “Please don't. He's entertaining.” She returned to the sketch, adding a few careful lines to the bird’s wings. “Why keep it?” I asked. “Because it reminds me of when we first started talking.” She paused. “And because it sounds like a name someone gives you when they're trying to be kind, even if they're too nervous to say anything normal.” I did not know what to do with that sentence, so I looked at the sketch again. “You can keep the name,” she added. “Just don't announce it across the courtyard.” “I'll try to control myself.” “Good. I would hate to lose my reputation.” That evening, she gave me her number. She said I could message if I needed something important, then added that “being bored” counted as a valid reason. Her mother checked her phone at night, however, so I was not to send messages after eleven. Our first conversation began with a plain *Hey*. She replied three minutes later: *Are you this boring in writing too?* For the next few weeks, our messages filled the small space between dinner and bedtime. We talked about school, songs, books, and the odd things our classmates said. At 10:55, Hina would announce that she had to go. At 10:59, she would send one final message—sometimes a joke, sometimes a random sticker, sometimes just *Hmm*. I began to look forward to that last message far more than was reasonable. One night, she asked, “Do you ever wonder what we'll be like next year?” I typed several answers and deleted them all. Finally, I wrote, “I hope we can still talk like this.” The typing indicator appeared, disappeared, and returned. At 10:59, her reply arrived. *Then don't disappear on me, Haruto.* I read it twice. ---

Chapter Four The Senior Who Came to Cheer

Seiran’s sports festival arrived with bright banners, loudspeakers, and enough enthusiasm to make the entire campus vibrate. The high-school students sat in one section of the stands, pretending to support every event equally. I was not pretending very convincingly when Hina’s badminton match began. She stepped onto the court with a focus I rarely saw during lunch. Her footwork was quick, her returns precise, and her expression so serious that I almost forgot she was the person who argued with me about gloves in August. She won in straight games. When she looked toward the stands afterward, I gave her a thumbs-up. She lifted her water bottle in a small salute, then turned away before her teammates could tease her. They teased her anyway. Yui later told me Hina had been smiling for the rest of the afternoon. I pretended not to hear. A few days later, I caught a cold and stayed in the high-school classroom during lunch. I sent Hina a message saying I was fine, mostly because I knew she would ask where I had gone. Five minutes later, there was a knock at the door. She stood in the hallway holding a small thermos. “My mother made extra soup,” she said. “This is not a special delivery. Don't make it weird.” “I wasn't going to.” “You look like you're about to.” She placed the thermos on my desk and turned to leave. Beneath it was a folded note. *Get well soon. The cafeteria is boring when you aren't there.* I smiled so hard that my cheeks hurt. In October, both divisions helped prepare for the school festival. I made posters for the decorations committee; Hina organised supplies and somehow convinced everyone to follow her plan. We worked in the same hall after classes, surrounded by paper lanterns, coloured string, and the smell of fresh paint. A teacher passed us carrying a box of decorations. She glanced at the two of us, then smiled to herself. Hina noticed. “Don't start,” she warned me. “I didn't say anything.” “You were thinking loudly.” “That's not a real thing.” “It is when you do it.” We laughed, and the teacher kept walking. That afternoon, I realised something had changed. I no longer spent every conversation wondering whether I had said the wrong thing. I could simply be with Hina, listen to her stories, and let the day be good without demanding that it become something more. At the end of the festival, we walked to the gate together. The evening air had turned cool. Hina took one earbud from her bag and held it out. “Share?” I gave her one side of my earphones instead. An old song played between us, quiet beneath the noise of students leaving the campus. Our shoulders touched. Neither of us moved away. ---

Chapter Five Our Kind of Normal

By December, the cold had settled over the campus. The zelkova trees had lost most of their leaves, and students hurried between buildings with scarves tucked into their coats. Hina and I had become part of each other's days. We met when we could, messaged when we could not, and shared the small things that would have seemed unimportant to anyone else. I learned that she drew when she was worried, hated overly sweet drinks, and always saved the last bite of lunch for later. She learned that I wrote stories in the margins of my notes, forgot my umbrella even when the forecast promised rain, and went quiet when I was trying to say something honest. One evening, we stood outside the school gate while the sky faded into winter blue. “Are you worried about next year?” she asked. “Exams, mostly. And what happens after them.” “You'll figure it out.” “You say that as if you're sure.” “I'm not sure.” She adjusted her scarf. “I'm saying it because I think you can.” I wanted to remember the exact way she said it. Then she handed me a folded sheet of paper. “Open it at home,” she instructed. Inside was the drawing of the Little Bird from her sketchbook, now finished with delicate feathers and a branch full of leaves. Beneath it, she had written: *The Little Bird that once seemed far away has found a familiar branch.* I read it twice before I realised I was smiling. The next afternoon, I gave her a small notebook. I had chosen one with a plain blue cover because I knew she would draw on it anyway. “For your sketches,” I said. She turned it over in her hands. “And what will you use it for?” “Writing, probably.” “Then we can compare pages.” “Is that a challenge?” “Obviously.” We walked out through the gate together for the first time without pretending it was a coincidence. Hina walked on my left. Near the corner, she reached over and caught the sleeve of my coat with two fingers. I stopped. She stopped too. For a moment, she looked as if she might say something. Instead, she smiled and let go. The touch was brief, but it stayed with me all the way to the station. Nothing had been formally named. We were still students with exams ahead, families to listen to, and futures we could not predict. Hina was in her second year of junior high; I was in my first year of high school. We had time to grow, and there was no reason to rush what we did not yet understand. But the space between us no longer felt like a wall. That night, at 10:58, my phone lit up. *Good night, Haruto.* A second message followed. *And don't forget your umbrella tomorrow. It's going to rain.* I laughed quietly and set the phone beside my notebook. The Little Bird had returned. Our ordinary days had found their rhythm again. And somewhere between shared lunches, rainy corridors, and one earbud passed across the cold, I had begun to understand that a relationship did not always arrive with a confession. Sometimes it grew, quietly, until two people found themselves walking home side by side. For now, that was our kind of normal. I did not yet know how quickly ordinary days could become important. The next school year would bring a different kind of question: not whether Hina and I could find our way back to each other, but whether we were brave enough to speak honestly about what had grown between us.
