Prologue The Month Left on the Calendar

March began on a Monday.

Itsuki Kurose noticed because the calendar on his refrigerator had a blue fish magnet holding it in place, and the first square of the month was still empty. February had ended with a university brochure, a few school notices, and several small notes: Nanami's spring reading day, Ayane's next community planning session, Mio's bookshop invitation, Riko's promised study session, and the course details Akari had sent. His mother's last message remained in his phone too. He had told her he would let her know when he was ready to talk, and she had accepted that. March had more space than he expected, but less than he wanted.

He stood in front of it with a mug of tea.

“You're doing it again.”

Nanami sat at the low table, sorting a stack of printed pages into two piles. Her school bag rested beside the sofa. She had arrived the previous evening and stayed for breakfast, which meant she had already inspected the refrigerator and found something to criticise.

“Doing what?”

“Looking at the calendar as if it might explain itself.”

“It has dates.”

“A remarkable discovery.”

Itsuki took a sip of tea.

“Your papers are crooked.”

Nanami looked down. “They're in order.”

“They're leaning.”

“That's because the table is uneven.”

“The table has been here longer than you have.”

“Then it should know better.”

He put the mug down and straightened the top sheet. The papers were the latest reading-day programme. Nanami had marked several places in blue and added small notes beside the volunteer assignments. The event was scheduled for Saturday, March 6, at the community library near the east station. It would be smaller than December's inter-school event, but Nanami seemed to have found more ways to worry about it.

“You don't have to check the same list every morning,” he said.

“I know.”

“You checked it last night.”

“I also checked it this morning.”

“That is the same list.”

“And yet it keeps being important.”

Itsuki returned to the calendar.

There were school assessments this week, a meeting with Ms. Shibata, forms to submit, and the graduation schedule the school had finally distributed. The ceremony would take place on Friday, March 19. The date was printed in bold at the top of the notice, as if the school had decided that nobody should be allowed to misunderstand it.

He had read it twice.

“You have your last examinations,” Nanami said. “Then the reading day. Then graduation.”

“That's the order.”

“And Seiran?”

“I have the application checklist.”

“That isn't what I asked.”

Itsuki looked at her.

“I submitted the main form. The school transcript has been sent. I still need to finish the written statement and confirm the final documents.”

“So you're doing it.”

“Yes.”

Nanami smiled, then returned to the programme.

Itsuki's phone vibrated on the counter. A message from Mio appeared.

Mio: The library has the March issue of the student journal. I thought you might want to see it after the assessments.

He read it once, then replied.

Itsuki: I'd like that. Let me know when you're going.

Another notification arrived from Riko, who had sent a photograph of a workbook with a single question circled in red.

Riko: This one is personally attacking me.

Itsuki: Have you read the condition at the bottom?

Riko: I was hoping it would become less important if I ignored it.

Itsuki: Has that worked?

Riko: I hate your questions.

He put the phone down.

Nanami glanced over.

“Busy already?”

“Apparently.”

“Good.”

“You sound pleased.”

“You used to leave every square empty.”

Itsuki looked at the calendar again. The month had only just begun, but the blank spaces were already being claimed by other people, by school, and by choices he had made himself.

He took the pen from its hook.

Under March 6, he wrote Reading day — Nanami. Under March 19, he wrote Graduation. Then he added the meeting with Ms. Shibata and the deadline for Seiran's remaining documents.

He left the other squares alone.

There was no need to fill the whole month in one morning.

“Onii-chan?”

“Hm?”

“Don't forget to eat lunch.”

“I know.”

“You say that and then forget.”

“I have a schedule.”

Nanami pointed at the refrigerator.

“Then write it down.”

Itsuki considered the suggestion.

“No.”

“I knew you would say that.”

She returned to her papers, satisfied with the exchange.

Outside, the morning train passed beyond the apartment blocks. Itsuki finished his tea and began getting ready for school. March had arrived, bringing the final examinations, the last forms, and a date on which he would no longer be a student at Hoshimiya Municipal High School.

He was not ready to call any of it a farewell.

There were still things to do first.

Chapter 1 The Notice with the Final Date

The graduation notice had been placed beside the classroom timetable.

Itsuki read it while the rest of Class 3-B gathered around the board. The school had listed the ceremony time, the required uniform, the arrival period, and the instructions for returning borrowed materials. Beneath that was a separate sheet about the final examinations and the remaining administrative procedures.

Riko stood beside him with her hands in her coat pockets.

“They've made a whole page about how to stand in a line.”

“It's an important skill.”

“I've been practising since primary school.”

“Then you should be qualified.”

She read the next line.

“No bags during the ceremony. No phones. No leaving the designated area.”

“They're trying to prevent you from escaping.”

“I wasn't planning to escape.”

Itsuki looked at her.

“Not yet,” she added.

Ayane approached with a folder. “Have you seen the schedule for the final assessments?”

“It's beside the graduation notice,” Itsuki said.

“I know. I wanted to check whether the dates had changed.”

Riko leaned closer to the page. “They haven't. Unfortunately.”

“You have been studying,” Ayane said.

“I have been negotiating with the material.”

“How is the negotiation going?”

“The material is stubborn.”

Itsuki started toward the classroom. “Try reading the questions.”

Riko followed him. “I do read them.”

“Then read them twice.”

“You make everything sound so easy.”

“It saves time.”

At their desks, Mr. Hasegawa distributed the final assessment schedule. The tests would take place over three days, with the results and remaining school procedures to follow. He reminded the class that the examinations were not a reason to abandon every other responsibility, then looked directly at Riko when he said it.

“I feel targeted,” she whispered.

“You are sitting in the front row.”

“That doesn't make it fair.”

Mr. Hasegawa began explaining the graduation arrangements. Students would return library books, clear their lockers, and complete a short reflection sheet for their homeroom teacher. The final school day would include classroom cleaning and the collection of remaining documents.

Itsuki wrote down the dates.

He had attended this school for three years. He knew the sound of the bell, the route between the main building and the library, and the small delay in the corridor door near the stairs. He knew which vending machine sometimes failed to return change and which classroom window rattled when the wind came from the east.

None of those things appeared on the notice.

At lunch, Akari arrived at the library table carrying a clear folder.

“I finally submitted the communications application.”

Itsuki looked up from his book. “The Seiran one?”

“Yes. The first preference and the practical placement choices are all in. My parents checked the costs with me, and we found a plan that works.”

“That's good.”

Akari sat down across from him. “I was nervous before pressing the final button.”

“That seems normal.”

“I know. I still wanted to tell someone.”

“You did the work.”

“And you can still say you're happy for me.”

Itsuki closed his book. “I'm happy for you.”

Akari smiled. “Thank you.”

She took a sheet from the folder and showed him a list of the remaining interview topics. The course asked applicants to describe a communication project they had found useful and explain how they would approach a small community campaign.

“I have ideas,” she said. “I'm trying not to write the answer as if I'm already giving a speech.”

“Then write it as if you're explaining it to one person.”

“You always make it sound simple.”

“It isn't. It's just a way to start.”

Akari considered that and wrote a note in the margin.

“I'll try.”

She put the papers away. Before leaving, she asked whether he had finished the Seiran statement.

“Not yet.”

“What's left?”

“A short explanation of why I want the programme.”

“That should be easy. You like English.”

“That's one sentence.”

“You like writing too.”

“That's another.”

“Then you're halfway there.”

“A generous calculation.”

Akari laughed and returned to her friends.

Itsuki opened his notebook. The blank statement page waited between the application checklist and a list of questions for Ms. Shibata.

He wrote one sentence.

Then he crossed out the last few words and tried again.

It was not finished, but it had stopped being empty.

Chapter 2 A Question with No Deadline

The school guidance office was quieter than usual after the final bell.

Itsuki arrived with his application folder and found Ms. Shibata reviewing a stack of forms. She looked up and gestured toward the chair.

“Kurose. I was expecting you.”

“I brought the updated documents.”

He placed the folder on her desk. The transcript request had been processed, the main application form submitted, and the admissions office had confirmed receipt. The remaining items were his written statement and a final confirmation of the financial-support documents.

Ms. Shibata checked the list.

“You've made good progress.”

“I had to rewrite the statement.”

“Why?”

“The first version sounded like I was trying to impress someone.”

She smiled. “What did you change?”

“I wrote what I actually want to study.”

“That is usually more useful.”

Itsuki opened the folder and showed her the revised page. He had written about his interest in English, the way language could carry meaning differently depending on context, and his curiosity about editing and communication. He had not named a career. He had explained why he wanted to study the subjects and what he hoped to learn.

Ms. Shibata read it carefully.

“This sounds more like you.”

“Is that good?”

“It is honest and specific. You are not promising that you will become a famous writer or solve a problem nobody else has noticed. You are explaining why this programme is worth studying.”

“I don't think I could promise the other things.”

“Good. Don't.”

She pointed to one sentence.

“You could make this a little clearer. You say you want to understand how words reach people, but you do not explain what interests you about that.”

Itsuki considered the line.

“I think it's because the same information can be understood differently depending on how it's presented.”

“Then write that.”

He made the change.

They reviewed the final documents and discussed the remaining fees. His mother had already confirmed the support she was prepared to provide, and his former stepfather had offered to help with some study-related expenses if needed. Itsuki did not want to assume that every cost would be covered without asking, so he had prepared a simple estimate of tuition, transport, books, and other expenses.

Ms. Shibata checked the figures.

“This is sensible. Have you discussed the arrangement with the people supporting you?”

“Yes. I still need to confirm the scholarship result and the first payment date.”

“Then put those on your checklist. You are allowed to ask questions about money. It is better than discovering a missing detail after the deadline.”

“I agree.”

He looked at the statement again.

“I still don't know what kind of work I want afterward.”

“You have said that before.”

“I thought I should have an answer by now.”

Ms. Shibata set the page down.

“You have chosen a subject you want to study. That is an answer to the question in front of you. Your eventual work will depend on what you learn, what you enjoy, and what opportunities you find. You can take that question with you without pretending it has already been solved.”

Itsuki nodded.

“I suppose.”

“You do not have to be certain about every year after graduation to make a reasonable decision now.”

He put the revised statement back into the folder.

At the door, Ms. Shibata called his name once more.

“Kurose.”

He turned.

“Whatever the result, you have done the work of considering this carefully. Remember that.”

“I will.”

He left the office.

In the corridor, a group of students were carrying a stack of old textbooks toward the storage room. One of them dropped a book. It landed flat with a sound that echoed down the hall.

“Careful,” another student said.

Itsuki stepped around them and headed for the library.

Mio was at the window table, reading a printed journal. She had a pencil beside her hand and a small stack of notes arranged in a neat line.

“How did it go?” she asked.

“Better. I revised the statement.”

“Did it sound like you?”

Itsuki sat down. “Apparently.”

“That's a good sign.”

He took out the page and showed her the paragraph he had changed.

Mio read it without rushing.

“I like this sentence,” she said, pointing to the line about how the same information could be understood differently depending on how it was presented.

“Ms. Shibata suggested I explain it.”

“It gives the interest a reason.”

“That's what she said.”

Mio returned the page.

“I should do the same with my application.”

“Have you decided on the publishing course?”

“I have. The programme at Kiyose College has an editorial track and a student journal. I visited the department last week and spoke with one of the lecturers.”

“You liked it?”

“I liked the work they showed me. The students edit each other's writing, prepare a small publication, and learn the practical side of production. I don't know whether I'll enjoy every part of it, but I want to try.”

“Then that sounds like your answer.”

She looked at him. “I still have to finish the application.”

“So do I.”

“We are both very organised.”

“A troubling development.”

Mio smiled.

They spent the rest of lunch reading. Itsuki's application folder remained beside his chair, no longer an unopened problem. The question about his future still had no deadline attached to it.

For once, he was content to leave it that way.

Interlude I The Programme in Nanami's Folder

Nanami had divided the spring reading-day programme into four sections.

The first contained the schedule. The second held the volunteer list. The third contained the book-exchange rules. The fourth was for everything she had not yet decided where to put.

Yui called it the miscellaneous section.

Nanami called it the section that kept the project alive.

They were sitting in the library committee room at Hoshimiya East High School, where a teacher had given them permission to use the large table after classes. A poster draft lay between them. Professor Whiskers appeared in one corner, wearing a paper scarf that Yui had insisted was seasonal.

“He looks cold,” Yui said.

“He's made of paper.”

“Exactly.”

Nanami adjusted the poster.

“The title needs to be larger.”

“The title is already larger.”

“Not enough.”

“You said that yesterday.”

“And I was right yesterday.”

Yui laughed.

The event would have a small book exchange, a reading corner, and three activity tables. One would be for recommendations, one for a short group reading, and one for puzzles suitable for younger children. Riko had offered to help with the puzzle table. Nanami had been relieved, then worried that she had assigned too much work to someone who still had examinations to prepare for.

“Riko said she can make the activity cards,” Yui reminded her.

“She said she could help. I don't want to give her the whole table.”

“Then ask what she wants to do.”

Nanami looked at her.

“You sound like Onii-chan.”

“I am becoming wise.”

“That's unfortunate.”

They checked the volunteer list again. Nanami had left a blank space beside her own name. She wanted to welcome visitors and introduce the reading corner, but she also wanted to spend time with the children who had helped choose the books. The schedule could not place her everywhere.

“You should put yourself where you want to be,” Yui said.

“I want to be in several places.”

“Then choose the one that matters most and let the others help.”

Nanami looked at the page.

She wrote her name beside the reading corner.

It was a small decision, but the space looked less unfinished.

Her phone vibrated. A message from her father asked whether she wanted to have lunch with him the following Sunday. He added that Akemi would be out of town that weekend, so they could have their usual time together if Nanami was free.

Nanami replied that she would come.

Yui glanced over.

“Your father?”

“He asked about lunch.”

“Are you going?”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

Nanami returned to the programme.

There were still things she wanted to ask her father. There were also things she did not need to ask him every time they met. Their relationship had become easier when she stopped treating every visit as a test of whether the old arrangement could return.

She could miss what had changed and still enjoy what remained.

“Yui.”

“Hm?”

“Can you check the poster one more time?”

Yui leaned over.

“The title is readable. The date is correct. The library address is correct. Professor Whiskers looks suspicious, but that's not a printing error.”

“He's thoughtful.”

“He looks like he knows something.”

“He does.”

Nanami saved the file.

The event was beginning to look like something she had made rather than something she was trying to keep from falling apart.

That was a good feeling.

Chapter 3 The Work of Being Ready

The community library smelled of paper, floor polish, and the faint sweetness of the bakery across the street.

Nanami arrived on Wednesday afternoon with Yui and a folder of printed schedules. The librarian had reserved the main room for Saturday and moved two tables near the entrance. The reading corner was arranged beside the window, and a low shelf held the books selected for the exchange.

“We need to check the labels,” Nanami said.

Yui looked at the shelves.

“We checked them yesterday.”

“We changed the order.”

“We changed two labels.”

“Exactly.”

They began checking.

Itsuki arrived twenty minutes later carrying two cardboard boxes. Nanami had asked him to help move the extra books from the storage room, and he had agreed after school.

“You're late,” she said.

He looked at the clock.

“I'm four minutes early.”

“The schedule has changed.”

“You didn't tell me.”

“I just did.”

Yui laughed from the other side of the table.

Itsuki set down the boxes.

“Where do these go?”

Nanami showed him the shelf beside the reading corner. He sorted the books by size while she checked the recommendation cards. Yui arranged the sign-in table and tested the small bell at the information desk.

“It still sounds like a spoon,” she said.

“A spoon?” Itsuki asked.

“Against a glass.”

“That's oddly specific.”

“It's a specific sound.”

Nanami looked up from the cards.

“Can you both stop evaluating the bell?”

“It started with her,” Itsuki said.

“You joined in.”

“I was gathering information.”

They finished the shelves and moved to the activity tables. Riko arrived carrying a folder of puzzle cards and a packet of coloured pencils.

“I bring intellectual entertainment.”

Nanami took the folder.

“You made all of these?”

“Some. Yui sent me the age groups, and I made three levels. The first is easy, the second requires a little thinking, and the third is for children who enjoy making adults look slow.”

Itsuki examined one card.

“This one has two possible answers.”

Riko looked at it.

“Does it?”

“If the child assumes the shapes can be rotated.”

She leaned over his shoulder.

“I didn't think of that.”

“You should add a note.”

Riko took the card back.

“Fine. You're now the official enemy of ambiguous puzzles.”

“A difficult position.”

“I expect a badge.”

Nanami smiled.

“You can have one if you finish the rest of the cards.”

“I have been manipulated.”

“You volunteered.”

“The details were hidden.”

They spent the next hour preparing the tables. Akari arrived after her interview practice, carrying a small stack of printed notices. She had offered to help make the event information easier to understand, and the new sheets included a simple map of the library and a clear timetable.

“I checked the address and the bus route,” she said. “The old notice had the entrance on the wrong side.”

Nanami looked at the map.

“That's much clearer.”

“I asked the librarian before printing it.”

“Thank you.”

Akari placed the notices beside the sign-in table.

“I can't stay all day on Saturday, but I'll come for the first hour.”

“That's fine,” Nanami said. “You don't have to stay longer than you can.”

Akari smiled.

“I know. I wanted to come.”

Ayane arrived a little later with a small box of bookmarks donated by the community centre. She had been speaking with the local planning group about the library's accessibility and asked whether they needed feedback forms for visitors.

“We could put a small card beside the exit,” she suggested. “People can write what they liked or what would make the next event easier to use.”

Nanami considered it.

“That would help. But I don't want the children to feel as if they're filling out a school survey.”

“We can make it optional and keep it simple.”

“Then let's do that.”

Ayane placed the bookmarks on the table.

“I can also stay for the afternoon.”

“You don't have to.”

“I know.”

Nanami looked around the room.

For a moment, the event felt less like a list she had to protect and more like a room full of people who had chosen to help. The tables were not perfectly straight. One label was still missing. Professor Whiskers had lost his paper scarf somewhere between the storage room and the reading corner.

It was not perfect.

It was ready enough.

“We'll finish the rest on Saturday morning,” Nanami said.

Riko held up the puzzle cards.

“And I'll bring the badge.”

“You don't have one.”

“I will.”

Itsuki looked at the shelf.

“Professor Whiskers has it.”

Nanami turned.

The paper cat had been placed on the top shelf, beside a stack of picture books. Its scarf was back around its neck.

“He looks responsible,” Yui said.

“He is,” Nanami replied.

Riko nodded solemnly.

“More than most of us.”

Itsuki picked up the empty box.

“I hope he remembers the schedule.”

Nanami laughed.

The library closed at six. They packed the remaining materials and left the room in order. Outside, the evening had grown cool, and the streetlights were beginning to come on.

Nanami walked with Itsuki toward the station.

“Thank you for helping.”

“You asked.”

“I know.”

She adjusted the strap of her bag.

“I think it'll be good.”

“It will.”

“You sound certain.”

“You prepared.”

Nanami smiled.

“That's not the same thing.”

“It's a reasonable start.”

She nodded.

They reached the station entrance and separated. Nanami went toward the east platform. Itsuki crossed to the other side.

He had spent the afternoon moving boxes and checking labels.

He was looking forward to Saturday.

Chapter 4 The River Path in the Present

Ayane sent the invitation on Tuesday evening.

Ayane: The community planning group is walking the river path on Thursday morning. They're collecting comments about the seating and lighting. Would you like to come?

Itsuki checked his calendar. The reading day was the following afternoon, and the morning was free.

Itsuki: Sure. What time?

Ayane: Ten, near the east bridge.

He replied that he would be there.

The river path was quiet when he arrived. A few people were walking dogs, and a cyclist passed with a small bell. Ayane stood near the bridge with a clipboard and a thermos.

“Good morning.”

“Morning.”

“You came.”

“I said I would.”

“I know. I still like seeing it happen.”

They joined the small group. A representative from the city planning office explained the proposed changes: two additional benches, a repaired section of pavement, and improved lighting near the steps. The group walked the route and noted where the path became narrow or difficult to use.

Ayane asked several questions. She was interested in how the city decided which improvements to prioritise and how residents could keep track of the work after submitting feedback.

Itsuki listened. He had not known much about the process before, but he could see why Ayane liked it. The discussion was practical. People disagreed about the placement of the benches, then found a way to compare the suggestions without treating the disagreement as a problem.

When the meeting ended, Ayane and Itsuki stayed near the bridge.

“I think I'll apply for the community development course,” she said.

“The one with fieldwork?”

“Yes. I checked the requirements. It includes public-space planning, local research, and a placement with a community organisation.”

“That sounds like what you were looking for.”

“I think so. I still have to compare the universities, but I know which direction I want.”

She looked toward the path.

“I used to think I wanted to preserve the places we remembered. Now I think I want to understand how people keep making them useful.”

Itsuki nodded.

“That sounds more difficult.”

“It is.”

“Good luck.”

Ayane smiled.

“Thank you.”

They began walking toward the station. The old bakery stood at the corner, its windows fogged slightly from the warm air inside. Ayane glanced at it but did not suggest going in.

“Do you remember when we used to come here after school?” she asked.

“Sometimes.”

“I remember the table by the window.”

“You liked watching the buses.”

She looked surprised.

“You remember that?”

“You used to count how many passed before the next train.”

Ayane smiled.

“I forgot you remembered.”

“I forgot a lot of other things.”

“So did I.”

They walked a little farther.

“I'm glad we're friends now,” Ayane said. “Not because we used to be. Because I like talking to you now.”

Itsuki considered his answer.

“I like talking to you too.”

“Even when I ask you to look at city maps?”

“Especially then.”

She laughed.

They reached the station and parted at the gates.

Itsuki went home with a folded map in his bag and a new date for the next planning meeting. He had not returned to the friendship they had when they were children.

He had spent the morning with the person Ayane was now.

That was better.

Chapter 5 A Puzzle with Three Answers

Riko had spent most of Thursday afternoon trying to make one puzzle more difficult.

She had begun with a simple sequence, added two conditions, removed one clue, and then discovered that the answer could be reached in three different ways. Itsuki found her at the library table with the page covered in arrows.

“What happened?”

“The puzzle is being difficult.”

“You made it.”

“That doesn't mean I have to like it.”

He sat opposite her and looked at the page.

“What do you want the children to work out?”

“The order of the books.”

“Then why are there four numbers and a shape?”

“Because I got ambitious.”

“You can remove one.”

Riko sighed.

“I know.”

She crossed out the shape.

“There. A tragedy.”

Itsuki checked the remaining clues.

“This still has two answers.”

Riko leaned back.

“I hate you.”

“You asked me to check it.”

“I expected support.”

“This is support.”

She laughed despite herself.

They worked through the clues together. Riko changed one condition and tested the result. This time, the answer was unique.

“There,” she said. “A perfect puzzle.”

“A usable puzzle.”

“You always have to reduce the achievement.”

“I am protecting you from overconfidence.”

“I was doing fine without your protection.”

She gathered the cards and placed them in the folder.

For a moment, she did not make another joke.

“I got the result from the support assessment,” she said.

Itsuki looked at her.

“How was it?”

“Better. Not perfect. I passed the part I was worried about, and I didn't leave anything blank.”

“That's good.”

“I know.”

She looked at the folder.

“I used to think that if I wasn't good at something immediately, I should make a joke before anyone noticed. It was easier than letting people see I was worried.”

Itsuki waited.

“I'm still going to make jokes,” she added. “I have a reputation to maintain.”

“A fragile one.”

“A respected one.”

She smiled.

“But I think I can ask for help without pretending I don't care.”

“You already do.”

“Sometimes.”

Riko turned the folder over in her hands.

“I like spending time with you, you know.”

Itsuki nodded.

“I like spending time with you too.”

She looked at him for a moment, then gave a small shrug.

“Good. I would hate to have to find another person to annoy.”

“There are several candidates.”

“None of them are as convenient.”

He smiled.

Riko stood and put the folder in her bag.

“I'm thinking about what to do after school,” she said. “I might look at a course related to education or childcare. I liked helping at the reading event last time, and I liked working with the younger children.”

“You should look into it.”

“I will. I'm not saying I'm going to become a teacher tomorrow.”

“That would be a sudden change.”

“I'd need a better timetable.”

She headed toward the exit, then turned back.

“And Kurose?”

“Yes?”

“Don't become so serious after graduation that you stop understanding jokes.”

“I'll try to maintain the skill.”

“Good.”

She left.

Itsuki looked at the empty chair.

Riko had not asked him to give her a promise. She had not needed a dramatic answer. She had simply told him something she had been hiding behind jokes, then carried her folder toward the next thing she wanted to try.

He was glad she had.

Chapter 6 The Last Assessment

By Friday, the final assessments had been underway for three days.

Class 3-B was quiet before the first paper was distributed. Students checked their pencils, moved their bags to the designated area, and tried not to look at the clock. Itsuki read the instructions and turned to the first page.

He knew the material. That did not mean every question would be easy.

The English paper began with a passage about translation and public information. Itsuki read it once for the general argument, then returned to the questions. He checked the wording carefully, especially where two answers seemed almost identical.

Across the room, a chair moved.

Someone coughed.

The clock continued its slow progress.

When the teacher called time, Itsuki placed his pencil down and reviewed the final answer. He had one minute left. He used it to check a sentence he had written in the short-response section, then handed in the paper.

Riko was waiting outside the classroom.

“How was it?”

“Fine.”

“That's not helpful.”

“It was an assessment.”

“You are impossible to interview.”

She held her own paper schedule in one hand.

It had been part of his year.

It would remain part of it.

Chapter 13 A Page Mio Had Not Shown Him

Mio found Itsuki in the library after school.

She had a notebook open, but it was not the blue journal she had bought at the bookshop. This one was older, with a plain dark cover and several loose pages tucked between the sections.

“Can I show you something?” she asked.

“Of course.”

She turned the notebook toward him.

It contained a short piece she had written during the winter. It was about a person who kept a list of places where they had once waited for someone. The writing was restrained, with a few details that made the scenes feel familiar without explaining why they mattered.

Itsuki read it slowly.

“You wrote this?”

“Yes.”

“When?”

“December. I wasn't sure whether I wanted anyone to read it.”

He reached the last page and looked up.

“Why are you showing me now?”

Mio rested her hands on the notebook.

“Because I want to submit it to the student journal at Kiyose. The editor asked whether I had something of my own to include. I have revised it twice, but I still don't know if it is ready.”

“Do you want me to read it as an editor or as a friend?”

She thought about that.

“Both, if you can.”

“I can try.”

He read it again, this time paying attention to the transitions. One paragraph repeated an idea from the previous page. Another ended before the image had settled.

He pointed to the first one.

“I think this sentence says what the next paragraph already shows.”

Mio read it.

“You're right.”

“And this ending feels like you stopped because you reached the bottom of the page.”

She looked at him.

“That is a very specific criticism.”

“I thought you wanted both.”

“I did.”

She made notes in the margin.

“I like the piece,” he added. “The quiet parts work. I just think you can trust the reader a little more.”

Mio looked at the final paragraph.

“I was afraid people wouldn't understand what I meant.”

“They might not understand every part. That's all right.”

She nodded.

“I spend a lot of time trying to make sure I understand other people. Sometimes I forget that I can write something without controlling how everyone reads it.”

Itsuki considered that.

“You can still make it clear.”

“Yes. But I don't have to explain every silence.”

He returned the notebook.

“I think you should submit it.”

“Even with the changes?”

“Especially after them.”

Mio smiled.

“Thank you.”

She closed the notebook.

For a while, they read quietly. The library was almost empty, and the afternoon light had moved from the window to the edge of their table.

When the closing announcement sounded, Mio put her things away.

“Would you like to walk with me?”

“To the station?”

“A little farther, if you don't mind.”

Itsuki looked at the time.

“I don't mind.”

They left the library and walked along the street behind the school. The road was quiet, with a few bicycles leaning against the fence and a shopkeeper arranging a display near the entrance.

“I have been thinking about something,” Mio said.

“What?”

She looked ahead.

“About what happens after graduation. I know where I am going. I know what I want to study. But I don't know how much of the routine we have now will remain.”

Itsuki walked beside her.

“We can still meet.”

“I know.”

“We can message.”

“I know that too.”

She smiled faintly.

“I don't think I'm asking about the practical part.”

He waited.

“I like having you in my ordinary days,” she said. “I don't want to assume that because we have become used to each other, it will simply continue without either of us choosing it.”

Itsuki looked at her.

He understood the question beneath the words. He also understood that she was not asking him to answer before he knew what he wanted.

“I don't want to leave it to habit either,” he said.

Mio nodded.

They reached the crossing. The signal was red.

“I should go,” she said.

“I'll walk you to the station.”

“All right.”

They crossed when the light changed.

At the entrance, Mio turned toward him.

“Thank you for reading it.”

“Thank you for showing me.”

She nodded and went down the stairs.

Itsuki remained by the entrance for a moment.

He had spent most of the year learning how to answer questions that other people asked. This one was different.

It was his to ask too.

The final week of class had begun. He still had something important to say, and this time he did not intend to leave it to chance.

Chapter 16 The Answer We Chose

Mio asked Itsuki to meet her on Thursday afternoon, the day before graduation.

She suggested the bookshop near the station, but when they arrived, the upper floor was crowded with students buying notebooks. They decided to walk instead.

The afternoon was clear. A light breeze moved along the shopping street, carrying the smell of bread from the bakery and the faint sound of traffic from the main road.

“I received a reply from the student journal,” Mio said.

Itsuki looked at her.

“Already?”

“I'm happy.”

Mio laughed quietly.

They reached a small park beside the shopping street. A few children were playing near the swings, and an older man was reading on a bench. Itsuki and Mio sat on the opposite side, leaving a comfortable distance between them.

“I have been thinking about what I said last week,” Mio began.

“About choosing what continues?”

She nodded.

“I don't want our friendship to become something we only keep because it is familiar. I want to keep seeing you because I choose to.”

“I want that too.”

Mio rested her hands on her bag.

“There is something else.”

“I have liked spending time with you for a long time. At first, I was curious about you because of the rumour. Then I wanted to understand the person who had been caught in it. After that, I kept finding reasons to talk to you even when there was nothing to investigate.”

“I think I started liking you somewhere along the way. I didn't know when it changed. I only knew that I wanted to tell you eventually.”

Itsuki listened without interrupting.

“I don't expect you to answer because I said it,” she continued. “I wanted to be honest. I don't want to keep hiding behind the fact that we're friends.”

He looked toward the playground. A child had dropped a small ball, and another had picked it up.

He thought about the first time he had seen Mio at the library, the notebook she had used to investigate the rumour, the bookshop afternoons, and the quiet conversations that had become part of his week. He thought about the times she had asked a question and waited for an answer rather than deciding what he must feel. He thought about the ways she had shown him her own uncertainty, her writing, and the things she wanted to try.

He had not been waiting for a perfect moment.

He had been waiting to understand what he wanted to say.

Mio looked at him.

“Not only because I got used to seeing you,” he continued. “I like talking with you. I like reading beside you. I like the things you notice, even when I don't understand why you're looking at something for so long.”

“What does that mean?”

“I want that too.”

“You do?”

“Yes.”

“All right.”

“That's your response?”

“I'm trying not to make it complicated.”

She smiled.

“I'm happy too.”

“At graduation.”

He had chosen something without knowing exactly how every part would work.

For once, that did not feel like a mistake.

Epilogue After the Ordinary

By the last week of March, the cherry trees along the road near Hoshimiya Station had begun to bloom.

Itsuki stood outside the apartment with a folder under one arm and a small paper bag in his hand. The folder contained the final Seiran enrolment documents. Itsuki: I'll be there in ten minutes.

“As a person.”

“That sounds difficult to edit.”

Mio smiled.

“I'll do my best.”

They sat together for a while. The shop was quiet, and the afternoon light moved slowly across the table.

After leaving, they walked toward the station. Mio had a meeting at Kiyose College the following week, and Itsuki had an orientation date at Seiran. Their schedules would change. The train routes would be different on some days. There would be new people, new assignments, and mornings when one of them would be too busy to reply immediately.

They had talked about that.

They had not promised that nothing would change.

At the crossing, Mio slipped her hand into his.

“Are you free next Saturday?” she asked.

“I think so.”

“I'd like to visit the library before the new term starts.”

“The one near the east station?”

“Yes. I want to look at the new shelves.”

“Then let's go.”

She smiled.

“I'll send you the time.”

“I'll write it down.”

They crossed together.

At the station, Mio's train arrived first. She stood beside the platform entrance, still holding his hand.

“I'll message you when I get home.”

“All right.”

“And you should eat something before you start reading.”

Itsuki looked at her.

“Nanami has been saying that.”

“Then she is probably right.”

“I was hoping you would disagree.”

“I don't think I will.”

He smiled.

Mio's train arrived. She let go of his hand and stepped toward the doors.

“See you soon.”

“See you.”

She boarded.

Itsuki waited until the train had left before turning toward the other platform.

His phone vibrated again. Nanami had sent a photograph of Professor Whiskers beside a new reading list. The paper cat's scarf had been replaced with a small blue bow.

Nanami: He has been promoted.

Itsuki: Congratulations to him.

Nanami: We might do another reading day in autumn. Don't make plans without checking.

Itsuki: I will consult the cat.

Nanami: He says you're available.

Itsuki put the phone away.

At home, he placed the university documents on the desk and the new notebook beside them. The calendar on the refrigerator had a few dates written in blue: orientation, Nanami's next reading project, and a day he had agreed to meet Mio.

There was still plenty of space.

He took the pen from its hook and wrote one more date.

Then he closed the calendar.

The school year had ended. The classroom, the library table, the walk to the station, and the familiar sound of the bell were no longer part of his daily schedule.

He would miss them.

But the people he had met there were not confined to those places, and the things he had learned did not have to stay behind.

His phone lit up with a new message.

Mio: I got home. Thank you for today.

Itsuki: Thank you too. I'll see you Saturday.

Mio: I look forward to it.

He set the phone beside the notebook.

Outside, a train passed through Hoshimiya, carrying people toward the next part of their day.

Itsuki opened the notebook Nanami had given him at graduation. The first page was blank.

He wrote the date at the top.
