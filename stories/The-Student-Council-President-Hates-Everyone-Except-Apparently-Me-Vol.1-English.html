PrologueThe Girl Who Hated Everyone

Hanako Shimizu hated three things.

Incompetence.

Unnecessary noise.

And boys.

In that order, although she occasionally rearranged the list depending on how irritating the boys in question happened to be.

At Seiran High School, where students changed into indoor shoes at the entrance and the student council maintained a form for nearly every complaint, Hanako's reputation travelled faster than the morning bell.

Unfortunately, the school had a great many boys. Most of them were noisy, several were incompetent, and almost all of them seemed to believe that being ignored by Hanako was an invitation to try harder.

As student council president, she had developed a reliable method for dealing with them.

She looked at them.

They stopped talking.

She looked at them again.

They apologized.

It was an efficient system.

The student council handbook did not officially authorize intimidation as an administrative technique. Hanako considered this a serious oversight.

Then Kaito Mizuno arrived.

He didn't stare at her. He didn't attempt a charming introduction. He didn't ask whether she had a boyfriend or offer to carry her books.

He simply entered the classroom, took the empty seat beside hers, placed his bag beneath the desk, and opened a book.

Hanako waited.

Nothing happened.

She adjusted her posture.

Still nothing.

Finally, she turned toward him.

"Do you know who I am?"

He looked up.

"Yes."

"And?"

"And you're sitting next to me."

Hanako stared at him.

He returned to his book.

Hanako's internal committee convened immediately. The chairperson was furious. The committee consisted entirely of Hanako.

For the first time in years, Hanako Shimizu had encountered a boy who appeared completely uninterested in her existence.

She decided she hated him.

It was a perfectly reasonable decision.

At least, that was what she told herself.

She didn't yet know about the coffee cans.

She didn't know about the hospital appointments, the medication tucked into his bag, or the quiet fear behind his habit of saying he was fine.

And Kaito didn't know that the girl beside him, who seemed determined to make his school life miserable, would eventually become the person whose footsteps he learned to recognize in a crowded hallway.

Neither of them knew how much trouble a single, unopened can of coffee could cause.

For now, they were just two students sharing a desk.

One hated everyone.

The other had become very good at making himself easy to overlook.

Neither was particularly good at being honest.

Chapter OneThe Boy Who Didn't Chase Her

"Question seven."

The history teacher tapped the board with a marker.

"Why did the alliance collapse?"

Silence.

A student near the window pretended to search his notebook. Someone in the back coughed. Hiro Takahashi, who had been drawing a dragon in the margin of his exercise book, looked around as if the answer might be hiding beneath somebody's chair.

Hanako raised her hand.

Then lowered it.

She knew the answer, obviously. She always knew the answer.

But the teacher was looking at Kaito.

"Would you like to try?"

Kaito looked up from his notes.

"The alliance didn't collapse because of one disagreement," he said. "The disagreements were already there. The final dispute just made it impossible to pretend they weren't."

The teacher paused.

"Go on."

Kaito explained the economic pressure, the conflicting objectives, and the way both sides had mistaken temporary cooperation for genuine trust.

It wasn't a dramatic answer. He didn't raise his voice or try to impress anyone.

He simply knew what he was talking about.

"Excellent," the teacher said.

A few students turned to look at him.

Hiro Takahashi slowly turned his dragon drawing toward Kaito, as if even the creature deserved to witness the historic event. The dragon had contributed more to the lesson than Hiro had, and everyone knew it.

Kaito returned to his notes.

Hanako's fingers tightened around her pen.

He could at least look pleased with himself.

After class, Hiro appeared beside Kaito's desk.

"Congratulations. You've officially made the rest of us look academically unemployed."

"I didn't know that was a competition."

"It is now. I'll be filing a complaint with the authorities."

"Which authorities?"

"The authorities of my imagination. They're very strict."

Kaito's mouth twitched.

Hiro noticed.

"Wait. Was that a smile? Someone record the date!"

"Don't."

"Too late. I'm telling your grandchildren."

Hanako closed her book with a sharp snap.

"Some people seem to have an impressive amount of free time."

Hiro turned toward her.

"Thank you. I cultivate it."

"I wasn't complimenting you."

"I choose to receive it as one."

"You're exhausting."

"And yet, here we are."

Hanako looked at Kaito.

"You should choose your friends more carefully."

Kaito put his notebook away.

"He's not as bad as you think."

Hiro stopped smiling for half a second.

It was subtle enough that Hanako almost missed it.

Then he recovered.

"See? My best friend believes in me."

"We've known each other for four days."

"And already our bond is unbreakable."

Kaito stood.

"You're still returning my pencil."

"Friendship is a complicated thing."

Hanako watched them leave.

She couldn't understand it.

Kaito barely spoke. He didn't seem especially interested in popularity, romance, or anything else that occupied the boys around him.

Yet he had defended Hiro without hesitation.

Not loudly. Not heroically.

He had simply said what he believed.

Hanako disliked that.

Mostly because she couldn't decide what to do with it.

Chapter TwoAn Extremely Unfortunate Lunch

The cafeteria was crowded.

Hanako hated crowded places.

She hated the noise, the careless elbows, the people who stopped walking in the middle of a path, and the students who somehow managed to turn standing in line into a competitive sport.

At Seiran, the lunch queue had three unofficial rules: keep moving, protect your tray, and never trust a student who said, 'I'm just saving a place for my friend.' That last rule had been written in Hanako's notebook after a particularly ugly incident involving croquettes.

She was carrying a tray when someone behind her stumbled.

A shoulder struck hers.

Her shoe slid.

For one brief, humiliating moment, the floor appeared to rise toward her.

Then a hand caught her arm.

"Careful."

Kaito.

Of course.

Hanako steadied herself and immediately pulled away.

"I had it under control."

"Naturally."

His expression was so neutral that she couldn't tell whether he was agreeing or making fun of her.

She chose the second option.

"Don't sound so pleased with yourself."

"I'm not."

"Then stop looking like that."

"Like what?"

"That."

Kaito glanced down at his own face, as though it might provide an explanation.

Hiro appeared beside them carrying enough food for three people.

"Are we having a fight? Excellent. I brought lunch and absolutely no useful advice."

"Go away," Hanako said.

"That's the nicest thing you've said to me today."

Kaito moved aside to let another student pass.

Hanako turned to leave.

Her tray tilted.

A carton of milk slid toward the edge.

Kaito caught it before it fell.

He placed it back on the tray.

"Second time."

Hanako's eyes narrowed.

"Would you like to say something?"

"Not particularly."

"Then don't."

"Okay."

He walked away.

Just like that.

No smug grin. No teasing remark. No attempt to turn the moment into an excuse to spend time with her.

Hanako stood in the middle of the cafeteria, gripping her tray.

Why does he keep doing that?

It was an irritating question.

An even more irritating question was why she wanted him to stay long enough to answer it.

That afternoon, she passed the classroom and heard voices inside.

Hiro was talking.

"You know she actually gets upset when you ignore her, right?"

Kaito answered without looking up.

"I don't ignore her."

"You absolutely do."

"I answer when she talks to me."

"That isn't the same thing."

A pause.

Then Kaito said, "I don't know what she wants from me."

Hiro lowered his voice.

"Maybe she doesn't know either."

Kaito closed his book.

"Whatever it is, I'm probably not the person she needs."

Hanako stopped outside the door.

She knew she should keep walking.

Instead, she listened.

Hiro sighed.

"You always do that."

"Do what?"

"Decide what other people need before they get a chance to tell you."

Neither spoke for a moment.

Then Kaito said, "I'm not interested in making her life more complicated."

Hanako's fingers curled around the strap of her bag.

She walked away before she could hear anything else.

By the time she reached the stairs, she had made a decision.

Kaito was not worth the effort.

If he didn't want her attention, he wouldn't get it.

It was an excellent decision.

She repeated it to herself all the way home.

Chapter ThreeAn Apology with Poor Timing

Three days passed.

Hanako didn't speak to Kaito.

She didn't look at him, either.

At least, not deliberately.

She merely happened to notice when he arrived late, when he rubbed his eyes during the last lesson, and when he left the classroom with his bag held a little closer than usual.

None of those observations meant anything.

She was student council president. Noticing things was part of her job.

Probably.

On Thursday afternoon, Kaito found her in the library.

"Shimizu?"

She kept reading.

"What?"

"Can I talk to you?"

"If you must."

He sat across from her.

For a moment, he appeared to be searching for the right words.

Then he said, "I'm sorry."

Hanako turned a page.

"For what?"

"For making you feel like I didn't want you around."

Her fingers stopped moving.

Kaito continued before she could respond.

"I don't know what you expected from me. I still don't. But I shouldn't have talked about you as if your feelings were an inconvenience."

Hanako looked at him properly for the first time in days.

He wasn't smiling.

He wasn't trying to charm his way out of trouble.

He looked uncomfortable, which was reassuringly familiar.

"That was a terrible apology," she said.

"I know."

"You didn't even explain yourself."

"I could explain. I just don't think it would make what I said better."

Hanako stared at him.

She had prepared several responses for an apology.

She hadn't prepared one for that.

"Fine," she said at last. "I accept."

Kaito nodded.

"Thank you."

He stood.

"Wait."

He stopped.

Hanako hated the fact that she had said that.

"Why did you apologize?"

"Because I was wrong."

"That's all?"

"What else would there be?"

She looked down at her book.

"Nothing. Go away."

He did.

Hanako watched him disappear between the shelves.

She had wanted him to argue. To defend himself. To say something that would give her a reason to remain angry.

Instead, he had apologized and left her alone.

It was becoming a pattern.

And she was beginning to suspect that her anger wasn't the only thing making her miserable.

Chapter FourOne Can of Coffee

The midterm results were posted on Monday.

Hanako scored ninety-six.

Kaito scored ninety-eight.

Hiro stared at the results board, then at Kaito, then at his own paper. 'I would like to appeal to the education system,' he announced. 'I have several concerns about the existence of people who study quietly.'

She stared at the list.

Then stared at it again.

Then checked whether the teacher had accidentally reversed their names.

He hadn't.

"Two points," she muttered.

Hiro leaned over her shoulder.

"Tragic. Should we organize a memorial service?"

"Do you want to die?"

"I'd prefer to postpone it until after lunch."

Kaito approached them.

"Congratulations," he said to Hanako. "That's a good score."

She turned slowly.

"Are you mocking me?"

"No."

"Are you gloating?"

"No."

"Then why are you smiling?"

"I'm not."

He wasn't.

That was the problem.

Hanako marched away.

The next morning, Kaito found a can of coffee on his desk.

He picked it up.

Black coffee. No sugar.

His usual choice.

He looked around the classroom.

Hiro was watching him with an expression of unbearable delight.

"Don't," Kaito said.

"I haven't said anything."

"You're about to."

"I was merely wondering whether mysterious beverages are a new school tradition."

Kaito turned the can in his hand.

There was no note.

He opened it.

"Well?" Hiro asked.

"Well, what?"

"Are you going to drink it?"

Kaito took a sip.

"Yes."

"That's it? No emotional speech?"

"It's coffee."

Hiro leaned back in his chair.

"Of course it is."

Across the room, Hanako pretended to organize papers.

She didn't look at Kaito.

Not once.

She did, however, notice when he finished the entire can.

That afternoon, she found herself in the library with him.

It wasn't intentional.

She had gone there to collect student council records. Kaito happened to be reading near the window.

He looked up when she approached.

"Hello."

"Don't sound surprised."

"I'm not."

She set the papers down.

The silence stretched.

Hanako could have left.

Instead, she asked, "Was the coffee all right?"

Kaito looked at her.

"What coffee?"

"The coffee on your desk."

"Oh."

He considered the question.

"It was good."

"Good."

Another silence.

Then Kaito added, "Thank you."

Hanako's face warmed.

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"All right."

"You should be more careful about accepting random things."

"I will."

"And don't assume it means anything."

"I won't."

He returned to his book.

Hanako's internal committee convened immediately. The chairperson was furious. The committee consisted entirely of Hanako.

Hanako stood there, furious.

He was supposed to ask who had left it.

He was supposed to wonder why.

He was supposed to make this difficult.

Instead, he had accepted the coffee, thanked her, and allowed her to leave without demanding anything in return.

She hated how easy he made it to care about him.

A week later, she left another can.

This time, he placed the empty one beside his notebook instead of throwing it away immediately.

Hanako noticed.

She pretended she didn't.

Chapter FiveThe Distance Between Two Desks

Cleaning duty was supposed to be simple.

Wipe the desks. Sweep the floor. Return the chairs.

Hanako had completed the first two tasks before Kaito managed to injure himself with a chair.

She stared at the chair. Then at Kaito. Then back at the chair. 'How did you lose a fight with furniture?' she asked. 'It was a surprise attack,' he said. 'It has four legs. It was not capable of surprise.'

Not seriously.

Not dramatically.

He had merely caught his shoulder against the edge of a desk while moving it.

Still, he went quiet.

Hanako noticed.

"What's wrong?"

"Nothing."

"That was a question, not an invitation to lie."

Kaito adjusted his sleeve.

"I hit my shoulder."

"I can see that."

"Then why did you ask?"

"Because apparently someone needs to explain how conversations work to you."

She put down her cloth and approached him.

"Let me see."

"It's fine."

"Kaito."

He hesitated.

That was unusual enough to worry her.

He pulled his sleeve back slightly. A bruise was already forming.

Hanako's expression changed.

Not dramatically.

She didn't gasp or start shouting.

She simply stopped looking annoyed.

"Does it hurt?"

"A little."

"Why didn't you say anything?"

"I didn't think it mattered."

Hanako stared at him.

Then she turned away and fetched the first-aid kit.

"You are unbelievably frustrating."

"I've heard that."

"From whom?"

"Hiro."

"At least he has good judgment."

She cleaned the small scrape near the bruise and applied a bandage.

Her hands were careful.

Kaito watched her work.

"You don't have to do this."

"I know."

"You seem angry."

"I am."

"At me?"

"Yes."

He considered that.

"Sorry."

Hanako pressed the bandage down a little more firmly than necessary.

He winced.

"Sorry," she repeated, this time more quietly.

Kaito looked at her.

For once, she didn't look away.

"You don't have to act like nothing matters," she said.

His expression became difficult to read.

"I don't."

"You do. You act as if you could disappear tomorrow and everyone would just continue with their day."

Kaito didn't answer.

Hanako regretted the words almost immediately.

She didn't know anything about his life.

Not really.

She knew his test scores. His preferred coffee. The fact that he didn't like noisy cafeterias.

She didn't know why he sometimes disappeared for medical appointments. She didn't know why he kept medication in his bag. She didn't know why he looked exhausted after climbing a flight of stairs on certain days and perfectly ordinary on others.

She knew almost nothing.

Yet she had the uncomfortable feeling that he had spent a long time trying to make sure people knew even less.

"Forget it," she muttered.

Kaito looked down at the bandage.

"Thank you."

Hanako packed the first-aid kit away.

"Go home when we're finished. And don't do anything stupid with that shoulder."

"Such as?"

"Anything that requires me to bandage you again."

"Understood."

She turned toward the door.

"Shimizu?"

"What?"

"I don't think I could disappear without anyone noticing."

Hanako stood still.

Kaito's voice remained quiet.

"I just don't always know what to do when someone does."

She had no answer.

For the first time since meeting him, she wished she could find the right words as easily as she found reasons to be angry.

Chapter SixThe Thing He Didn't Tell Her

The first time Hanako heard Kaito coughing in an empty classroom, she assumed he had caught a cold.

The second time, she wondered whether he was getting worse.

The third time, she stopped pretending not to notice.

"Kaito?"

He was sitting near the window, one hand resting against his chest. His breathing sounded strained, and he looked paler than usual.

He tried to straighten up when he saw her.

"Hi."

"Don't 'hi' me. What's wrong?"

"I'm having a difficult day."

"That isn't an answer."

"It's the one I have."

Hanako approached him.

"Do you need the nurse?"

He shook his head, then seemed to reconsider.

"Actually, could you get her?"

Hanako didn't argue.

She left immediately.

By the time she returned with the school nurse, Kaito was struggling to speak comfortably. The nurse assessed him, followed his existing medical emergency plan, and contacted the appropriate medical assistance.

Hanako stood back, frightened by how little she understood.

Kaito's bag was on the floor.

His medication pouch had fallen open.

She saw the inhaler inside and remembered the times he had quietly excused himself from class. The unexplained absences. The tiredness he waved away. The way he sometimes stopped halfway up the stairs and pretended to be checking his phone.

None of it had made sense to her before.

Now every small detail seemed to demand an explanation.

The nurse looked at Hanako.

"Could you give us some room, please?"

Hanako nodded.

She stepped into the corridor.

Her hands were shaking.

She hated that, too.

She hated being frightened when there was nothing she could do. She hated how easily the world could change while she stood in it, unable to bargain or threaten or demand a better outcome.

Most of all, she hated the memory of Kaito sitting across from her in the library, apologizing for making her feel unwanted.

He had been worried about inconveniencing her.

While carrying all of this.

When the medical team arrived, Hanako moved aside and let them work.

She did not try to be his doctor.

She did not try to make him smile.

She simply stayed in the corridor until Hiro arrived, breathless and unusually quiet.

"Where is he?"

Hanako pointed toward the room.

Hiro looked through the open doorway.

For once, he had nothing funny to say.

He sat beside Hanako.

Neither spoke for several minutes.

Finally, Hanako whispered, "Why didn't he tell me?"

Hiro stared at the floor.

"Because he thinks telling people makes it their problem."

"That's stupid."

"Yeah."

"He's stupid."

"Sometimes."

Hanako pressed her palms against her knees.

"I don't want him to be in trouble just because he thinks he has to hide everything."

Hiro looked at her.

"Then tell him that when he can hear you."

She nodded.

It was the only promise she could make.

Chapter SevenA Kiss Is Not a Cure

Kaito returned to school several days later, following medical advice and a recovery plan.

He looked tired.

Hanako noticed immediately.

She also noticed the way he hesitated before sitting down, as though he expected her to ask questions he didn't know how to answer.

She waited until class ended.

"Walk with me."

"Where?"

"Somewhere we can talk without Hiro providing a live commentary."

From the other side of the room, Hiro raised a hand.

"I heard that."

"Good," Hanako said.

Kaito followed her into the courtyard.

For a while, neither spoke.

Then Hanako turned toward him.

"You scared me."

Kaito looked down.

"I'm sorry."

"Stop apologizing as if that fixes everything."

He fell silent.

She took a breath.

"I don't need to know every detail of your medical history. I don't need you to tell me things you're not ready to talk about. But if you're in trouble, I want you to tell someone who can help."

He nodded.

"Okay."

"And don't say okay just to make me stop talking."

"I won't."

She studied his face.

"Do you understand?"

"Yes."

"Why didn't you tell me you were feeling unwell that day?"

Kaito rubbed the back of his neck.

"I thought I could handle it."

"And if you couldn't?"

He had no answer.

Hanako looked away.

"I don't know what we're supposed to be to each other yet," she said. "We're not even friends. Probably."

"Probably?"

"Don't interrupt."

"Sorry."

She gave him a sharp look.

He closed his mouth.

Hanako's cheeks warmed, but she forced herself to continue.

"I don't want you to think you have to pretend you're fine around me. That's all."

Kaito looked at her for a long moment.

"Why?"

The question was quiet.

Not challenging.

Not teasing.

He genuinely didn't understand.

Hanako's heart gave an uncomfortable little twist.

"Because I care, you idiot."

The words escaped before she could stop them.

Kaito went still.

Hanako's eyes widened.

"I mean—"

She stopped.

There was no useful explanation.

Kaito looked at the ground.

Then, very quietly, he said, "I don't know what to do with that."

Hanako's anger softened.

"You don't have to do anything."

"Just accept it?"

"Yes."

"That sounds difficult."

"It is. For me, too."

He looked up.

For a moment, neither of them moved.

Then Kaito took a small step closer.

Hanako didn't step away.

He hesitated, giving her time to change her mind.

She didn't.

Their first kiss was brief and awkward, more a question than an answer.

When they separated, both looked startled by what they had done.

Hanako touched her lips.

Kaito stared at her.

"That was—"

"Don't."

"I wasn't going to say anything."

"Good."

A pause.

Then Kaito said, "I wasn't expecting that."

Hanako's face turned red.

"Neither was I!"

He looked as if he wanted to smile but wasn't sure whether he was allowed.

She caught the expression.

"Don't you dare laugh."

"I'm not laughing."

"You are thinking about it."

"A little."

She hit his arm lightly with the back of her hand.

He laughed.

And for once, Hanako didn't feel the need to make him stop.

The kiss had not cured anything.

It hadn't changed his medical condition or erased the fear they had both experienced.

But it had changed one small thing.

For the first time, they had both admitted that the distance between them was not quite what it used to be.

Neither knew what came next.

For now, that was enough.

Chapter EightThe Empty Seat

Kaito was absent on Monday.

Then Tuesday.

By Wednesday, Hanako had checked the classroom door so many times that Hiro finally moved his chair into her line of sight.

'I'm not saying you're worried,' Hiro said, 'but if you stare at that door any harder, the school will charge you rent for the view.'

"You're doing it again."

"Doing what?"

"Looking at the door."

"I'm waiting for the teacher."

"The teacher is standing behind you."

Hanako turned.

The teacher was, indeed, standing behind her.

She closed her eyes.

Hiro pressed his lips together.

"Not one word," she warned.

"I would never."

"You are physically struggling not to."

"It's a medical condition."

"Get a real one."

He leaned back.

"Kaito told me he had a follow-up appointment. He said he'd be back when he could."

Hanako's shoulders relaxed slightly.

"Why didn't you tell me?"

"Because you didn't ask."

She stared at him.

Hiro raised both hands.

"Sorry. Bad joke."

"Does he have someone with him?"

"His family, probably."

"Probably?"

"I don't know every detail, Hanako."

It was the first time Hiro had called her by her first name.

She noticed.

He noticed that she noticed.

Neither commented on it.

Hanako looked toward Kaito's empty desk.

"I thought he was getting better."

"He might be. I don't know."

"I hate not knowing."

Hiro's expression became serious.

"Then you'll have to learn to live with it sometimes."

She looked at him sharply.

He didn't look away.

"You can care about someone without being able to control everything that happens to them," he said. "It doesn't make the caring pointless."

Hanako lowered her eyes.

For once, she had no argument.

That afternoon, she bought two cans of coffee.

She placed one on Kaito's desk.

The other remained in her bag.

She didn't know when he would return.

She didn't know whether he would be well enough to drink it.

She didn't even know whether he would want it.

But she had bought it anyway.

And for the first time, she understood that caring wasn't a transaction.

You didn't offer something because you were guaranteed a response.

Sometimes you did it because someone mattered.

Even when they weren't there to see it.

Chapter NineThirty-One Reasons to Be Angry

Kaito returned the following week.

He looked better than he had before his absence, though there were still shadows beneath his eyes.

Hanako waited until the classroom emptied.

Then she approached his desk.

"Are you feeling better?"

"Better than last week."

"That's not the same as well."

"No."

She appreciated the honesty.

She hated how much she appreciated it.

"Can I ask you something?"

"Sure."

"Why do you have so many medical appointments?"

Kaito's expression changed.

Not much.

Enough.

He looked at his hands.

"It's complicated."

"I figured."

He was quiet for so long that Hanako thought he might refuse to answer.

Then he said, "I have a chronic lung condition. I've had several major procedures, and I've spent more time in hospitals than I'd like to remember."

Hanako sat down beside him.

"How many?"

"Procedures?"

"Yes."

He hesitated.

"More than thirty significant interventions over the years, if you count everything."

She stared at him.

"Over the years?"

"Since I was very young."

"Kaito."

"I know."

"No, I don't think you do."

Her voice shook.

She hated that.

"You sit beside me every day. You make jokes with Hiro. You complain about homework. You drink terrible coffee. And you've been dealing with all of this while acting like the most inconvenient thing in your life is a missing pencil."

He looked down.

"I didn't want people to treat me differently."

"So you decided to make sure they couldn't understand you at all?"

He flinched.

Hanako immediately regretted the sharpness of her words.

She took a breath.

"I'm not angry because you're sick," she said more quietly. "I'm angry because you seem to believe that being honest about it would make you a burden."

Kaito didn't answer.

She continued.

"I can't promise I'll always know what to say. Sometimes I'll probably say something stupid. I already have."

"That's true."

She glared at him.

"Don't make jokes when I'm being sincere."

"Sorry."

"But I can listen. I can help you find someone who can help. I can sit with you when you're scared, if you want me there."

Her eyes burned.

"And if you don't want me there, you can tell me that, too. Just don't make the decision for me."

Kaito looked at her.

Something in his expression had changed.

Not relief exactly.

More like the exhaustion of maintaining a wall he had forgotten how to lower.

"I thought hiding it was kinder," he admitted.

"To whom?"

He had no answer.

Hanako looked at the coffee can on his desk.

"I don't want to be your doctor, Kaito. I don't want to be responsible for keeping you alive. That's not something I can promise, and I don't think it's fair to ask anyone to promise it."

He listened.

"But I want to be someone you can tell the truth to."

For a moment, he looked younger than seventeen.

Not helpless.

Just tired.

"I think I'd like that," he said.

Hanako nodded.

"Good."

She stood, then hesitated.

"One more thing."

"What?"

"If you ever call yourself a burden in front of me, I'm going to throw your coffee out the window."

He blinked.

"That's wasteful."

"Then don't do it."

He smiled.

It wasn't a large smile.

It was enough.

InterludeHiro Knows When to Stop Joking

Hiro had always believed that friendship was mostly about timing.

Knowing when to speak.

Knowing when to shut up.

Knowing when a joke would save someone from embarrassment and when it would only make them feel smaller.

With Kaito, he had spent years learning the difference.

They had met when they were younger, back when Kaito still believed that being sick was something he could outwork. He remembered hospital visits, cancelled plans, and messages that arrived hours later with some variation of Sorry, something came up.

At first, Hiro had thought Kaito simply didn't want to hang out.

Then he learned the truth.

After that, he became careful.

Not gentle. Kaito hated being treated as fragile.

Just careful.

He made jokes when Kaito needed to feel normal. He changed the subject when questions became too much. He never demanded explanations for every absence.

But there was a difference between respecting someone's privacy and helping them disappear.

Hiro had been slow to recognize it.

The day Kaito was taken to the hospital, Hiro had called his mother.

He hadn't intended to pry. He just needed to know whether Kaito was safe.

His mother answered after the second ring.

"He's being looked after," she said. "The doctors are monitoring him."

Hiro sat on the stairs outside the school office.

"Does he do this a lot?"

A pause.

"More than he tells people."

Hiro rubbed his face.

"Why doesn't he say anything?"

"Because he worries about everyone else. He thinks that if he hides the difficult parts, people won't have to worry."

"That sounds like him."

"It does."

Her voice softened.

"But worrying is not the same as being harmed. Sometimes people want the chance to care about you, even when they can't fix anything."

Hiro didn't have an answer.

After the call, he sat on the stairs for a long time.

He thought about every time Kaito had brushed off a question, every time he had joked instead of asking again.

He had believed he was respecting his friend's wishes.

Perhaps he had also been making it easier for Kaito to stay hidden.

When Kaito finally returned to school, Hiro didn't demand an explanation.

He simply walked beside him.

After a while, Kaito said, "You called my mother."

Hiro looked ahead.

"Yeah."

"Why?"

"Because I was worried."

"You could've asked me."

"You weren't exactly available."

Kaito was quiet.

Then he said, "You didn't have to do that."

Hiro stopped walking.

"Listen to me for a second."

Kaito turned.

"I'm not asking you to tell me everything. I'm not entitled to your medical records or your private thoughts. But if you disappear and I have a reason to think you're in trouble, I'm going to care. You don't get to forbid me from that."

Kaito looked startled.

Hiro sighed.

"I'm not trying to control you. I'm telling you that you're my friend. Those are different things."

For once, Kaito had no clever response.

Hiro started walking again.

After a few steps, Kaito followed.

"That was surprisingly well put," Kaito said.

Hiro looked offended.

"Surprisingly?"

"I was being sincere."

"Don't ruin it."

Kaito laughed.

And Hiro decided that, for the moment, the conversation had done what it needed to do.

Not fixed everything.

Just opened a door.

Chapter TenThe Truth About Breathing

A few days later, Kaito had a scheduled medical follow-up.

Hanako didn't ask to accompany him.

She wanted to.

She also understood that wanting something didn't automatically give her the right to it.

Instead, she sent a message.

Don't skip your appointment.

His reply came a minute later.

I wasn't planning to.

Good.

A pause.

Then another message appeared.

Would you like me to tell you how it went afterward?

Hanako stared at the screen.

She read the message twice.

Then she typed:

Only if you want to.

His response was immediate.

I do.

That evening, Kaito told her that his doctors had adjusted part of his treatment plan and emphasized the importance of following his prescribed instructions when symptoms worsened.

He didn't share every detail.

She didn't ask him to.

They sat together on a bench near the station, watching the evening commuters hurry past.

After a while, Kaito spoke.

"About the kiss."

Hanako nearly dropped her drink.

"Why are you bringing that up?"

"Because I think we should talk about it."

Her face warmed.

"Fine. Talk."

"I don't think it fixed anything."

She blinked.

"What?"

"The kiss. I don't think it helped my lungs. If I was feeling unwell, I needed medical help. Not a kiss."

Hanako looked down at her hands.

"Obviously."

"I wanted to be close to you. That's the honest answer."

She went very still.

Kaito continued, his gaze fixed on the road.

"I was nervous. I didn't know how to tell you what I was feeling. I still don't, sometimes."

He glanced at her.

"But I don't want you to think you have to rescue me. Or that being close to me means taking responsibility for things you can't control."

Hanako was silent.

She had imagined this conversation several times.

In none of those versions had Kaito been so direct.

"You're making it difficult to be angry with you," she muttered.

"I'm sorry."

"Stop apologizing."

"Right."

She stared at him.

Then, slowly, she held out her hand.

Kaito looked at it.

"Is this—"

"Don't make me explain everything."

He took her hand.

His fingers were warm.

Neither of them said anything for a while.

The train arrived.

They boarded together and found two seats beside the window.

Hanako didn't let go of his hand.

Kaito didn't ask her to.

Outside, the city slipped past in streaks of evening light.

For once, neither of them seemed to need a clever thing to say.

Chapter ElevenThe Debt That Didn't Exist

"You're taking me out on Saturday."

Kaito looked up from his notebook.

His pencil stopped moving. Hanako had the expression of someone issuing an executive order and expecting the universe to comply by Saturday.

"Am I?"

"Yes."

"Where?"

"I haven't decided."

"Then how do you know I'm taking you out?"

Hanako placed both hands on his desk.

"Because you owe me."

Kaito considered this.

"For what?"

"For worrying."

He blinked.

"That's not a debt."

"It is now."

"Is there a formal repayment schedule?"

"Saturday. Ten in the morning. Don't be late."

She turned and walked away before he could ask another question.

Hiro, who had been listening from the next desk, slowly rotated his chair.

"Congratulations."

Kaito sighed.

"What did I do?"

"Apparently, you have acquired a girlfriend who operates on an entirely different legal system."

"She's not my girlfriend."

Hiro stared at him.

Then he stared at the door through which Hanako had disappeared.

Then back at Kaito.

"Sure."

Saturday arrived with clear skies and a mild breeze.

Hanako had spent the previous evening choosing what to wear, then spent the morning insisting to herself that she hadn't spent the previous evening choosing what to wear.

Kaito arrived at the station at 9:55.

She was already there.

"You're early," she said.

"So are you."

"I was making sure you weren't late."

"Of course."

They walked through a shopping district, stopped at a bookstore, and eventually found themselves in a small café.

Hanako ordered tea.

Kaito ordered coffee.

She eyed his cup.

"You're predictable."

"You knew what I was going to order."

"I didn't say it was a bad thing."

He looked amused.

They talked about school, Hiro's latest ridiculous theory about the student council, and a book Kaito had been reading.

It was ordinary.

That was what surprised Hanako most.

She had expected the day to be awkward. She had prepared arguments, emergency topics, and several excuses for leaving early.

Instead, conversation came more easily than she had anticipated.

When they stepped outside again, she noticed Kaito had gone quiet.

"Are you tired?"

"A little."

"Do you want to sit down?"

He considered the question rather than dismissing it.

"Yes, actually."

They found a bench in a nearby park.

Hanako sat beside him.

She didn't tell him to stop being tired. She didn't accuse him of ruining the outing.

She simply sat with him until he was ready to continue.

Eventually, Kaito spoke.

"Thank you."

"For what?"

"Today."

She looked away.

"You haven't repaid your debt yet."

"I see."

"You'll have to take me out again."

"That sounds expensive."

"I'll choose the places."

"That sounds even more expensive."

She gave him a light shove.

He laughed.

Then his expression became thoughtful.

"Can I tell you something?"

Hanako nodded.

"I haven't had someone ask me whether I needed to sit down without making me feel like I'd failed at something."

She looked at him.

He wasn't asking for pity.

He was simply telling her something true.

"I don't always know how to handle my condition," he continued. "Some days are easier. Some aren't. I've spent a long time trying to act like the difficult days don't exist."

Hanako listened.

"I don't want to keep doing that," he said. "Not with you."

Her throat tightened.

"Good."

He glanced at her.

"That's all?"

"What else do you want me to say?"

"I don't know."

"Then don't make me invent a speech."

He smiled faintly.

She took a breath.

"For the record, you don't owe me for worrying about you."

Kaito looked at her.

"You said I did."

"I was lying."

"That's disappointing."

"You're welcome."

He laughed again.

Hanako looked at him, and the words she had been avoiding for weeks pressed against the back of her teeth.

She wasn't ready to say them.

Not yet.

But she was getting there.

And for the first time, the thought didn't frighten her quite as much as it used to.

Chapter TwelveStay With Me

Monday morning began with a broken pen.

Hanako had been trying to write a note for the student council when the pen stopped working.

She clicked it twice, as if the pen might respond to a formal warning. It did not. Hanako was now considering whether stationery could be disciplined for insubordination.

She pressed harder.

The tip snapped.

She stared at it.

Then she looked across the classroom.

Kaito was talking to Hiro.

He laughed at something Hiro said.

Hanako looked down at the broken pen again.

This is ridiculous.

She had been thinking about Saturday for two days.

Not the café. Not the walk. Not even the way Kaito had thanked her.

She kept remembering what he had said about wanting to stop pretending everything was fine.

And she kept thinking about how much she wanted to be part of the life he was trying to live honestly.

Not as his nurse.

Not as his rescuer.

Not as someone who had to be useful to deserve a place beside him.

Just as herself.

The realization was embarrassing.

It was also impossible to ignore.

During lunch, she found Kaito in the courtyard.

"Come with me."

He closed his book.

"Where?"

"Somewhere private."

His eyebrows rose.

Hanako's face warmed.

"Not like that! I mean somewhere we can talk!"

"I didn't say anything."

"You were thinking it."

"I wasn't."

"Liar."

She led him to the quiet side of the school building.

For several seconds, she stood with her arms folded, staring at a patch of pavement.

Kaito waited.

That was one of the most irritating things about him.

He could wait forever.

"Kaito."

"Yes?"

"I've been thinking."

"That sounds serious."

"It is."

He straightened slightly.

Hanako took a breath.

"I like you."

The words came out too quietly.

She cleared her throat.

"I said I like you."

"I heard you."

"Then why aren't you answering?"

"Because I don't want to interrupt."

She looked ready to throw something at him.

He wisely remained silent.

Hanako pressed her hands against her cheeks, then dropped them.

"I like you, and I don't know what I'm supposed to do about it. You're frustrating, and you hide things, and you make me worry, and you never react the way I expect you to."

Kaito listened.

"And I know I can't fix your problems. I know I'm not supposed to be responsible for your health, and I don't want you to feel like you have to pretend to be okay for me."

Her voice faltered.

She hated that it did.

"But I want to be there. I want to hear the truth, even when it's difficult. I want to sit with you when you don't feel like talking. I want to drink coffee with you and argue about stupid things and—"

She stopped.

Kaito's expression had softened.

That made everything worse.

Hanako looked away.

"So stay with me."

Silence.

She swallowed.

"Not just today. I mean—"

She squeezed her eyes shut.

"Let's be together."

The words were out.

There was no taking them back.

She opened her eyes.

Kaito was looking at her as if she had just handed him something precious and he was afraid of dropping it.

Hanako's face burned.

"Say something!"

He took a breath.

"I like you, too."

She stared at him.

"Then why are you making that face?"

"Because I didn't think you'd say it first."

"Why not?"

"You're usually threatening to throw things at me."

"That's not an answer!"

He laughed softly.

Then he grew serious.

"I want to be with you," he said. "But I need to learn how to let you in. I might get it wrong sometimes."

Hanako's anger dissolved into something quieter.

"So will I."

"I don't want you to feel responsible for me."

"I don't want to be responsible for you. I want to be with you."

Kaito nodded.

"I think I can do that."

She stared at him for another second.

Then she stepped forward and hugged him.

It was an awkward hug.

She held on too tightly. He hesitated before returning it. Neither knew exactly where to put their hands.

It was perfect.

Not because it was graceful.

Because neither of them tried to pretend it was anything other than what it was.

When they separated, Hanako looked at him.

"Can I kiss you?"

Kaito's expression changed.

He smiled.

"Yes."

Their second kiss was gentler than the first.

It didn't solve anything.

It didn't promise that every day would be easy.

It simply marked the beginning of something they had both chosen.

When they pulled apart, Hanako rested her forehead briefly against his.

"If you tell anyone about this, I'll deny everything."

"Understood."

"And stop smiling."

"I'm trying."

"Try harder."

He laughed.

She smiled despite herself.

Then she took his hand.

They walked back toward the classroom together.

Neither knew what the future would bring.

But this time, neither was pretending they had to face it alone.

EpilogueOne Can, Two Straws, and a Broken Pen

The first week of their relationship was surprisingly ordinary.

Kaito still forgot to bring his umbrella.

Hanako still complained about his handwriting.

She had started carrying a spare umbrella, of course. It was not for Kaito. It was for weather-related risk management. The fact that it was always the exact kind he preferred was a coincidence she refused to discuss.

Hiro discovered the truth within forty-eight hours and reacted as if he had personally orchestrated the entire romance.

"I knew it!"

Hanako pointed at him.

"You knew nothing."

"I knew everything."

"You thought we were going to get married because he lent me an eraser."

"It was a very meaningful eraser."

Kaito shook his head.

"I want a different friend."

"Denied," Hiro said. "Your application was rejected."

Some things changed, though.

Kaito began telling Hanako when he needed to rest instead of automatically insisting he was fine.

Sometimes he still struggled to say what he meant. Sometimes he withdrew when he was worried. Hanako occasionally mistook his silence for rejection, and he occasionally mistook her anger for a request to leave.

They argued.

They apologized.

They learned.

Neither became a perfect partner overnight.

One afternoon, Hanako arrived at Kaito's desk carrying two cans of coffee.

She placed one in front of him.

He looked at it.

"Is this another debt?"

"Maybe."

"What do I owe you this time?"

She sat beside him.

"Nothing."

He raised an eyebrow.

"Nothing?"

"Nothing. I just wanted to bring you one."

Kaito turned the can in his hands.

Then he opened his bag and took out a small box.

Hanako stared at it.

"What is that?"

"A collection."

"Of what?"

He opened it.

Inside were several empty coffee cans, a few folded scraps of paper, and one broken pen.

Hanako recognized the pen immediately.

It was the one she had snapped the morning she confessed.

Her face turned red.

"You kept that?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

He considered the question.

"Because I wanted to remember."

"Remember what?"

He looked at her.

"That you were scared, too."

Hanako had no response.

For once, she didn't try to invent one.

She reached for his hand beneath the desk.

He took it.

Across the classroom, Hiro noticed and began grinning.

Hanako lifted her free hand and pointed at him without turning around.

"Don't."

Hiro slowly lowered his head behind his notebook.

Kaito laughed.

Hanako looked at him.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"You're smiling."

"I am."

"Why?"

He squeezed her hand gently.

"Because I'm happy."

She looked away.

"That's a stupid reason."

"Probably."

She leaned her shoulder against his.

Outside, the school bell rang. Students filled the corridor. Someone shouted about a missing homework assignment. Hiro began loudly accusing an innocent classmate of stealing his pencil.

The world continued.

For a long time, Kaito had believed that the safest way to care about people was to keep the difficult parts of himself hidden.

Hanako had believed that caring meant finding a way to make people listen.

Neither belief had been entirely right.

He was learning that honesty could be an invitation rather than a burden.

She was learning that love didn't require her to solve every problem.

Sometimes it meant asking a question and accepting the answer.

Sometimes it meant staying.

And sometimes, it meant leaving a can of coffee on someone's desk without expecting anything in return.

Hanako glanced at the little box again.

"You're not keeping that forever."

"Why not?"

"It's embarrassing."

"That's why I'm keeping it."

She narrowed her eyes.

"I hate you."

Kaito smiled.

"No, you don't."

She opened her mouth.

Then closed it.

Finally, she leaned closer and murmured, "Don't make me regret liking you."

"I'll try not to."

"That's not reassuring."

"I'll do my best."

She rested her head against his shoulder.

For once, neither of them tried to fill the silence.

The coffee was cold.

The pen was broken.

And the girl who hated everyone had finally found someone she didn't need to chase.

Because he had chosen to stay.

AfterwordA Small Thing, Repeated

Some stories are remembered for grand gestures.

A dramatic confession in the rain. A promise made beneath fireworks. A person crossing an entire city because they cannot bear to leave something unsaid.

This story has those feelings, but its most important gestures are smaller.

A hand offered before someone falls.

An apology that doesn't demand forgiveness.

A question asked without assuming the answer.

A friend who knows when to stop joking.

A boy who begins to say, "I'm not feeling well," before someone else has to notice.

And a can of coffee placed on a desk.

Hanako never became a quiet person. Kaito never became someone who could explain every feeling perfectly. Their problems didn't disappear when they started dating, and affection didn't make them immune to the difficult parts of life.

What changed was their willingness to stop pretending.

She learned that she could care without controlling.

He learned that he could accept care without owing someone his entire life in return.

And somewhere between the coffee cans, the arguments, and the broken pens, they discovered that being understood didn't require being understood perfectly.

Sometimes it only required giving another person the chance to try.

That was the beginning of their story.

Not the moment they kissed.

Not the moment they confessed.

The moment they stopped deciding what the other person could handle—and started letting each other choose.

One can of coffee. One honest answer. One day at a time.

That was enough to begin.

— Kaito

Months later, when Kaito's mother visited, she paused over an old photograph tucked into the back of a drawer. For one brief second, her expression changed.

“Do you remember this?” she asked.

Kaito looked at the photograph. He recognised the place, but not the date written on its back—or the unfamiliar name beside his own.

“Should I?” he asked.

His mother closed the drawer. “Not tonight.”

He let the question go. There were things he still had not learned how to ask, and he had begun to believe that honesty meant giving difficult conversations time to arrive.

He did not know that the past he had never questioned was already on its way back.
