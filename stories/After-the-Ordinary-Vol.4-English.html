Prologue The Date We Kept

January arrived with a calendar that looked almost suspiciously clean.

Itsuki Kurose stood in front of the refrigerator on the morning of January 8, holding a mug of tea and studying the first page of the new year. December had been filled with school deadlines, the reading day, and several small reminders. January had one note written near the top.

January 9 — bookshop, Mio.

He had written it himself.

That still felt slightly unusual.

His winter-break assignments were stacked beside the low table. Most were finished. The remaining pages were not difficult, but they had been waiting long enough to acquire a personality.

Itsuki suspected it was an unpleasant one.

His phone vibrated.

Nanami: Are you awake?

Itsuki: No.

Nanami: Then who's answering?

Itsuki: The refrigerator.

Nanami: Tell it I said good morning.

He glanced at the blue fish magnet holding the calendar in place.

Itsuki: It says you're too loud.

Nanami: That's because it has excellent hearing.

He put the phone down and finished his tea.

The first day back at school was not especially eventful. Mr. Hasegawa reminded Class 3-B about the coming assessments, collected the winter-break assignment sheets, and returned a few forms that students had forgotten to sign. Riko complained about the amount of work as if the assignments had been delivered personally to inconvenience her.

“I thought winter break was supposed to be a break,” she said, dropping into the chair beside Itsuki's desk.

“It was.”

“Then why did they give us homework?”

“To make sure you remembered school existed.”

Riko looked at him.

“You could have kept that to yourself.”

“I could have.”

“And yet?”

“I didn't.”

She sighed and took out her workbook.

Ayane arrived with a folder tucked under one arm. She greeted them, asked Riko whether she had finished the assignments, and received a carefully noncommittal answer.

“Most of them,” Riko said.

“That's better than none,” Ayane replied.

“Don't encourage her,” Itsuki said.

“I'm encouraging progress.”

“That's how it starts.”

Riko pointed her pencil at him. “You see? This is why I need supportive friends.”

“You have them,” Ayane said.

“I need more.”

The bell rang before she could begin recruiting.

At the end of the day, Itsuki checked the message Mio had sent the previous evening. The bookshop event was still scheduled for the next afternoon. She had added a note that the organisers expected the first talk to begin at three, but the tables would be open earlier.

He replied that he would meet her outside the shop.

It was a small plan, no more complicated than choosing a train and arriving at the right time.

Still, as he walked toward the station, he found himself looking forward to it.

Winter break had ended. The school year had not.

For now, that was enough to think about.

Chapter 1 The Bookshop on the Ninth

On Saturday, January 9, the bookshop had placed a handwritten sign beside the entrance.

Winter Reading Afternoon — Second Floor.

Itsuki arrived seven minutes before the time he and Mio had agreed on. The street was quieter than it had been during the autumn event. Several shopfronts were still decorated with small New Year displays, and a paper lantern hung beside the bakery near the crossing.

Mio was standing outside the bookshop, reading the sign.

“Kurose.”

“Kanzaki.”

She folded the leaflet in her hands.

“You're early.”

“The train was on time.”

“I expected that answer.”

“Then I should find another one.”

“You don't have to.”

They went upstairs.

The event was smaller than the October gathering. Two local writers were scheduled to speak, and a handful of independent publishers had brought books to display. The room smelled faintly of paper and coffee. A heater stood near the back wall, making a quiet clicking sound every few minutes.

Mio moved toward a table of short-story collections.

“I wanted to look at these first,” she said. “The organiser said one of the writers edits a small literary magazine.”

“Are you interested in the magazine?”

“A little. I want to see how they choose the stories.”

Itsuki looked over the display. Several books had handwritten cards beside them, each with a short description and a note from the editor.

“You could ask during the talk.”

“I might.”

“You usually do.”

Mio glanced at him. “Not always.”

“You asked about quiet scenes last time.”

“That was different.”

“How?”

She considered the question.

“I already knew what I wanted to ask.”

“That's generally how questions work.”

“Not for everyone.”

They browsed separately for a few minutes, occasionally showing each other a title. Mio found a thin collection of essays about editing and bought it. Itsuki chose a book of translated short fiction, mostly because the first page had caught his attention.

When the talk began, the editor spoke about receiving submissions, choosing a sequence for an issue, and deciding when a piece was ready to be read by someone else.

“Sometimes a writer knows what they intended,” she said, “but the reader only has the words on the page. The editor's job is not to replace the writer's intention. It's to help the work communicate what is already there.”

Itsuki wrote the sentence in the margin of the leaflet.

Mio noticed.

“You liked that?”

“It made sense.”

“You usually say that when you agree with something.”

“It's an efficient phrase.”

After the talk, Mio asked about the difference between editing for clarity and editing for style. The speaker answered with an example from a short story, explaining how a small change could make a character sound more certain than the writer intended.

Mio listened closely.

Itsuki watched the speaker's hands move as she explained. He was not sure he wanted to work with words for a living. He liked reading them. He liked noticing how they fitted together. Whether he would enjoy doing that for eight hours a day was a different question.

Outside, the afternoon had grown colder.

They walked toward the station, carrying their books in paper bags.

“Did you find what you wanted?” Itsuki asked.

“I found something close.”

“That's usually how it goes.”

“I also got the magazine editor's name. I want to look up the publication.”

“You have a plan.”

“A small one.”

They reached the crossing.

Mio looked at the book in his bag.

“What did you choose?”

“A collection of translated stories.”

“Why that one?”

“The first story started with a person waiting for a train that had already left.”

“That sounds inconvenient.”

“It was.”

“Did the person know?”

“Not at first.”

Mio looked at him for a moment.

“I hope the story is better than that sounds.”

“I'll tell you.”

Her train was due first. She checked the time, then turned toward the station entrance.

“Thank you for coming.”

“I wanted to.”

Mio paused slightly.

Then she nodded.

“I'm glad.”

She went down the stairs.

Itsuki stood beside the crossing until the signal changed. He had spent an afternoon reading, listening, and talking about books. Nothing unusual had happened.

He was beginning to appreciate how much could fit inside an ordinary day.

Chapter 2 A Morning for Questions

The school guidance office had not changed during winter break.

Itsuki visited on Tuesday, January 12, carrying the folder Ms. Shibata had given him in December. The office smelled faintly of printer paper and tea. A stack of university brochures stood beside the desk, and a calendar on the wall had several information sessions marked in red.

Ms. Shibata looked up when he entered.

“Kurose. Welcome back.”

“Thank you.”

“You wanted to follow up on the programmes we discussed?”

“Yes. I looked through the course outlines.”

He placed two brochures on the desk. One described a language programme at Seiran University. The other covered a broader humanities course at a different institution.

Ms. Shibata opened the first.

“What caught your attention?”

“The English courses. Also the classes about communication and writing. I like reading, but I don't know whether I want to study literature by itself.”

“That's useful to know.”

She turned to the course list.

“This programme has a language foundation, then lets students choose several areas of study. You could take writing, cultural studies, or communication electives.”

“Would I have to decide the specialisation immediately?”

“No. You would have required subjects first. The more specific choices come later.”

Itsuki read the page.

He had expected the brochures to make the answer obvious. Instead, they had given him more questions.

“I don't know what job I would want afterward,” he said.

Ms. Shibata folded her hands.

“You don't need to turn an interest into a job title before you understand it. But you should consider what kind of study you are willing to continue. What subjects would you keep working on even when they become difficult?”

“English, probably.”

“Anything else?”

“Writing. Maybe editing. I don't know enough about either to say.”

“Then learn more about them.”

She showed him the dates for a programme consultation and suggested that he speak with a student representative. It would not be a full open campus, but the university had arranged a winter information day for prospective applicants.

Itsuki wrote the date in his notebook.

“I can attend that.”

“Good. You should also compare the entry requirements and costs. Interest matters, but so do practical details.”

“I understand.”

“And speak with your family about the options. You do not have to let them choose for you, but you should not make the decision without considering the support you have.”

Itsuki nodded.

He glanced at the tuition section of the brochure.

“Could I ask one more thing? How should I compare the costs? The course fees are listed, but there are textbooks, travel, and other expenses.”

Ms. Shibata turned the brochure toward him.

“Start with the full yearly estimate. Then ask the admissions office about scholarships and support programmes. Don't assume you are ineligible, but don't build your plan around money you haven't been offered.”

“I see.”

“You should also speak with the adults who are helping support you. That conversation may be uncomfortable, but practical questions are easier to handle when everyone knows what they are.”

Itsuki nodded again.

He had not considered that choosing a course would involve more than deciding which subjects interested him. The cost was not a reason to abandon the idea. It was a part of the idea he needed to understand.

He thanked her and left.

Outside the office, the corridor was busy. Students were returning borrowed equipment, and a teacher was asking someone to move a stack of chairs away from the stairwell.

Riko appeared near the notice board.

“Guidance?”

“Yes.”

“Did they give you a future?”

“A date.”

“That's disappointing.”

“I thought so too.”

She leaned against the wall.

“I have to talk to Mr. Hasegawa about my mathematics support plan.”

“How's that going?”

“Better. I still don't like the subject.”

“You don't have to like it.”

“You and that sentence.”

“It's reliable.”

Riko looked at the folder under his arm.

“You really are thinking about university.”

“I'm looking at it.”

“That's more than you were doing in September.”

“Apparently.”

She nodded.

“Good. I hope you find something you don't hate.”

“That's a generous wish.”

“I'm a generous person.”

“You charged me for half a biscuit last week.”

“That was business.”

They walked toward the classroom.

At lunch, Itsuki found Mio at the library window table. She had a new notebook open and a small list of course names written on the first page.

“You started yours,” he said.

“I had the information already.”

“Writing and editing?”

“Yes. I want to compare programmes that include publishing or language work.”

Itsuki placed his folder on the table.

“I have a university information day next month.”

“Which one?”

“Seiran. The English and humanities programme.”

“That sounds interesting.”

“I haven't decided whether it suits me.”

Mio looked at the brochure.

“You can find out when you go.”

“That's what Ms. Shibata said.”

“Then she is probably right.”

Itsuki sat down.

They opened their books.

For the first time, the question of what came after school had a date attached to it, a place to visit, and a list of things he wanted to ask.

It was still uncertain.

It was no longer entirely distant.

Interlude I A Different Kind of Weekend

Nanami Kurose had three messages waiting when she arrived at Hoshimiya East High School.

The first was from Yui, who had sent a photograph of a notebook covered in small sketches. The second was from the teacher supervising the library committee, asking whether Nanami could stay after class to discuss the spring reading day. The third was from her father.

Dad: Would you like to have dinner with us on Sunday? Akemi found a place near the river that you might like.

Nanami read the message while walking toward the shoe lockers.

“You're going to walk into someone,” Yui said.

Nanami looked up.

“I wasn't.”

“You were looking at your phone.”

“I can do two things.”

“You nearly walked into a sign.”

“The sign moved.”

Yui laughed.

They changed their shoes and went upstairs. The library committee had been discussing whether to organise another reading day in spring. The previous event had gone well, and the community library had offered to host a smaller follow-up activity if the students wanted to continue.

Nanami liked the idea.

She also knew the work would be different this time. The winter event had required a lot of coordination, and she had spent too much time trying to make every part of it run exactly as planned.

“We could do something smaller,” Yui said during their meeting.

“A reading corner and a book exchange?”

“Maybe. We don't need the full local-history display again.”

Nanami checked the notes.

“We could invite the younger students to bring one book they liked and recommend it.”

“That sounds fun.”

“And we could have a quiet table for people who don't want to read aloud.”

Yui smiled.

“You already have a plan.”

“A rough one.”

“That's a plan.”

Nanami wrote down the idea and left the date blank.

After class, she checked her father's message again. She wanted to see him. She also wanted to know whether Akemi would be there, and whether the evening would turn into another occasion where she had to work out how to fit herself into a routine that had changed without asking her first.

She typed a reply.

Nanami: Sunday is okay. Could I have some time with you before dinner? Just the two of us?

She read it once, then sent it.

Her father replied a few minutes later.

Dad: Of course. We can meet for lunch first. Dinner can be with Akemi if you're still comfortable with that.

Nanami felt her shoulders ease.

Nanami: That's fine. Thank you.

She put her phone away.

Yui looked at her.

“Everything okay?”

“Yes.”

“You look relieved.”

“I asked for something.”

Yui waited.

“And he said yes.”

“That's usually how asking works.”

Nanami gave her a look.

“You sound like my brother.”

“That's unfortunate.”

They laughed and packed their books.

Nanami left school with the spring reading-day notes in her bag and a Sunday plan that felt a little more comfortable than the one she had first been offered.

She had not solved anything.

She had simply said what she wanted.

Chapter 3 A Table That Had Changed

Nanami met her father outside the restaurant near the river shortly before noon on Sunday.

He was standing beside the entrance with his coat buttoned and his phone in one hand. When he saw her, he put it away.

“Nanami.”

“Dad.”

He smiled.

“You look cold.”

“It's January.”

“That's true.”

“You asked me to come outside.”

“I thought the walk would be pleasant.”

Nanami looked at the grey sky.

“You have an interesting definition of pleasant.”

Her father laughed.

They went inside.

The restaurant was small, with windows facing the river path. It was not crowded, and the staff showed them to a table near the glass. Nanami ordered a warm meal and tea. Her father chose soba and coffee.

For the first few minutes, they talked about school.

Nanami told him about Yui's plan for the spring reading day. Her father asked whether the community library had confirmed the date. She said they were still discussing it.

“You enjoyed the winter event,” he said.

“I did.”

“You looked happy when I saw you there.”

“I was.”

He nodded.

“I'm glad.”

Nanami stirred her tea.

“I wanted to ask you something.”

“Of course.”

“Could we sometimes do things just the two of us? Not every time I visit. I don't mind having dinner with Akemi. I just don't want to feel as if I have to fit into every plan that already exists.”

Her father did not answer immediately.

He looked at the table, then back at her.

“I didn't realise it had started to feel that way.”

“I don't think anyone meant it to.”

“That doesn't mean I shouldn't have noticed.”

Nanami looked down at her cup.

“I like Akemi. She's kind. I just don't know what I'm supposed to call her, or whether I'm supposed to act as if she's part of the family now.”

“You don't have to call her anything other than her name,” he said. “And you don't have to decide what she means to you. She is someone I care about. She is not here to replace anyone.”

Nanami nodded.

“I know.”

“Do you?”

She thought about it.

“I know it in my head. Sometimes the rest of me needs longer.”

Her father smiled gently.

“That's all right.”

They ate.

After lunch, he asked whether she wanted to walk along the river before meeting Akemi for dinner. Nanami agreed. They spent half an hour looking at the small shops near the bridge and discussing a book she had borrowed from the library.

When they met Akemi later, the evening was ordinary. Akemi asked about the reading project and listened while Nanami explained the smaller plan for spring. She did not ask whether Nanami had enjoyed lunch alone with her father, and she did not try to turn the dinner into a family occasion.

“The quiet reading table sounds like a good idea,” Akemi said.

“Some children don't like reading aloud.”

“I didn't when I was younger.”

Nanami looked at her.

“Really?”

Akemi nodded.

“I liked books. I didn't like being asked to read in front of everyone.”

Nanami smiled.

“Then I'll tell Yui we have another reason to keep it.”

Akemi laughed.

Nothing about the evening felt like a test.

When Nanami left, her father walked her to the station.

“Thank you for telling me what you wanted,” he said.

“I wasn't sure how to say it.”

“You said it clearly.”

She smiled.

“I'll come again.”

“Any time.”

Nanami went down the stairs.

She still missed the old arrangement. She also liked parts of the new one. The two feelings did not cancel each other out.

For now, she could make room for both.

Chapter 4 The Form with More Than One Answer

Akari Fujisaki had placed three university brochures on her desk and was trying to decide which one to read first.

The communications programme at Seiran University was still at the top of her list. She liked the practical projects, the writing courses, and the possibility of working with local organisations. The business programme her parents had first suggested remained interesting, but it no longer felt like the only sensible choice.

Her mother knocked on the open door.

“Are you working on the questionnaire?”

“Yes.”

“Can I come in?”

“Of course.”

Her mother sat on the chair beside the desk and looked at the brochures.

“You have the communications course first.”

“I think so.”

“You still want to compare the business programme?”

“Yes. I don't want to choose only because one feels familiar.”

Her mother nodded.

“That's sensible.”

Akari turned the questionnaire around.

“I want to put communications as my first preference. I still want to look at the required subjects and the practical placement options, but I think that's what I want to study.”

Her mother read the line.

“Then write it.”

Akari hesitated.

“You're not disappointed?”

“Why would I be?”

“Because Dad thought business might suit me.”

“Your father thought it was worth considering. He didn't ask you to choose it.”

Akari looked at the brochures.

“I know. I just wanted to be sure.”

Her mother touched the edge of the paper.

“You can take advice seriously without treating it as an instruction.”

Akari smiled.

“That's another good sentence.”

“You should start keeping a list.”

“I already have one.”

Her mother laughed.

Akari wrote Communications — first preference, subject to final course review in the first box. She left the second box blank until she had checked the remaining details.

Her father came home while she was putting the brochures back into their folder. He paused at the open door.

“Still working on the courses?”

“I think I've decided what to put first.”

He came in and looked at the form.

“Communications.”

“Yes. I want to study how people share information, how organisations reach people, and how local projects are planned. I like the practical side of it.”

Her father sat on the edge of the bed.

“I thought business might suit you because you are good at organising people. I didn't mean that you had to choose it.”

“I know. I just didn't want to disappoint you.”

He looked at her for a moment.

“I would rather you choose a course you are interested in than choose one because you think it will make me happy.”

Akari smiled a little.

“You both make it sound easy.”

“The choice is yours. The paperwork may still be annoying.”

“That part is definitely true.”

Her father picked up the brochure and looked through the course outline. He asked about the practical placement and the required subjects, then suggested that they compare the tuition and travel costs together before she submitted the final application.

“I can do that,” Akari said.

“Good. You don't have to do every part alone.”

She nodded.

When he left the room, Akari returned the brochures to the folder. The first preference was still written in pencil, but it no longer felt like a guess.

At school the next day, Saki noticed the brochure in her folder.

“You chose?”

“I chose what I want to investigate first.”

“That sounds like you.”

Akari smiled.

“I think I'm going to put communications first.”

“Seiran?”

“Yes.”

“That's good.”

“I'm still checking the course details.”

“You can do that after choosing the direction.”

Akari nodded.

At lunch, she found Itsuki near the library entrance.

“Kurose-kun.”

He turned.

“Fujisaki.”

“Do you have a minute?”

“Sure.”

She showed him the brochure.

“I'm leaning toward communications at Seiran. I wanted to tell someone who knows how long I've been looking at these forms.”

“That's a lot of forms.”

“It is.”

“Are you happy with the choice?”

Akari considered the question.

“I'm happy with the direction. I still need to compare the details.”

“Then it sounds like a good choice.”

She smiled.

“Thank you.”

“You did the research.”

“You can still say you're glad for me.”

“I'm glad for you.”

Akari looked satisfied.

“There. That wasn't difficult.”

“I had to think about it.”

She laughed.

“I'll send you the course information when I finish comparing it.”

“All right.”

She returned to her friends.

Itsuki went into the library.

Akari had not asked him to decide anything. She had simply told him about a decision she was beginning to own.

He was glad she had.

Chapter 5 A Seat at the Fujimotos' Table

Ayane invited Itsuki to dinner on a Saturday evening.

Her message was short. Her mother had made too much food, and her father had asked whether Itsuki was free. It was not an unusual invitation. Their families had been close for years, and there had been a time when Itsuki could walk into the Fujimoto house without knocking.

He still knocked.

Ayane opened the door.

“You're early.”

“Your message said seven.”

“It's six fifty-eight.”

“A serious violation.”

She stepped aside, smiling.

“Come in.”

The house smelled of grilled fish and miso soup. Ayane's mother greeted him from the kitchen, and her father called hello from the dining room. The welcome was familiar enough that Itsuki found himself relaxing before he had taken off his coat.

“You have grown taller,” Ayane's mother said.

“I think that happened a while ago.”

“It has been a while since you came for dinner.”

“That's true.”

Ayane took his coat and hung it beside hers.

“Don't let her start comparing you to the height marks on the doorframe.”

“I wasn't going to,” her mother called.

“You were thinking about it.”

Her father laughed from the dining room.

They sat down together. The meal was ordinary: rice, soup, grilled fish, and several side dishes. Ayane's father asked about school and the guidance session. Itsuki explained that he was looking at English and communication programmes, though he had not settled on a course yet.

“That sounds like a good place to start,” Ayane's mother said.

“I'm still comparing the practical details.”

“You always did like English,” Ayane's father said. “You used to correct the labels on the board games.”

Itsuki looked at him.

“I don't remember that.”

Ayane smiled.

“You corrected mine.”

“It was wrong.”

“I was eight.”

“The rules were still written in English.”

“You were very strict.”

“I was helpful.”

Her father laughed.

“You two used to argue about the rules for half an hour.”

“We still do,” Ayane said.

Itsuki considered that.

“The subject has changed.”

“The method hasn't.”

After dinner, Ayane brought out a folder. It contained information about courses related to urban studies, community development, and public administration.

“I've been reading these,” she said. “I thought I wanted to study local history, but I'm more interested in what happens to a neighbourhood as it changes.”

Itsuki looked through the course descriptions.

“You want to work on planning?”

“Maybe. I like the history, but I don't want to spend all my time looking backward. I want to understand how people decide what a place needs now.”

Her father nodded.

“She has been asking the neighbourhood association about their projects.”

Ayane gave him a look.

“You don't have to announce everything.”

“I'm proud of you.”

She looked away, embarrassed.

Itsuki turned another page.

“This programme has a fieldwork component.”

“That's the one I'm interested in.”

“Have you checked the requirements?”

“I have. I still need to compare the universities.”

“Then you're doing the same thing I am.”

Ayane smiled.

“Apparently.”

Her mother brought tea and sat with them for a few minutes. She asked whether Itsuki had been to the bakery near the station lately. Ayane said they had gone in October, and her mother looked pleased.

“You used to spend entire afternoons there,” she said.

“We were children,” Ayane replied.

“You were also very particular about which table you wanted.”

Itsuki remembered the window seat, though he could not recall why it had mattered.

“It had the best view,” he said.

Ayane looked at him.

“You remember that?”

“I remember the table.”

She smiled, but did not ask him to remember anything else.

When Itsuki left, Ayane walked him to the gate.

“Thanks for coming.”

“Your mother made enough food for three more people.”

“She always does.”

They stood beside the gate for a moment.

“It was nice having you there,” Ayane said. “Not because you used to come here. Just because you came today.”

Itsuki nodded.

“I enjoyed it.”

“Good.”

She went back inside.

Itsuki walked toward the station. The house had been familiar, but he had not felt as if he had stepped into an old photograph. They had talked about courses, school, and ordinary things. Ayane had shown him the person she was becoming, and he had been able to do the same.

That was better than trying to remember every detail of who they had been.

Chapter 6 The Work That Stayed

Riko's first mathematics assessment after winter break was not a disaster.

It was also not the result she had hoped for.

She looked at the paper during lunch, then folded it once and put it inside her workbook.

Itsuki noticed.

“How was it?”

“Fine.”

“That's not a score.”

“It's a feeling.”

He waited.

Riko took the paper out again.

“I improved a little. I still made mistakes on the questions with more than one condition.”

“Did you understand where?”

“Mostly. I rushed the second step.”

Itsuki nodded.

“Then you know what to practise.”

“I was hoping the paper would magically become easier.”

“Did it?”

“No.”

“Unfortunate.”

She laughed, though the sound was smaller than usual.

“Mr. Hasegawa wants me to continue the support sessions.”

“Are you going to?”

“Yes. I don't want to lose the routine.”

“Good.”

Riko looked at the paper again.

“I thought I'd be further along by now.”

Itsuki considered his answer.

“You are further along than you were.”

“That's not the same as being finished.”

“No.”

She folded the paper carefully.

“I know. I just wanted to complain.”

“You can do that.”

“Thank you for granting permission.”

They returned to their lunches.

After school, Riko went to the support classroom. She had brought her workbook and the marked assessment. Mr. Hasegawa asked her to choose two questions she had found difficult and explain where her reasoning had changed.

Riko worked through the first one.

She identified the correct values, set up the equation, and then stopped at the second condition.

“I treated the remaining amount as the original amount,” she said.

“Why?”

“I saw the number and used it before checking what it referred to.”

Mr. Hasegawa nodded.

“That's a useful mistake to understand.”

Riko corrected the calculation.

“I still don't like this.”

“You don't have to enjoy every subject.”

She glanced toward the door.

“Kurose said that too.”

“Then he has good advice occasionally.”

Riko smiled.

“Occasionally.”

When the session ended, she packed her things and checked the schedule on her phone. She had two more support sessions before the next assessment, and she had promised her parents she would show them the completed work on Sunday.

At home, her mother asked how it had gone.

“I found the mistake.”

“Good.”

“I still got the question wrong on the assessment.”

“That part can't be changed. What will you do with the next one?”

Riko took out her workbook.

“Practise reading the conditions before I calculate.”

Her mother nodded.

“Then keep doing that.”

There was no lecture. Her father asked whether she wanted to review the question together after dinner, and Riko accepted.

She was still not comfortable showing them every part of her school life. But she had begun to understand that hiding a difficulty did not make it smaller.

Later, she sent Itsuki a message.

Chapter 10 The Question He Could Not Leave Alone

Itsuki looked at the table.

“Why didn't you tell me?”

There was no argument in the room.

Itsuki did not answer.

Itsuki looked at him.

“I am.”

The older man nodded.

“I know.”

Itsuki nodded.

“I'll think about it.”

Outside, the afternoon was cold and clear.

He walked toward the station without checking his phone.

The answer had been direct.

Everything around it was not.

“Then keep that question on the page. You don't need to invent an answer.”

Riko leaned back when the exercise ended.

“I wrote three questions.”

“About your future?” Ayane asked.

“One about my future, one about the reading day, and one about why Mr. Hasegawa thinks we enjoy paperwork.”

Akari laughed.

“That last one might remain unanswered.”

“Then I have a mystery to solve.”

Itsuki looked at the page in front of him. The question about work remained unanswered, but he had not left the space blank.

That was something.

After classes, Itsuki visited Ms. Shibata to discuss the Seiran programme. He had checked the admission requirements, tuition information, and available support. He still needed to complete the application process, but he had decided that he wanted to pursue the English and communication course.

“You seem clearer than you did in December,” Ms. Shibata said.

“I know what I want to study now.”

“And what do you want to do afterward?”

Itsuki considered the question.

“I don't know yet.”

She nodded.

“That's honest. You can explore career options while you study. The important thing is that you have chosen a direction for a reason.”

“I like the subjects. I want to learn more about writing and communication. I think I would regret not trying.”

Ms. Shibata smiled.

“That's a good reason.”

He thanked her and left the office.

At the library, Mio was waiting near the window table.

“How did it go?”

“I decided.”

“Seiran?”

“I'm glad.”

“I'm still comparing. I found one that includes publishing, but I want to ask about the practical work.”

At the end of the day, he walked home with the university brochure inside his bag. The application details still needed attention. There would be more forms, more questions, and probably at least one conversation he would rather avoid.

He had not solved everything.

He had chosen what to do next.

That was enough for February.

Epilogue The Distance Between Yesterday and Tomorrow

On the evening of February 28, Itsuki opened the calendar on his refrigerator.

January had been filled with school, the bookshop event, guidance, and a few visits. February had more marks than he remembered adding: the university information day, a meeting with Ms. Shibata, a lunch with Akari, and several library afternoons.

March was mostly empty.

Not completely.

Nanami had written the spring reading day in blue. Ayane had sent him the date of the next community planning session. Mio had asked whether he wanted to visit the bookshop after the school paperwork was finished. Riko had mentioned a study session and then insisted it would not involve a workbook.

Akari had sent the final course details she had found.

Itsuki had answered all of them.

He took the pen from its hook and added one more note.

Seiran — application details.

He looked at the words.

They did not describe the rest of his life. They did not promise that the course would be perfect or that he would know what job he wanted afterward.

They described a choice he had made after looking at the options.

That was enough.

His phone vibrated.

Mom: I hope you're doing well. I won't ask to meet. I just wanted to wish you luck with your school decisions.

Itsuki read the message.

He did not feel ready to meet her. He did not feel that he had stopped caring. The two facts remained where they were.

He typed:

Itsuki: Thank you. I'll let you know when I'm ready to talk.

He sent it.

A reply arrived a little later.

Mom: I understand. Take your time.

Itsuki put the phone down.

He went to the kitchen and began preparing dinner. The rice cooker clicked. The refrigerator hummed. Outside, someone was walking up the stairs, and a door closed somewhere down the corridor.

The ordinary sounds remained.

He thought about the photographs in the envelope on his shelf. The people in them had been happy on some days and distant on others. The marriage had ended. The explanation he had been given had been false. None of those facts erased the afternoons he remembered, the man who had helped him with schoolwork, or the mother who still wanted to speak to him.

He did not know what their relationship would become.

He did know that he did not have to decide it tonight.

Tomorrow would be March.

The final month of the school year was waiting, with its forms, its last routines, and the things they had not yet finished saying.

Itsuki turned off the kitchen light and carried his dinner to the table.

The distance between yesterday and tomorrow had not disappeared.

For the first time, he was no longer waiting for someone else to cross it for him.
