Prologue A Date Already Written

October arrived on a Thursday.

Itsuki Kurose knew this because the first thing he saw after waking was the calendar on his refrigerator, where September had ended with a small collection of blue and black marks. The last Wednesday of the month had been ordinary enough: school, a message from Riko about a workbook, a reminder from Nanami, and the details of an October book event that Mio had sent him.

October had one date written in advance.

He had not written it himself.

Book event — Mio.

Itsuki stood in front of the calendar with a mug of tea.

There was something strange about seeing a plan that had begun with his own answer. Mio had asked whether he wanted to go. He had said yes. No one had needed to persuade him, remind him, or explain why he should make time.

It was a small decision.

He was beginning to notice those.

His phone vibrated on the counter.

Nanami: Is it cold there?

He glanced toward the balcony. The morning air had lost the last of its summer warmth. A thin layer of cloud covered the sky, and the neighbour across the courtyard was taking a jacket off a clothesline.

Itsuki: Not especially.

Nanami: That's not a weather report.

Itsuki: It's a temperature opinion.

Nanami: Wear a jacket.

He looked at the light jacket hanging beside the entrance.

Itsuki: I was planning to.

Nanami: Good. I don't want to hear that you were cold later.

Itsuki: I'll try to suffer quietly.

She sent a sticker of a small cat holding a clipboard.

Itsuki put the phone down and finished his tea.

At school, the change in season was more obvious. The morning air moved through the corridors, carrying the smell of damp leaves from the courtyard. Students had begun wearing cardigans and light jackets over their uniforms. The windows in Class 3-B were open, but no one complained about the breeze.

Riko Tachibana arrived with a scarf looped loosely around her neck.

Itsuki looked at it.

“It's October.”

“Excellent observation.”

“It's not winter.”

“I know. That's why I'm wearing a scarf and not a blanket.”

“A narrow distinction.”

Riko dropped into her seat. “You could try being supportive.”

“I could.”

“And yet?”

“The scarf is fine.”

“There. Was that difficult?”

“A little.”

She laughed and pulled a folded paper from her bag. Itsuki recognised the school logo at the top.

“What's that?”

“A form.”

“I can see that.”

“Then why ask?”

“I was hoping for useful information.”

Riko tapped the paper against her desk. “Career guidance. We have to write down what we're considering after graduation.”

“Again?”

“Apparently the first questionnaire wasn't enough to make us panic.”

Itsuki took his own copy from his bag. The school had distributed an updated sheet with spaces for possible university courses, vocational programmes, and questions students wanted to ask during the next guidance session.

He read the first section.

There were more boxes than answers.

“Have you decided?” Riko asked.

“No.”

“Good. I was worried you'd become responsible over the summer.”

“I did buy groceries.”

“That's not a future.”

“It could be a career.”

Riko leaned back. “You'd be a terrible supermarket employee.”

“I would keep the shelves organised.”

“You'd tell customers the vegetables were being unreasonable.”

“Some of them are.”

Before she could reply, Mr. Hasegawa entered the classroom and asked everyone to put the forms away until homeroom. Riko folded hers in half and slid it beneath her notebook.

Itsuki did the same.

Outside the window, a few leaves moved across the sports field.

October had begun.

There was already something on the calendar.

For the first time, Itsuki wondered what else he might choose to put there.

Chapter 1 The Book Event We Agreed To

The October book event was held in a small hall above a bookshop near the station.

Itsuki arrived twelve minutes before the time Mio had given him. The entrance was easy to miss: a narrow staircase beside the shop window, with a printed sign taped to the wall and an arrow pointing upstairs. He checked the message again to make sure he had the right place.

He did.

He was considering whether to go inside when Mio appeared at the top of the stairs.

“Kurose.”

“Kanzaki.”

She came down two steps, holding a folded leaflet.

“You're early.”

“The train was on time.”

“You use that explanation often.”

“It remains accurate.”

Mio looked at the station clock visible through the shop window. “The event starts in twelve minutes.”

“I know.”

“Then you have successfully arrived early.”

“Thank you for confirming.”

Her expression shifted slightly. It was the closest thing to a smile she usually offered before she had settled into a conversation.

“Come up. They're letting people browse the tables before the talk.”

The hall was smaller than Itsuki expected. Several folding tables had been arranged in two rows, each displaying books from independent publishers and local writers. A few visitors stood near the windows, reading sample pages. At the front, a woman was checking a microphone that did not seem to be connected to anything.

“Is this a school event?” he asked.

“No. The bookshop hosts it every autumn. Local writers and small publishers bring their work. There's a short talk later, but most people come to browse.”

“You come every year?”

“When I can.”

Mio moved toward the first table. She did not hurry, and she did not explain every display. Itsuki followed at a comfortable distance, stopping when a title caught his attention.

One table held short essay collections. Another had illustrated books about the city and its history. A third displayed novels with plain covers and handwritten recommendation cards.

“You said there was a book you wanted to find,” Itsuki said.

“A collection of short stories. The author published a new edition this year.”

“Do you know what it looks like?”

“I have a photograph.”

She showed him her phone. The cover was pale grey, with a small drawing of a train platform.

Itsuki looked at the tables.

“That should be easy to recognise.”

“That's what I thought.”

They searched the displays. Mio found two books by the same publisher but not the edition she wanted. Itsuki checked the labels on the lower shelf while she asked the vendor whether the new edition had arrived.

“We sold the last copy this morning,” the woman said. “I'm sorry.”

Mio nodded. “That's all right. Thank you.”

They moved away from the table.

“You look disappointed,” Itsuki said.

“A little. I wanted to compare the new edition with the old one.”

“Could you order it?”

“I could. It isn't the same as finding it here.”

“No.”

She glanced toward the next row. “There are other books.”

“A convincing recovery.”

“I'm trying.”

They spent the next twenty minutes browsing. Mio picked up a small collection of essays about train journeys, read several pages, and returned it to the table. Itsuki found a book about everyday architecture and bought it after reading the first chapter. He had not planned to buy anything, but the author had described a familiar shopping street in a way that made him curious about the rest.

“You found something,” Mio said.

“Apparently.”

“What is it about?”

“Buildings people stop noticing.”

She looked at the cover. “That sounds like something you would choose.”

“Is that good or bad?”

“I haven't decided.”

Itsuki paid for the book.

The event began with a short talk by a local editor. She spoke about how small details could change the way a reader understood a place, and how writers often had to decide which details to leave out. The microphone worked for the first five minutes, stopped for the next two, and returned after someone changed the cable.

“A strong beginning,” Itsuki whispered.

Mio looked toward the front. “It establishes the setting.”

“A technical success.”

She lowered her gaze to the leaflet, but he saw the small movement at the corner of her mouth.

After the talk, the visitors were invited to ask questions. Mio raised her hand once, asking how an editor decided whether a quiet scene needed more explanation or should be left alone. The speaker answered thoughtfully, and Mio listened without writing anything down.

Itsuki found himself watching the way she listened. She did not interrupt. She did not look away to check whether someone else was paying attention. When the answer ended, she thanked the speaker and sat back.

Outside the hall, the afternoon had become cooler. The bookshop bell rang behind them as another visitor entered.

“Did you enjoy it?” Mio asked.

“Yes.”

“Even without the book you wanted?”

“I found another one.”

“That's true.”

They walked toward the station. The shopping street was busy with people buying groceries and students stopping at the convenience store. A bicycle bell rang behind them, and they stepped closer to the shopfront to let it pass.

“Thank you for coming,” Mio said.

Itsuki looked at her.

“You invited me.”

“I know. I still wanted to say it.”

“Then you're welcome.”

They reached the crossing. Mio's train was due first, and she checked the time on her phone.

“I'll look for the book again next week,” she said.

“Good luck.”

“Would you like to come to the bookshop with me?”

Itsuki considered his schedule. He had no particular plans for the following Saturday.

“Sure.”

“I'll message you.”

The crossing signal changed. Mio went toward the station entrance, while Itsuki turned toward the bus stop.

He had bought a book, listened to a talk, and spent an afternoon with someone who did not require him to explain every silence.

It had been a good use of a Saturday.

He decided not to make a habit of saying that aloud.

Chapter 2 The Bakery We Still Remembered

Ayane's message arrived on a Friday afternoon.

Ayane: Are you still free tomorrow? The bakery near the station?

Itsuki looked at the calendar on his phone. He had a grocery reminder in the morning and nothing after lunch.

Itsuki: Yes. What time?

Ayane: Around two?

Itsuki: All right.

He put the phone away.

It was not the first time they had met outside school. They had shared lunch, helped with the neighbourhood activity, and walked home together after the festival. The bakery visit had been mentioned during the summer and confirmed in the September messages. It had simply taken them until October to find a date.

On Saturday, Itsuki arrived at two minutes past two.

Ayane was standing near the bakery window, looking at the display of cakes. Her shoulder-length black hair moved in the breeze, and she wore a light brown cardigan over her uniform. When she noticed him, she straightened.

“Itsuki.”

“Sorry. The crossing took a while.”

“It's okay. I only just arrived.”

She looked at the display again.

“They have the chestnut bread today.”

“You like that one?”

“I used to.”

“Used to?”

“I haven't had it in a long time.”

“Then you can find out whether you still do.”

Ayane smiled. “That's a very practical approach.”

Inside, the bakery was warm and smelled of butter and roasted coffee. The front counter displayed small cakes, sweet rolls, and sandwiches. They chose two drinks and a table beside the window.

Ayane bought the chestnut bread. Itsuki chose a plain roll and tea.

She looked at his plate.

“You always choose something simple.”

“It's bread.”

“You say that as if bread has no variety.”

“It has enough.”

Ayane laughed and took a bite of hers.

“It's still good.”

“That's fortunate.”

“I was worried I'd remembered it better than it actually was.”

Itsuki looked at the bread in his hand. “That happens.”

“With food?”

“With anything.”

She considered his answer, then looked out of the window. A group of younger students crossed the street, one of them carrying a paper bag almost as large as her school bag.

“Do you remember when our families used to come here after visiting the river?” Ayane asked.

“Sometimes.”

“Your mother used to buy the same cake every time.”

“Strawberry shortcake.”

“Yes. And you always said you didn't want any, then ate half of hers.”

“I don't remember that.”

“I do.”

“Maybe she offered.”

“She did. After you stared at it for ten minutes.”

Itsuki considered this. “That sounds like a reasonable negotiation.”

Ayane laughed again.

For a while, they talked about their current classes. Ayane had been helping her mother with a local-history display for the neighbourhood association. The group had collected old photographs of the station district, the river path, and the shopping street before several of the buildings had been renovated.

“Some of the photographs are older than we are,” she said. “My father remembers where they were taken, but the captions are missing.”

“You could ask the people who took them.”

“Some of them moved away. A few have passed on.”

“Then you have to work it out from the picture.”

“That's what we're doing.”

She took a photograph from her bag. It showed a narrow street with a row of shops and a small bicycle parked near a sign. The image was faded at the edges.

Itsuki leaned closer.

“That corner is still there.”

“You recognise it?”

“The wall. The shop sign is different now, but the wall hasn't changed.”

Ayane looked at the picture again.

“I thought so. My father said the same thing.”

She placed the photograph back in its envelope.

“Would you mind helping me look through the rest sometime? You remember the neighbourhood differently from my parents.”

“I can try.”

“Thank you.”

They finished their food and ordered another drink. The afternoon moved slowly around them. A customer came in to collect a birthday cake. The cashier changed the display cards. Outside, a delivery bicycle stopped near the corner.

Ayane did not ask Itsuki to remember more than he could. When he recognised a place, she listened. When he did not, she moved on.

That made the old photographs easier to look at.

“I used to think I remembered everything about this neighbourhood,” she said.

“You probably remember more than I do.”

“Maybe. But I keep finding things I don't know.”

“That's normal.”

She smiled faintly.

“I know. I just used to think remembering was the same as knowing.”

Itsuki looked at the envelope on the table.

“It isn't.”

“No.”

She took a final sip of her drink.

“I'd like to do this again. Not only for the photographs.”

Itsuki looked at her.

Ayane met his gaze, then looked down at her empty plate.

“I mean, we can meet without needing an old reason. We could go somewhere, or have lunch at school. Whatever is comfortable.”

“That's fine.”

She nodded, relieved.

“Good.”

Itsuki put his cup down.

“You can just ask.”

Ayane looked up.

“I know.”

“You don't need to bring a photograph.”

She laughed, and this time the sound came easily.

“I'll remember that.”

They left the bakery a little after four. At the crossing, Ayane turned toward her neighbourhood and Itsuki headed for the station.

“See you on Monday,” she said.

“See you.”

He walked away with the taste of tea still in his mouth and the image of the old street in his thoughts.

It was strange how a place could remain familiar while the people who remembered it changed.

He did not know what the old photographs would show them.

He was willing to look.

Interlude I The Schedule in Nanami's Bag

Nanami Kurose kept the inter-school reading project schedule in a blue folder.

It was not the same folder she used for ordinary school assignments. That one had a cracked corner and a small sticker Yui had put there without permission. The blue folder was newer, with separate pockets for the participating schools, the community library, and the volunteer list.

Yui had called it excessive.

Nanami had called it necessary.

“You made another column,” Yui said, leaning over the desk.

“The previous one was full.”

“You could have made the writing smaller.”

“Then nobody could read it.”

“I can read it.”

“You draw over the words.”

Yui looked at the page.

“That's a separate issue.”

They were in the Hoshimiya East High School library after classes. The teacher supervising their project had asked them to prepare a preliminary plan for a December reading day at the community library near the east station. Students from both schools would help arrange a small book display, read picture books to younger visitors, and create a local-history corner using photographs supplied by residents.

Nanami had volunteered to coordinate the student schedule.

Yui had volunteered to make the display.

Both had immediately regretted the amount of paperwork.

“We need to confirm the number of tables,” Nanami said. “The library has four small ones and two folding tables. We also need to know how many students from Municipal High are coming.”

“The teacher said they would ask this week.”

“Then I need to leave space for them.”

Yui drew a small star beside the date.

“And we need a name for the event.”

“The teacher called it the winter reading day.”

“That's not a name. That's a description.”

“It describes the event.”

“It doesn't sound inviting.”

Nanami considered the page.

“A Day of Local Stories?”

Yui's face brightened.

“That's good.”

“It can be the working title.”

“You always have to make something sound temporary.”

“Because it is temporary until the teacher approves it.”

Yui sighed and wrote the words at the top of her sketch.

Nanami checked the volunteer list. Several students had signed up for the reading corner. One wanted to help with the craft table. Another had offered to manage the book returns. Yui had listed herself for the display and Nanami for coordination.

“You should add yourself to the reading corner too,” Yui said.

“I can't be in two places at once.”

“You could help for a while.”

“If we have enough people.”

Yui studied her.

“You like reading to the little kids.”

“I do.”

“Then you should do it.”

Nanami looked at the list again. She had left one blank space beside the reading corner.

“I'll put myself down as a reserve.”

“That's not the same thing.”

“It's a start.”

Yui gave her a look, but did not argue.

Nanami's phone vibrated. Her father had sent a message asking whether she would be home for dinner on Sunday. He added that his girlfriend had found a small stationery shop near his office and thought Nanami might like the paper they sold.

Nanami read the message twice.

She liked the idea of seeing her father. She also liked having her own plans. The two things did not have to compete, though she sometimes needed a moment to remember that.

Nanami: I'll come on Sunday. I'd like to see the paper shop too.

Her father replied with a simple confirmation.

Yui glanced at her.

“Your dad?”

“Yes. He asked me to come over on Sunday.”

“Are you going?”

“I said yes.”

“Good.”

Nanami put the phone away.

“You make everything sound easy.”

“Not everything.”

“You do when it's my family.”

Yui looked at her for a moment.

“You don't have to solve it all before dinner.”

Nanami smiled.

“That's what Onii-chan says.”

“Then he has a good point.”

“Don't tell him. He'll become unbearable.”

Yui laughed.

They returned to the folder. The schedule still had blank spaces, the display still needed a final design, and the name still needed the teacher's approval.

Nanami wrote one more note beneath the volunteer list.

Ask Municipal High about the reading corner.

It was a small task.

It was hers.

Chapter 3 A Result in Red

Riko Tachibana discovered that a red mark could ruin an otherwise pleasant morning.

It was not a particularly large mark. It sat beside the final question on her mathematics assessment, where she had lost several points for setting up the problem incorrectly. The total score was printed at the top of the page.

She looked at it once.

Then she turned the paper over.

“That bad?” Itsuki asked.

“No.”

He glanced at the page.

“You turned it over.”

“I'm giving it privacy.”

“The score is still there.”

“It needs time.”

They were in Class 3-B after the morning mathematics lesson. Several students were comparing results, some pleased and others pretending they had not looked. Mr. Hasegawa had asked anyone who wanted to discuss their marks to speak with him after class.

Riko slid the paper beneath her notebook.

“It's not the worst score I've ever had.”

“That's a very specific achievement.”

“Thank you. I have a long history.”

Itsuki did not tease her further. He had seen her work through the practice pages over the previous weeks. She had improved, but she still made mistakes when a question required several steps and she became impatient with the wording.

Riko tapped the notebook.

“Don't look so serious. It's only one test.”

“I didn't say anything.”

“Your face did.”

“My face is not involved.”

“Then tell it to stop judging me.”

The bell rang before he could answer.

After lunch, Mr. Hasegawa asked Riko to stay behind.

Itsuki was packing his books when he heard her name. Riko glanced at him, made a small face, and went to the teacher's desk.

“I'll wait,” Itsuki said.

“You don't have to.”

“I know.”

She looked at him for a moment, then nodded.

Mr. Hasegawa spoke quietly. He did not raise his voice or make the score sound like a catastrophe. He explained that Riko had fallen below the school's expected standard in the latest mathematics assessment and that several earlier assignments were incomplete. She would need to attend a short series of supervised support sessions and submit the missing work before the end of the month.

He also wanted a parent or guardian to sign the notice.

Riko listened with her hands folded on the desk.

“I understand,” she said.

“This isn't a punishment,” Mr. Hasegawa told her. “It's a chance to address the gaps before the next assessment. You can do the work. But you need to show that you're doing it consistently.”

“Yes, sensei.”

“If you have trouble attending a session, speak to me beforehand. Don't simply miss it.”

“I won't.”

He handed her the notice.

Riko read it carefully. The schedule listed three after-school sessions, a set of revision exercises, and a deadline for the missing assignments. At the bottom was a space for a guardian's signature.

“I'll bring it back tomorrow.”

“Good.”

When she left the desk, Itsuki was waiting near the door.

“Everything okay?”

“I've been promoted.”

“To what?”

“The mathematics support department.”

“Congratulations.”

“Thank you. The benefits are terrible.”

They walked into the corridor together. Riko kept the notice inside her folder, between two sheets of blank paper.

“Do you want to talk about it?” Itsuki asked.

“Not particularly.”

“All right.”

She looked at him.

“That's it?”

“You said you didn't want to.”

“You could at least pretend to be curious.”

“I'm curious. I'm also capable of waiting.”

Riko looked down the corridor. A group of students passed them, laughing about something that had happened in the gym.

“I have to tell my parents,” she said.

“The form says so.”

“I can read.”

“Good.”

“You're supposed to say something encouraging.”

Itsuki considered it. “You can do the work.”

Riko's expression softened for half a second.

Then she raised an eyebrow.

“That sounded suspiciously sincere.”

“I'll be more careful.”

“Please do.”

They reached the shoe lockers. Riko stopped to change her shoes, then looked at the folder again.

“I don't want you to explain everything to me,” she said. “I know you can, but I need to understand it myself.”

Itsuki nodded.

“Then ask me to check your working, not do it for you.”

“I can do that.”

“Good.”

She put the folder into her bag.

“And don't tell anyone.”

“I wasn't planning to.”

“Especially not Nanami. She'll make a schedule.”

“She might colour-code it.”

“Exactly. I don't need that kind of pressure.”

Riko headed toward the bus stop.

Itsuki watched her go, then took the train home.

He had not offered to fix the problem.

She had not asked him to.

That seemed like a better arrangement.

Chapter 4 The Form with Two Boxes

Akari Fujisaki's updated career questionnaire had two boxes labelled First choice and Second choice.

She disliked both of them.

Not because the school expected her to make a decision. She understood why the teachers needed a starting point for guidance. The problem was that the boxes made every possibility look like a competition she had to settle before she knew what she wanted.

She sat with Saki in the classroom after the final bell, the form spread between them.

“What are you thinking?” Saki asked.

“I'm thinking the boxes are too small.”

“You can write on the back.”

“That's not the point.”

Saki looked at the form.

“Then what's the point?”

Akari leaned back.

“My parents have talked about business programmes for a while. They think it would give me a lot of options. And I understand why.”

“Do you want to study business?”

“Maybe. I like organising things. I like working with people. I like planning events.”

“That sounds like a reason.”

“It is. But I also want to look at communications and media courses. I don't know if I'd like the actual work, though.”

Saki folded her arms on the desk.

“Then go look.”

“At an open campus?”

“Yes.”

“I was thinking about it.”

“You say that a lot.”

Akari smiled.

“I know.”

She had spent much of the previous week looking through university brochures. Her family had never told her that she had to choose a particular school. They had suggested programmes, shared information, and offered to arrange visits. Their expectations were real, but they were not commands.

That made it harder to dismiss them.

At home, her mother was arranging flowers in the dining room when Akari brought up the open-campus dates.

“There's a communications programme at Seiran University,” Akari said. “They have an open day next month.”

Her mother looked up.

“Would you like to visit?”

“Yes. I also want to look at the business programme at the same university.”

“That's sensible. You can compare them.”

Akari had expected a question about why she was considering something different. Instead, her mother asked what time the event started.

Her father joined them a few minutes later. He listened while Akari explained the two programmes and the subjects she wanted to compare.

“I think it's worth looking at both,” he said. “You don't have to choose based on the name of the course. Ask about what students actually study.”

“I will.”

“And if you decide that neither is right, that's useful information too.”

Akari nodded.

Her mother placed the flower scissors on the table.

“We suggested business because we thought it would suit your interests and give you flexibility. But you should understand what you're choosing.”

“I know.”

“You don't have to agree with us just because we've suggested it.”

Akari looked at her mother.

There was no dramatic pause. Her mother simply returned to the flowers, adjusting one stem so it sat more naturally beside the others.

“Thank you,” Akari said.

“Of course.”

Later that evening, Akari opened the questionnaire again. She did not fill in the two boxes. Instead, she wrote a list of questions on the back: required subjects, practical projects, internship options, and how much freedom students had to choose electives.

She photographed the list and sent it to Saki.

Akari: I made a plan.

Saki: Is it a plan or a list?

Akari: A list that could become a plan.

Saki: Very convincing.

Akari smiled.

She opened another message thread.

Akari: Kurose-kun, did you look at the career information sheet yet?

His reply arrived a little later.

Itsuki: I looked at it.

Akari: Any useful conclusions?

Itsuki: It contains many boxes.

Akari: I knew you'd understand.

Itsuki: I'm considering writing that down as my first choice.

Akari laughed quietly.

Akari: I'll let you know if I find a better answer.

She put the phone down and returned to the list.

The two boxes could wait.

Chapter 5 The Quiet Table

The library table near the windows had become an ordinary meeting place.

Itsuki noticed this on a Tuesday afternoon when he arrived and found Mio already there. She had placed two books on the table, one beside her notebook and the other on the empty chair opposite her.

“That's mine?” he asked.

“I thought you might want to read it.”

He picked it up.

The cover showed a row of houses beside a railway line. The title was printed in small letters near the bottom.

“What is it about?”

“People who live near a station.”

“That's broad.”

“It's a collection of short stories.”

“That's less broad.”

“You're welcome.”

Itsuki sat down and opened the first page.

They read for a while. Mio worked through a chapter in her own book, occasionally making a small note in the margin of her notebook. Itsuki read the first story, then turned back to the opening page to check a detail he thought he had missed.

“Do you like it?” Mio asked.

“I haven't decided.”

“That's fair.”

“The first story ends before it explains why the neighbour stopped using the station.”

“I noticed.”

“Was that intentional?”

“I think so. The next story might answer it.”

Itsuki looked at her.

“You read it already?”

“Yes.”

“Then why ask me?”

“I wanted to know whether you would notice.”

He returned to the page.

“That's a strange test.”

“It wasn't a test.”

“It sounds like one.”

“Then you passed.”

They continued reading.

When the library announcement reminded students that the study area would close in twenty minutes, Mio put her bookmark between the pages.

“Do you want to get something to drink?” she asked.

“Sure.”

They went to the vending machines near the entrance. Mio chose hot tea. Itsuki chose the same, partly because the other options were cold and partly because he did not want to spend five minutes deciding.

“You always choose tea,” Mio said.

“It's reliable.”

“So is water.”

“Water doesn't come in a can.”

“It could.”

“That sounds unnecessary.”

Mio considered the vending machine.

“I think I saw bottled water in a can once.”

“Then I hope it was an emergency.”

She looked at him.

“You make ordinary things sound unreasonable.”

“I try to be consistent.”

They stood near the window while the drinks cooled. Outside, the sky had turned pale grey. A few leaves had gathered along the pavement near the bicycle racks.

“Do you usually stay at the library until it closes?” Itsuki asked.

“Not every day.”

“What do you do when you don't?”

“Sometimes I go home. Sometimes I visit the bookshop. Sometimes I walk around.”

“Alone?”

“Usually.”

She said it without embarrassment.

“Do you mind?” he asked.

Mio looked at the drink in her hands.

“No. I like being alone when I've chosen it.”

Itsuki remembered their conversation in the summer.

“And when you haven't?”

“Then I should probably tell someone.”

He nodded.

“That sounds useful.”

“It's still something I'm learning.”

They returned to the table and packed their books. At the entrance, Mio checked the time.

“I'll be here Thursday.”

“For the book?”

“For the book. And because I have a free period.”

“All right.”

She looked at him.

“You can come too, if you want.”

“I probably will.”

Mio nodded.

They separated outside the library.

Itsuki walked toward the station with the borrowed book inside his bag. He had not been asked to investigate anything. He had not needed to explain why he was quiet. They had simply read, talked, and bought tea from a machine.

It was becoming familiar.

He did not mind that.

Chapter 6 The Street in the Photograph

Ayane invited Itsuki to her family's house on the last Saturday of October.

Her message was brief.

Ayane: My parents are sorting the old neighbourhood photographs tomorrow. Would you like to help us identify a few places?

Itsuki replied that he could come in the afternoon.

He arrived at three with a small box of sweets from the station bakery. Ayane's mother greeted him at the door and thanked him for coming. The house smelled of tea and something baking in the kitchen.

“Ayane is in the living room,” she said. “Her father has spread everything across the table.”

“That sounds organised.”

Her mother laughed.

“It was organised for about five minutes.”

Itsuki removed his shoes and followed the hallway.

The living room table was covered with photographs, envelopes, and a large notebook. Ayane sat on the floor beside one stack, sorting pictures by location. Her father was standing near the cabinet, looking through a folder.

“Itsuki,” Ayane said. “You came.”

“I said I would.”

“I know. Thank you.”

Her father greeted him and moved a stack of photographs to make room.

“We're trying to identify the old shopping street. Some of these were taken before the station renovation.”

Itsuki sat down.

“I'll see what I recognise.”

They began with a photograph of the river path. The trees were smaller, the railing had a different shape, and the path itself was narrower. Itsuki recognised the bridge but not the shop visible in the background.

Ayane wrote a note.

“The bridge is certain. The shop isn't.”

Her father nodded.

“We'll leave that one open.”

They moved through several more photographs. Itsuki identified a corner near the station and a small playground that had since been rebuilt. Ayane recognised a bakery sign from the colour of its awning. Her father supplied the year for a picture of the old bus terminal.

Then Ayane took out a smaller envelope.

“These are from our families,” she said. “My mother found them in the cabinet.”

Itsuki looked at the photographs.

One showed a group of adults standing outside a house. Another showed several children around a low table. He recognised the living room immediately.

“That's your old carpet,” he said.

Ayane looked closer.

“You're right.”

The photograph showed Itsuki and Ayane sitting beside a half-built tower of wooden blocks. A younger Nanami was not in the picture; she had not yet been born. Itsuki's hair was shorter, and he was looking at the blocks with a seriousness that seemed disproportionate to the task.

Ayane laughed.

“You were always careful with those.”

“They fell over easily.”

“You used to make me wait until you finished the bottom row.”

“It needed to be straight.”

“I remember.”

She placed the photograph beside another.

This one showed them outside near the river. Ayane was holding a small pinwheel, and Itsuki stood beside her with a paper bag in his hand. Both were looking toward someone outside the frame.

“I don't remember this day,” Itsuki said.

“Neither do I.”

Her father leaned over.

“That was the neighbourhood summer event. You both wanted to go to the river after the food stalls closed.”

“I remember the river,” Ayane said. “Not the pinwheel.”

Itsuki looked at the paper bag.

“I might have been carrying snacks.”

“You usually were,” her father said.

Ayane smiled.

“You were always hungry.”

“I was growing.”

“You were seven.”

“A demanding age.”

They continued sorting.

After a while, Ayane's father was called into the kitchen to answer a phone call. Her mother came in with tea and placed a plate of small cakes on the table.

“Take a break,” she said. “You can finish the rest later.”

Ayane looked at the remaining photographs.

“We still have a lot.”

“They aren't going anywhere.”

Her mother smiled and returned to the kitchen.

Ayane picked up one of the photographs.

“I used to think I remembered every afternoon we spent together.”

Itsuki took a sip of tea.

“That's a lot of afternoons.”

“I know. But I remembered the feeling more than the details.”

She turned the photograph over. There was no writing on the back.

“Sometimes I remember something and then find out it happened on a different day. Or that you remember it differently.”

“That happens.”

“I don't want to keep asking you to remember things just because I miss them.”

Itsuki looked at her.

Ayane's voice remained quiet.

“I like knowing we had those days. But I don't want to use them as a reason you have to spend time with me now.”

He set his cup down.

“You don't have to.”

“I know.”

“I come because I want to.”

Ayane looked up.

He had not intended the sentence to sound important. It was simply the truth.

Her expression eased.

“Thank you.”

“You don't need to thank me every time.”

“I know.”

She smiled.

“I'll try to remember that too.”

They returned to the photographs. When Itsuki left later that afternoon, Ayane walked him to the entrance.

“Would you like to have lunch together next week?” she asked.

“Sure.”

“I'll message you.”

“All right.”

He stepped outside.

The wind chime near the door moved once, making the same small sound he remembered from years ago.

Itsuki walked toward the station.

The past had not become less important.

It had simply stopped being the only place they could meet.

Interlude II The Form on the Kitchen Table

Riko placed the school notice on the kitchen table before her mother could ask about it.

She had considered leaving it in her bag until after dinner. She had also considered asking Itsuki to check the form first, though she knew that would only delay the conversation by another day.

Her mother looked at the paper.

“What's this?”

“A notice from school.”

“About?”

“Mathematics.”

Her mother pulled out a chair.

Riko sat opposite her.

She explained the assessment result, the missing assignments, and the support sessions. She did not make a joke about the score. She did not blame the teacher or the wording of the questions. She told her mother that she had not been keeping up consistently and that the school wanted her to complete the work before the next assessment.

Her mother listened without interrupting.

“Why didn't you tell us when the earlier assignments were missing?”

Riko looked at the notice.

“I thought I could catch up.”

“Could you?”

“Not properly.”

Her mother folded her hands on the table.

“I don't want you to hide things until they become bigger problems.”

“I know.”

“Your father and I want you to do well. We also need to know when you need help.”

Riko nodded.

“I'm going to the sessions.”

“Good.”

Her mother read the schedule and signed the form. She asked whether Riko had enough time after school to attend the sessions and finish the remaining assignments.

“I think so. I'll have to move a few things around.”

“Then make a schedule you can actually follow.”

Riko almost smiled.

“Not one with three colours?”

“If it helps.”

“The last one was mostly pink.”

“I remember.”

Her mother slid the signed notice back across the table.

“You can do better than this result. But I don't want you to study only because we're watching.”

Riko looked up.

“I don't want that either.”

Her mother nodded.

“Then we'll talk again after the first session.”

She stood and returned to the kitchen.

Riko remained at the table for a moment.

She had expected disappointment. It was there, perhaps, but it had not become anger. Her mother had asked questions, listened to the answers, and signed the form.

It was not a disaster.

It was also not something she could laugh away.

Riko took out her phone.

Riko: Told my mother. She signed it.

Itsuki replied a few minutes later.

Itsuki: Good.

Riko: That's all?

Itsuki: You wanted me to say good job?

Riko: Maybe.

Itsuki: Good job telling her.

Riko looked at the message.

Then she put the phone down and opened the mathematics workbook.

There was still work to do.

Chapter 7 The Work She Chose

Riko's first support session was held in an ordinary classroom after school.

There were six students, two teachers, and a stack of worksheets arranged at the front. The room was quiet except for the sound of pencils and the occasional page turning.

Riko took a seat near the window.

She had brought her workbook, the missing assignments, and a pencil case with more pens than she needed.

Mr. Hasegawa checked the attendance list.

“We'll work through the questions you marked. If you understand a step, write it down. If you don't, ask. Don't spend twenty minutes pretending the page is going to explain itself.”

Riko looked at the worksheet.

“That's very specific,” she murmured.

The student beside her gave a quiet laugh.

They began with equations and moved on to word problems. Riko made mistakes. She also corrected several without help. When she became stuck, she raised her hand rather than turning the page.

It was slower than she wanted.

It was more useful than she expected.

At the end of the session, Mr. Hasegawa checked her work.

“This is better. You showed the steps instead of jumping to the answer.”

“I was told to.”

“And you listened.”

“Sometimes.”

He smiled.

“Keep doing it.”

Riko packed her books and left the classroom.

Itsuki was waiting near the shoe lockers. He had stayed to return a library book and was checking a message on his phone.

“You waited,” she said.

“I was already here.”

“That's not what I asked.”

“I waited.”

She smiled.

“How was your afternoon?”

“Quiet.”

“Good. Mine was full of equations.”

“How did it go?”

“I did the work.”

“That's good.”

Riko walked beside him toward the entrance.

“I think I understand why I kept getting the same questions wrong.”

“Why?”

“I knew what to do when the example looked like the one before it. When the wording changed, I tried to guess which method the teacher wanted instead of reading the conditions.”

Itsuki nodded.

“That makes sense.”

“I still don't like it.”

“You don't have to like it.”

“You make a terrible motivational speaker.”

“I wasn't applying.”

She laughed.

They reached the shoe lockers. Riko changed into her outdoor shoes and took the signed notice from her folder.

“My mother asked me to make a schedule.”

“Will you?”

“Yes. A realistic one.”

“Good.”

She looked at him.

“I don't want you to think I'm only doing this because the school told me to.”

“I don't.”

“You don't?”

“You could have ignored the form.”

“I couldn't. My mother signed it.”

“You could still have done the minimum.”

Riko considered that.

“I suppose.”

They stepped outside. The air had cooled, and the sky was already beginning to darken. A few students were leaving the sports field with club bags over their shoulders.

“Do you want to get something to eat?” Riko asked.

Itsuki glanced at the time.

“I have to go to the supermarket.”

“Then I'll come with you.”

“Why?”

“Because I want to.”

He looked at her.

Riko's expression was casual, but she did not turn it into a joke.

“All right,” he said.

They walked toward the station district. Riko told him about a classmate who had fallen asleep during the support session and woken up when the teacher said the word “fraction.” Itsuki asked whether the student had understood the problem. Riko said she had not checked.

At the supermarket, they bought rice, vegetables, and a carton of milk. Riko picked up a packet of biscuits and placed it in the basket.

“Those aren't on your list,” Itsuki said.

“They're for morale.”

“Whose?”

“Mine.”

“Then you can pay for them.”

“Cruel.”

She put the biscuits back, then picked up a smaller packet.

“These are within the budget.”

“Your budget?”

“My emotional budget.”

Itsuki did not argue.

At the checkout, Riko paid for the biscuits herself.

Outside, she held up the small bag.

“A responsible purchase.”

“Congratulations.”

“You're learning.”

They reached the crossing and stopped beneath the traffic light.

“Thanks for coming with me,” Riko said.

“You came with me.”

“I know.”

She smiled, then looked away.

For once, the joke did not arrive immediately.

Itsuki noticed.

He did not ask her to explain.

The signal changed, and they crossed with the crowd.

Chapter 8 The Campus on the Hill

Seiran University's open campus was held on a clear Saturday in early November.

Akari arrived with Saki and a folder containing the two programme brochures she had printed at home. The university stood on a hill beyond the central station, with a broad entrance path lined by trees and a row of buildings that looked newer than the schools she was used to visiting.

“This is bigger than I expected,” Saki said.

“You said you wanted to see the campus.”

“I did. I didn't expect to need a map.”

Akari checked the programme sheet.

“The communications session starts in twenty minutes.”

“Then let's go.”

They followed the signs to a lecture room where a group of students were waiting. A staff member handed them a booklet and explained the schedule. The session covered the course structure, practical projects, and the kinds of work graduates sometimes pursued.

Akari listened carefully.

She liked the idea of studying how people communicated, how information moved between groups, and how public events were planned. The course included writing, research, presentation work, and collaborative projects.

It also included subjects she had not expected.

After the session, she asked a student guide how much freedom students had to choose electives.

“Quite a bit after the first year,” the guide said. “But the required courses are important. You should look at the full curriculum before deciding.”

“Thank you.”

Saki waited until they were outside.

“You looked interested.”

“I was.”

“More than the business session?”

Akari considered it.

“In a different way.”

They visited the business programme next. The presentation was clear and practical. It covered management, finance, marketing, and the role of organisations in local communities. Akari recognised several topics her parents had mentioned before.

She liked some of them.

She also found herself thinking about the communications session.

At lunch, they sat outside near the courtyard. Students were handing out campus maps, and a small group was performing music near the main building.

“So?” Saki asked.

Akari opened her folder.

“I don't know yet.”

“That's allowed.”

“I know. I just thought one of them would feel obviously right.”

“Maybe that's not how it works.”

Akari smiled.

“You sound like my mother.”

“Your mother seems sensible.”

“She is.”

They finished lunch and visited the library. Akari asked about student projects and the possibility of joining an event-planning committee. The staff member explained that students could apply for several volunteer programmes after enrolment.

Akari wrote the information in her notebook.

When they left the campus, the afternoon sun had begun to soften.

“Do you want to tell your parents?” Saki asked.

“Yes.”

“What will you say?”

Akari looked at the two brochures.

“That I want to keep both options open for now. But I want to learn more about communications before I decide.”

“Sounds good.”

“It feels strange saying it.”

“Why?”

“Because it sounds like I'm choosing something for myself.”

Saki bumped her shoulder lightly.

“You are allowed to do that.”

Akari laughed.

“I know.”

Her phone vibrated while they waited for the train. A message from her father asked how the visit had gone.

Akari: Good. I liked the communications programme. I want to compare the full course details before choosing.

His reply came a few minutes later.

Father: That sounds sensible. We can look at the information together this evening.

Akari put the phone away.

She had not chosen her future.

She had chosen what to investigate next.

For now, that was enough.
