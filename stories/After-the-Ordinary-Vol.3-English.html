Prologue A Date Already Written

October arrived on a Thursday.

Itsuki Kurose knew this because the first thing he saw after waking was the calendar on his refrigerator, where September had ended with a small collection of blue and black marks. The last Wednesday of the month had been ordinary enough: school, a message from Riko about a workbook, a reminder from Nanami, and the details of an October book event that Mio had sent him.

October had one date written in advance.

He had not written it himself.

Book event — Mio.

Itsuki stood in front of the calendar with a mug of tea.

There was something strange about seeing a plan that had begun with his own answer. Mio had asked whether he wanted to go. He had said yes. No one had needed to persuade him, remind him, or explain why he should make time.

It was a small decision.

He was beginning to notice those.

His phone vibrated on the counter.

Nanami: Is it cold there?

He glanced toward the balcony. The morning air had lost the last of its summer warmth. A thin layer of cloud covered the sky, and the neighbour across the courtyard was taking a jacket off a clothesline.

Itsuki: Not especially.

Nanami: That's not a weather report.

Itsuki: It's a temperature opinion.

Nanami: Wear a jacket.

He looked at the light jacket hanging beside the entrance.

Itsuki: I was planning to.

Nanami: Good. I don't want to hear that you were cold later.

Itsuki: I'll try to suffer quietly.

She sent a sticker of a small cat holding a clipboard.

Itsuki put the phone down and finished his tea.

At school, the change in season was more obvious. The morning air moved through the corridors, carrying the smell of damp leaves from the courtyard. Students had begun wearing cardigans and light jackets over their uniforms. The windows in Class 3-B were open, but no one complained about the breeze.

Riko Tachibana arrived with a scarf looped loosely around her neck.

Itsuki looked at it.

“It's October.”

“Excellent observation.”

“It's not winter.”

“I know. That's why I'm wearing a scarf and not a blanket.”

“A narrow distinction.”

Riko dropped into her seat. “You could try being supportive.”

“I could.”

“And yet?”

“The scarf is fine.”

“There. Was that difficult?”

“A little.”

She laughed and pulled a folded paper from her bag. Itsuki recognised the school logo at the top.

“What's that?”

“A form.”

“I can see that.”

“Then why ask?”

“I was hoping for useful information.”

Riko tapped the paper against her desk. “Career guidance. We have to write down what we're considering after graduation.”

“Again?”

“Apparently the first questionnaire wasn't enough to make us panic.”

Itsuki took his own copy from his bag. The school had distributed an updated sheet with spaces for possible university courses, vocational programmes, and questions students wanted to ask during the next guidance session.

He read the first section.

There were more boxes than answers.

“Have you decided?” Riko asked.

“No.”

“Good. I was worried you'd become responsible over the summer.”

“I did buy groceries.”

“That's not a future.”

“It could be a career.”

Riko leaned back. “You'd be a terrible supermarket employee.”

“I would keep the shelves organised.”

“You'd tell customers the vegetables were being unreasonable.”

“Some of them are.”

Before she could reply, Mr. Hasegawa entered the classroom and asked everyone to put the forms away until homeroom. Riko folded hers in half and slid it beneath her notebook.

Itsuki did the same.

Outside the window, a few leaves moved across the sports field.

October had begun.

There was already something on the calendar.

For the first time, Itsuki wondered what else he might choose to put there.

Chapter 1 The Book Event We Agreed To

On Saturday, October 10, the book event was held in a small hall above a bookshop near the station.

Itsuki arrived twelve minutes before the time Mio had given him. The entrance was easy to miss: a narrow staircase beside the shop window, with a printed sign taped to the wall and an arrow pointing upstairs. He checked the message again to make sure he had the right place.

He did.

He was considering whether to go inside when Mio appeared at the top of the stairs.

“Kurose.”

“Kanzaki.”

She came down two steps, holding a folded leaflet.

“You're early.”

“The train was on time.”

“You use that explanation often.”

“It remains accurate.”

Mio looked at the station clock visible through the shop window. “The event starts in twelve minutes.”

“I know.”

“Then you have successfully arrived early.”

“Thank you for confirming.”

Her expression shifted slightly. It was the closest thing to a smile she usually offered before she had settled into a conversation.

“Come up. They're letting people browse the tables before the talk.”

The hall was smaller than Itsuki expected. Several folding tables had been arranged in two rows, each displaying books from independent publishers and local writers. A few visitors stood near the windows, reading sample pages. At the front, a woman was checking a microphone that did not seem to be connected to anything.

“Is this a school event?” he asked.

“No. The bookshop hosts it every autumn. Local writers and small publishers bring their work. There's a short talk later, but most people come to browse.”

“You come every year?”

“When I can.”

Mio moved toward the first table. She did not hurry, and she did not explain every display. Itsuki followed at a comfortable distance, stopping when a title caught his attention.

One table held short essay collections. Another had illustrated books about the city and its history. A third displayed novels with plain covers and handwritten recommendation cards.

“You said there was a book you wanted to find,” Itsuki said.

“A collection of short stories. The author published a new edition this year.”

“Do you know what it looks like?”

“I have a photograph.”

She showed him her phone. The cover was pale grey, with a small drawing of a train platform.

Itsuki looked at the tables.

“That should be easy to recognise.”

“That's what I thought.”

They searched the displays. Mio found two books by the same publisher but not the edition she wanted. Itsuki checked the labels on the lower shelf while she asked the vendor whether the new edition had arrived.

“We sold the last copy this morning,” the woman said. “I'm sorry.”

Mio nodded. “That's all right. Thank you.”

They moved away from the table.

“You look disappointed,” Itsuki said.

“A little. I wanted to compare the new edition with the old one.”

“Could you order it?”

“I could. It isn't the same as finding it here.”

“No.”

She glanced toward the next row. “There are other books.”

“A convincing recovery.”

“I'm trying.”

They spent the next twenty minutes browsing. Mio picked up a small collection of essays about train journeys, read several pages, and returned it to the table. Itsuki found a book about everyday architecture and bought it after reading the first chapter. He had not planned to buy anything, but the author had described a familiar shopping street in a way that made him curious about the rest.

“You found something,” Mio said.

“Apparently.”

“What is it about?”

“Buildings people stop noticing.”

She looked at the cover. “That sounds like something you would choose.”

“Is that good or bad?”

“I haven't decided.”

Itsuki paid for the book.

The event began with a short talk by a local editor. She spoke about how small details could change the way a reader understood a place, and how writers often had to decide which details to leave out. The microphone worked for the first five minutes, stopped for the next two, and returned after someone changed the cable.

“A strong beginning,” Itsuki whispered.

Mio looked toward the front. “It establishes the setting.”

“A technical success.”

She lowered her gaze to the leaflet, but he saw the small movement at the corner of her mouth.

After the talk, the visitors were invited to ask questions. Mio raised her hand once, asking how an editor decided whether a quiet scene needed more explanation or should be left alone. The speaker answered thoughtfully, and Mio listened without writing anything down.

Itsuki found himself watching the way she listened. She did not interrupt. She did not look away to check whether someone else was paying attention. When the answer ended, she thanked the speaker and sat back.

Outside the hall, the afternoon had become cooler. The bookshop bell rang behind them as another visitor entered.

“Did you enjoy it?” Mio asked.

“Yes.”

“Even without the book you wanted?”

“I found another one.”

“That's true.”

They walked toward the station. The shopping street was busy with people buying groceries and students stopping at the convenience store. A bicycle bell rang behind them, and they stepped closer to the shopfront to let it pass.

“Thank you for coming,” Mio said.

Itsuki looked at her.

“You invited me.”

“I know. I still wanted to say it.”

“Then you're welcome.”

They reached the crossing. Mio's train was due first, and she checked the time on her phone.

“I'll look for the book again next week,” she said.

“Good luck.”

“Would you like to come to the bookshop with me?”

Itsuki considered his schedule. He had no particular plans for the following Saturday.

“Sure.”

“I'll message you.”

The crossing signal changed. Mio went toward the station entrance, while Itsuki turned toward the bus stop.

He had bought a book, listened to a talk, and spent an afternoon with someone who did not require him to explain every silence.

It had been a good use of a Saturday.

He decided not to make a habit of saying that aloud.

Chapter 2 The Bakery We Still Remembered

Ayane's message arrived on Friday, October 16.

Ayane: Are you still free tomorrow? The bakery near the station?

Itsuki looked at the calendar on his phone. He had a grocery reminder in the morning and nothing after lunch.

Itsuki: Yes. What time?

Ayane: Around two?

Itsuki: All right.

He put the phone away.

It was not the first time they had met outside school. They had shared lunch, helped with the neighbourhood activity, and walked home together after the festival. The bakery visit had been mentioned during the summer and confirmed in the September messages. It had simply taken them until October to find a date.

On Saturday, October 17, Itsuki arrived at two minutes past two.

Ayane was standing near the bakery window, looking at the display of cakes. Her shoulder-length black hair moved in the breeze, and she wore a light brown cardigan over her uniform. When she noticed him, she straightened.

“Itsuki.”

“Sorry. The crossing took a while.”

“It's okay. I only just arrived.”

She looked at the display again.

“They have the chestnut bread today.”

“You like that one?”

“I used to.”

“Used to?”

“I haven't had it in a long time.”

“Then you can find out whether you still do.”

Ayane smiled. “That's a very practical approach.”

Inside, the bakery was warm and smelled of butter and roasted coffee. The front counter displayed small cakes, sweet rolls, and sandwiches. They chose two drinks and a table beside the window.

Ayane bought the chestnut bread. Itsuki chose a plain roll and tea.

She looked at his plate.

“You always choose something simple.”

“It's bread.”

“You say that as if bread has no variety.”

“It has enough.”

Ayane laughed and took a bite of hers.

“It's still good.”

“That's fortunate.”

“I was worried I'd remembered it better than it actually was.”

Itsuki looked at the bread in his hand. “That happens.”

“With food?”

“With anything.”

She considered his answer, then looked out of the window. A group of younger students crossed the street, one of them carrying a paper bag almost as large as her school bag.

“Do you remember when our families used to come here after visiting the river?” Ayane asked.

“Sometimes.”

“Your mother used to buy the same cake every time.”

“Strawberry shortcake.”

“Yes. And you always said you didn't want any, then ate half of hers.”

“I don't remember that.”

“I do.”

“Maybe she offered.”

“She did. After you stared at it for ten minutes.”

Itsuki considered this. “That sounds like a reasonable negotiation.”

Ayane laughed again.

For a while, they talked about their current classes. Ayane had been helping her mother with a local-history display for the neighbourhood association. The group had collected old photographs of the station district, the river path, and the shopping street before several of the buildings had been renovated.

“Some of the photographs are older than we are,” she said. “My father remembers where they were taken, but the captions are missing.”

“You could ask the people who took them.”

“Some of them moved away. A few have passed on.”

“Then you have to work it out from the picture.”

“That's what we're doing.”

She took a photograph from her bag. It showed a narrow street with a row of shops and a small bicycle parked near a sign. The image was faded at the edges.

Itsuki leaned closer.

“That corner is still there.”

“You recognise it?”

“The wall. The shop sign is different now, but the wall hasn't changed.”

Ayane looked at the picture again.

“I thought so. My father said the same thing.”

She placed the photograph back in its envelope.

“Would you mind helping me look through the rest sometime? You remember the neighbourhood differently from my parents.”

“I can try.”

“Thank you.”

They finished their food and ordered another drink. The afternoon moved slowly around them. A customer came in to collect a birthday cake. The cashier changed the display cards. Outside, a delivery bicycle stopped near the corner.

Ayane did not ask Itsuki to remember more than he could. When he recognised a place, she listened. When he did not, she moved on.

That made the old photographs easier to look at.

“I used to think I remembered everything about this neighbourhood,” she said.

“You probably remember more than I do.”

“Maybe. But I keep finding things I don't know.”

“That's normal.”

She smiled faintly.

“I know. I just used to think remembering was the same as knowing.”

Itsuki looked at the envelope on the table.

“It isn't.”

“No.”

She took a final sip of her drink.

“I'd like to do this again. Not only for the photographs.”

Itsuki looked at her.

Ayane met his gaze, then looked down at her empty plate.

“I mean, we can meet without needing an old reason. We could go somewhere, or have lunch at school. Whatever is comfortable.”

“That's fine.”

She nodded, relieved.

“Good.”

Itsuki put his cup down.

“You can just ask.”

Ayane looked up.

“I know.”

“You don't need to bring a photograph.”

She laughed, and this time the sound came easily.

“I'll remember that.”

They left the bakery a little after four. At the crossing, Ayane turned toward her neighbourhood and Itsuki headed for the station.

“See you on Monday,” she said.

“See you.”

He walked away with the taste of tea still in his mouth and the image of the old street in his thoughts.

It was strange how a place could remain familiar while the people who remembered it changed.

He did not know what the old photographs would show them.

He was willing to look.

Interlude I The Schedule in Nanami's Bag

Nanami Kurose kept the inter-school reading project schedule in a blue folder.

It was not the same folder she used for ordinary school assignments. That one had a cracked corner and a small sticker Yui had put there without permission. The blue folder was newer, with separate pockets for the participating schools, the community library, and the volunteer list.

Yui had called it excessive.

Nanami had called it necessary.

“You made another column,” Yui said, leaning over the desk.

“The previous one was full.”

“You could have made the writing smaller.”

“Then nobody could read it.”

“I can read it.”

“You draw over the words.”

Yui looked at the page.

“That's a separate issue.”

They were in the Hoshimiya East High School library after classes. The teacher supervising their project had asked them to prepare a preliminary plan for a December reading day at the community library near the east station. Students from both schools would help arrange a small book display, read picture books to younger visitors, and create a local-history corner using photographs supplied by residents.

Nanami had volunteered to coordinate the student schedule.

Yui had volunteered to make the display.

Both had immediately regretted the amount of paperwork.

“We need to confirm the number of tables,” Nanami said. “The library has four small ones and two folding tables. We also need to know how many students from Municipal High are coming.”

“The teacher said they would ask this week.”

“Then I need to leave space for them.”

Yui drew a small star beside the date.

“And we need a name for the event.”

“The teacher called it the winter reading day.”

“That's not a name. That's a description.”

“It describes the event.”

“It doesn't sound inviting.”

Nanami considered the page.

“A Day of Local Stories?”

Yui's face brightened.

“That's good.”

“It can be the working title.”

“You always have to make something sound temporary.”

“Because it is temporary until the teacher approves it.”

Yui sighed and wrote the words at the top of her sketch.

Nanami checked the volunteer list. Several students had signed up for the reading corner. One wanted to help with the craft table. Another had offered to manage the book returns. Yui had listed herself for the display and Nanami for coordination.

“You should add yourself to the reading corner too,” Yui said.

“I can't be in two places at once.”

“You could help for a while.”

“If we have enough people.”

Yui studied her.

“You like reading to the little kids.”

“I do.”

“Then you should do it.”

Nanami looked at the list again. She had left one blank space beside the reading corner.

“I'll put myself down as a reserve.”

“That's not the same thing.”

“It's a start.”

Yui gave her a look, but did not argue.

Nanami's phone vibrated. Her father had sent a message asking whether she would be home for dinner on Sunday. He added that his girlfriend had found a small stationery shop near his office and thought Nanami might like the paper they sold.

Nanami read the message twice.

She liked the idea of seeing her father. She also liked having her own plans. The two things did not have to compete, though she sometimes needed a moment to remember that.

Nanami: I'll come on Sunday. I'd like to see the paper shop too.

Her father replied with a simple confirmation.

Yui glanced at her.

“Your dad?”

“Yes. He asked me to come over on Sunday.”

“Are you going?”

“I said yes.”

“Good.”

Nanami put the phone away.

“You make everything sound easy.”

“Not everything.”

“You do when it's my family.”

Yui looked at her for a moment.

“You don't have to solve it all before dinner.”

Nanami smiled.

“That's what Onii-chan says.”

“Then he has a good point.”

“Don't tell him. He'll become unbearable.”

Yui laughed.

They returned to the folder. The schedule still had blank spaces, the display still needed a final design, and the name still needed the teacher's approval.

Nanami wrote one more note beneath the volunteer list.

Ask Municipal High about the reading corner.

It was a small task.

It was hers.

Chapter 3 A Result in Red

Riko Tachibana discovered that a red mark could ruin an otherwise pleasant morning.

It was not a particularly large mark. It sat beside the final question on her mathematics assessment, where she had lost several points for setting up the problem incorrectly. The total score was printed at the top of the page.

She looked at it once.

Then she turned the paper over.

“That bad?” Itsuki asked.

“No.”

He glanced at the page.

“You turned it over.”

“I'm giving it privacy.”

“The score is still there.”

“It needs time.”

They were in Class 3-B after the morning mathematics lesson. Several students were comparing results, some pleased and others pretending they had not looked. Mr. Hasegawa had asked anyone who wanted to discuss their marks to speak with him after class.

Riko slid the paper beneath her notebook.

“It's not the worst score I've ever had.”

“That's a very specific achievement.”

“Thank you. I have a long history.”

Itsuki did not tease her further. He had seen her work through the practice pages over the previous weeks. She had improved, but she still made mistakes when a question required several steps and she became impatient with the wording.

Riko tapped the notebook.

“Don't look so serious. It's only one test.”

“I didn't say anything.”

“Your face did.”

“My face is not involved.”

“Then tell it to stop judging me.”

The bell rang before he could answer.

After lunch, Mr. Hasegawa asked Riko to stay behind.

Itsuki was packing his books when he heard her name. Riko glanced at him, made a small face, and went to the teacher's desk.

“I'll wait,” Itsuki said.

“You don't have to.”

“I know.”

She looked at him for a moment, then nodded.

Mr. Hasegawa spoke quietly. He did not raise his voice or make the score sound like a catastrophe. He explained that Riko had fallen below the school's expected standard in the latest mathematics assessment and that several earlier assignments were incomplete. She would need to attend a short series of supervised support sessions and submit the missing work before the end of the month.

He also wanted a parent or guardian to sign the notice.

Riko listened with her hands folded on the desk.

“I understand,” she said.

“This isn't a punishment,” Mr. Hasegawa told her. “It's a chance to address the gaps before the next assessment. You can do the work. But you need to show that you're doing it consistently.”

“Yes, sensei.”

“If you have trouble attending a session, speak to me beforehand. Don't simply miss it.”

“I won't.”

He handed her the notice.

Riko read it carefully. The schedule listed three after-school sessions, a set of revision exercises, and a deadline for the missing assignments. At the bottom was a space for a guardian's signature.

“I'll bring it back tomorrow.”

“Good.”

When she left the desk, Itsuki was waiting near the door.

“Everything okay?”

“I've been promoted.”

“To what?”

“The mathematics support department.”

“Congratulations.”

“Thank you. The benefits are terrible.”

They walked into the corridor together. Riko kept the notice inside her folder, between two sheets of blank paper.

“Do you want to talk about it?” Itsuki asked.

“Not particularly.”

“All right.”

She looked at him.

“That's it?”

“You said you didn't want to.”

“You could at least pretend to be curious.”

“I'm curious. I'm also capable of waiting.”

Riko looked down the corridor. A group of students passed them, laughing about something that had happened in the gym.

“I have to tell my parents,” she said.

“The form says so.”

“I can read.”

“Good.”

“You're supposed to say something encouraging.”

Itsuki considered it. “You can do the work.”

Riko's expression softened for half a second.

Then she raised an eyebrow.

“That sounded suspiciously sincere.”

“I'll be more careful.”

“Please do.”

They reached the shoe lockers. Riko stopped to change her shoes, then looked at the folder again.

“I don't want you to explain everything to me,” she said. “I know you can, but I need to understand it myself.”

Itsuki nodded.

“Then ask me to check your working, not do it for you.”

“I can do that.”

“Good.”

She put the folder into her bag.

“And don't tell anyone.”

“I wasn't planning to.”

“Especially not Nanami. She'll make a schedule.”

“She might colour-code it.”

“Exactly. I don't need that kind of pressure.”

Riko headed toward the bus stop.

Itsuki watched her go, then took the train home.

He had not offered to fix the problem.

She had not asked him to.

That seemed like a better arrangement.

Chapter 4 The Form with Two Boxes

Akari Fujisaki's updated career questionnaire had two boxes labelled First choice and Second choice.

She disliked both of them.

Not because the school expected her to make a decision. She understood why the teachers needed a starting point for guidance. The problem was that the boxes made every possibility look like a competition she had to settle before she knew what she wanted.

She sat with Saki in the classroom after the final bell, the form spread between them.

“What are you thinking?” Saki asked.

“I'm thinking the boxes are too small.”

“You can write on the back.”

“That's not the point.”

Saki looked at the form.

“Then what's the point?”

Akari leaned back.

“My parents have talked about business programmes for a while. They think it would give me a lot of options. And I understand why.”

“Do you want to study business?”

“Maybe. I like organising things. I like working with people. I like planning events.”

“That sounds like a reason.”

“It is. But I also want to look at communications and media courses. I don't know if I'd like the actual work, though.”

Saki folded her arms on the desk.

“Then go look.”

“At an open campus?”

“Yes.”

“I was thinking about it.”

“You say that a lot.”

Akari smiled.

“I know.”

She had spent much of the previous week looking through university brochures. Her family had never told her that she had to choose a particular school. They had suggested programmes, shared information, and offered to arrange visits. Their expectations were real, but they were not commands.

That made it harder to dismiss them.

At home, her mother was arranging flowers in the dining room when Akari brought up the open-campus dates.

“There's a communications programme at Seiran University,” Akari said. “They have an open day next month.”

Her mother looked up.

“Would you like to visit?”

“Yes. I also want to look at the business programme at the same university.”

“That's sensible. You can compare them.”

Akari had expected a question about why she was considering something different. Instead, her mother asked what time the event started.

Her father joined them a few minutes later. He listened while Akari explained the two programmes and the subjects she wanted to compare.

“I think it's worth looking at both,” he said. “You don't have to choose based on the name of the course. Ask about what students actually study.”

“I will.”

“And if you decide that neither is right, that's useful information too.”

Akari nodded.

Her mother placed the flower scissors on the table.

“We suggested business because we thought it would suit your interests and give you flexibility. But you should understand what you're choosing.”

“I know.”

“You don't have to agree with us just because we've suggested it.”

Akari looked at her mother.

There was no dramatic pause. Her mother simply returned to the flowers, adjusting one stem so it sat more naturally beside the others.

“Thank you,” Akari said.

“Of course.”

Later that evening, Akari opened the questionnaire again. She did not fill in the two boxes. Instead, she wrote a list of questions on the back: required subjects, practical projects, internship options, and how much freedom students had to choose electives.

She photographed the list and sent it to Saki.

Akari: I made a plan.

Saki: Is it a plan or a list?

Akari: A list that could become a plan.

Saki: Very convincing.

Akari smiled.

She opened another message thread.

Akari: Kurose-kun, did you look at the career information sheet yet?

His reply arrived a little later.

Itsuki: I looked at it.

Akari: Any useful conclusions?

Itsuki: It contains many boxes.

Akari: I knew you'd understand.

Itsuki: I'm considering writing that down as my first choice.

Akari laughed quietly.

Akari: I'll let you know if I find a better answer.

She put the phone down and returned to the list.

The two boxes could wait.

The next day, Akari was asked to help with another school activity.

A classmate approached her near the shoe lockers with a clipboard and an apologetic smile. The student council was preparing a small information corner for the upcoming guidance week, and they wanted someone who could explain the schedule to first-year students.

“You'd be good at it,” the classmate said. “You know how to talk to people.”

Akari smiled automatically.

“I can help.”

Then she remembered the open-campus information she still wanted to read, and the questions she had promised herself she would ask before the next family discussion.

“Actually, could I help for one afternoon instead of the whole week?” she asked. “I have some university information sessions to prepare for.”

The classmate blinked, then checked the schedule.

“That should be fine. We can ask someone else to cover the other days.”

“Thank you. I can still help make the information sheet.”

“That would be great.”

When the classmate left, Akari looked at Saki, who had been standing beside her.

“You said no,” Saki observed.

“I offered a different amount.”

“That's a very polite way to say no.”

Akari laughed.

“I still wanted to help.”

“I know. You just didn't promise the entire week.”

Akari looked toward the notice board.

“I used to think that if I could do something, I should.”

“You can do a lot of things.”

“That's the problem.”

Saki smiled.

“Then choose the ones you actually want to do.”

Akari nodded.

She was still going to help with the information corner. She liked that kind of work. But she had also kept time for something that mattered to her, and the school had not fallen apart because she had asked someone else to take a turn.

It was a small discovery.

Later that week, a classmate noticed the university brochure in Akari's folder.

“Your parents probably already know where you'll go,” she said. “Your family has so many connections.”

Akari smiled politely.

“They've given me suggestions. I still have to choose.”

“I guess it's easier when people can introduce you to everyone.”

“It can help,” Akari said. “But it doesn't tell me what I want to study.”

The classmate nodded and moved on. The conversation had been brief, and the other girl had not meant anything unkind. Still, Akari found herself thinking about it after the bell.

She liked being recognised. She liked that people remembered her name and came to her when they needed help. What she disliked was the way some people treated her future as if it had already been arranged somewhere outside her reach.

When she told Saki about it, Saki listened.

“You can enjoy being known without letting everyone decide what that means,” she said.

Akari looked at her.

“That's a good sentence.”

“I have those sometimes.”

“I'll write it down.”

Saki laughed.

Chapter 5 The Quiet Table

The library table near the windows had become an ordinary meeting place.

Itsuki noticed this on a Tuesday afternoon when he arrived and found Mio already there. She had placed two books on the table, one beside her notebook and the other on the empty chair opposite her.

“That's mine?” he asked.

“I thought you might want to read it.”

He picked it up.

The cover showed a row of houses beside a railway line. The title was printed in small letters near the bottom.

“What is it about?”

“People who live near a station.”

“That's broad.”

“It's a collection of short stories.”

“That's less broad.”

“You're welcome.”

Itsuki sat down and opened the first page.

They read for a while. Mio worked through a chapter in her own book, occasionally making a small note in the margin of her notebook. Itsuki read the first story, then turned back to the opening page to check a detail he thought he had missed.

“Do you like it?” Mio asked.

“I haven't decided.”

“That's fair.”

“The first story ends before it explains why the neighbour stopped using the station.”

“I noticed.”

“Was that intentional?”

“I think so. The next story might answer it.”

Itsuki looked at her.

“You read it already?”

“Yes.”

“Then why ask me?”

“I wanted to know whether you would notice.”

He returned to the page.

“That's a strange test.”

“It wasn't a test.”

“It sounds like one.”

“Then you passed.”

They continued reading.

When the library announcement reminded students that the study area would close in twenty minutes, Mio put her bookmark between the pages.

“Do you want to get something to drink?” she asked.

“Sure.”

They went to the vending machines near the entrance. Mio chose hot tea. Itsuki chose the same, partly because the other options were cold and partly because he did not want to spend five minutes deciding.

“You always choose tea,” Mio said.

“It's reliable.”

“So is water.”

“Water doesn't come in a can.”

“It could.”

“That sounds unnecessary.”

Mio considered the vending machine.

“I think I saw bottled water in a can once.”

“Then I hope it was an emergency.”

She looked at him.

“You make ordinary things sound unreasonable.”

“I try to be consistent.”

They stood near the window while the drinks cooled. Outside, the sky had turned pale grey. A few leaves had gathered along the pavement near the bicycle racks.

“Do you usually stay at the library until it closes?” Itsuki asked.

“Not every day.”

“What do you do when you don't?”

“Sometimes I go home. Sometimes I visit the bookshop. Sometimes I walk around.”

“Alone?”

“Usually.”

She said it without embarrassment.

“Do you mind?” he asked.

Mio looked at the drink in her hands.

“No. I like being alone when I've chosen it.”

Itsuki remembered their conversation in the summer.

“And when you haven't?”

“Then I should probably tell someone.”

He nodded.

“That sounds useful.”

“It's still something I'm learning.”

They returned to the table and packed their books. At the entrance, Mio checked the time.

“I'll be here Thursday.”

“For the book?”

“For the book. And because I have a free period.”

“All right.”

She looked at him.

“You can come too, if you want.”

“I probably will.”

Mio nodded.

They separated outside the library.

Itsuki walked toward the station with the borrowed book inside his bag. He had not been asked to investigate anything. He had not needed to explain why he was quiet. They had simply read, talked, and bought tea from a machine.

It was becoming familiar.

He did not mind that.

On the following Thursday, Itsuki arrived at the library before Mio.

He chose the window table and opened the essay collection she had lent him. The book had a narrow bookmark tucked into the middle, with a small note written on it.

Page 46 — the sentence about the station clock.

He turned to the page.

The sentence described a clock that had stopped at 4:17. The people who used the station had grown accustomed to checking their phones instead, so no one had complained for several weeks.

Itsuki read the paragraph again.

“You found it,” Mio said from behind him.

He looked up.

“The clock?”

“The sentence.”

She sat down and placed her bag beside the chair.

“I thought you might like it.”

“It's good.”

“Why?”

“The clock stopped, but the station didn't. Everyone found another way to check the time.”

Mio nodded.

“The author never says whether the clock gets repaired.”

“Maybe it does.”

“Maybe.”

Itsuki closed the book.

“Do you keep notes like this for every book?”

“Not every book. Only the ones I want to remember.”

“How many?”

“A lot.”

She opened her notebook and showed him a page of titles, dates, and short observations. Some were about books. Others were about places where she had read them: the library window, a bench near the river, a café table that wobbled whenever someone moved their elbow.

“You record where you read them too?”

“Sometimes. It helps me remember the day.”

Itsuki looked at the page.

“That's more organised than my calendar.”

“Your calendar has groceries.”

“Groceries are important.”

“I didn't say they weren't.”

She turned to a clean page.

“I used to write down small things because I didn't want to forget them. Then I started doing it because I liked noticing them.”

“What's the difference?”

Mio thought for a moment.

“The first is about keeping something. The second is about being there when it happens.”

Itsuki considered that.

“You can do both.”

“I know.”

She smiled faintly.

They spent the next hour reading. When the librarian announced the closing time, Mio put her notebook away and asked whether Itsuki would be there the following week.

“Probably.”

“I'll bring another book.”

“Then I'll try to finish this one.”

“You don't have to rush.”

“I wasn't planning to.”

They left the library together.

Chapter 6 The Street in the Photograph

Ayane invited Itsuki to her family's house on the last Saturday of October.

Her message was brief.

Ayane: My parents are sorting the old neighbourhood photographs tomorrow. Would you like to help us identify a few places?

Itsuki replied that he could come in the afternoon.

He arrived at three with a small box of sweets from the station bakery. Ayane's mother greeted him at the door and thanked him for coming. The house smelled of tea and something baking in the kitchen.

“Ayane is in the living room,” she said. “Her father has spread everything across the table.”

“That sounds organised.”

Her mother laughed.

“It was organised for about five minutes.”

Itsuki removed his shoes and followed the hallway.

The living room table was covered with photographs, envelopes, and a large notebook. Ayane sat on the floor beside one stack, sorting pictures by location. Her father was standing near the cabinet, looking through a folder.

“Itsuki,” Ayane said. “You came.”

“I said I would.”

“I know. Thank you.”

Her father greeted him and moved a stack of photographs to make room.

“We're trying to identify the old shopping street. Some of these were taken before the station renovation.”

Itsuki sat down.

“I'll see what I recognise.”

They began with a photograph of the river path. The trees were smaller, the railing had a different shape, and the path itself was narrower. Itsuki recognised the bridge but not the shop visible in the background.

Ayane wrote a note.

“The bridge is certain. The shop isn't.”

Her father nodded.

“We'll leave that one open.”

They moved through several more photographs. Itsuki identified a corner near the station and a small playground that had since been rebuilt. Ayane recognised a bakery sign from the colour of its awning. Her father supplied the year for a picture of the old bus terminal.

Then Ayane took out a smaller envelope.

“These are from our families,” she said. “My mother found them in the cabinet.”

Itsuki looked at the photographs.

One showed a group of adults standing outside a house. Another showed several children around a low table. He recognised the living room immediately.

“That's your old carpet,” he said.

Ayane looked closer.

“You're right.”

The photograph showed Itsuki and Ayane sitting beside a half-built tower of wooden blocks. A younger Nanami was not in the picture; she had not yet been born. Itsuki's hair was shorter, and he was looking at the blocks with a seriousness that seemed disproportionate to the task.

Ayane laughed.

“You were always careful with those.”

“They fell over easily.”

“You used to make me wait until you finished the bottom row.”

“It needed to be straight.”

“I remember.”

She placed the photograph beside another.

This one showed them outside near the river. Ayane was holding a small pinwheel, and Itsuki stood beside her with a paper bag in his hand. Both were looking toward someone outside the frame.

“I don't remember this day,” Itsuki said.

“Neither do I.”

Her father leaned over.

“That was the neighbourhood summer event. You both wanted to go to the river after the food stalls closed.”

“I remember the river,” Ayane said. “Not the pinwheel.”

Itsuki looked at the paper bag.

“I might have been carrying snacks.”

“You usually were,” her father said.

Ayane smiled.

“You were always hungry.”

“I was growing.”

“You were seven.”

“A demanding age.”

They continued sorting.

After a while, Ayane's father was called into the kitchen to answer a phone call. Her mother came in with tea and placed a plate of small cakes on the table.

“Take a break,” she said. “You can finish the rest later.”

Ayane looked at the remaining photographs.

“We still have a lot.”

“They aren't going anywhere.”

Her mother smiled and returned to the kitchen.

Ayane picked up one of the photographs.

“I used to think I remembered every afternoon we spent together.”

Itsuki took a sip of tea.

“That's a lot of afternoons.”

“I know. But I remembered the feeling more than the details.”

She turned the photograph over. There was no writing on the back.

“Sometimes I remember something and then find out it happened on a different day. Or that you remember it differently.”

“That happens.”

“I don't want to keep asking you to remember things just because I miss them.”

Itsuki looked at her.

Ayane's voice remained quiet.

“I like knowing we had those days. But I don't want to use them as a reason you have to spend time with me now.”

He set his cup down.

“You don't have to.”

“I know.”

“I come because I want to.”

Ayane looked up.

He had not intended the sentence to sound important. It was simply the truth.

Her expression eased.

“Thank you.”

“You don't need to thank me every time.”

“I know.”

She smiled.

“I'll try to remember that too.”

They returned to the photographs. When Itsuki left later that afternoon, Ayane walked him to the entrance.

“Would you like to have lunch together next week?” she asked.

“Sure.”

“I'll message you.”

“All right.”

He stepped outside.

The wind chime near the door moved once, making the same small sound he remembered from years ago.

Itsuki walked toward the station.

The past had not become less important.

It had simply stopped being the only place they could meet.

Two days later, Ayane sent him a photograph of the same street from a different angle.

Ayane: My father found this one in another envelope. I think it's from the same year.

Itsuki enlarged the image. The photograph showed the old shopping street from the river side. A bicycle leaned against the wall, and a shopkeeper was pulling down a striped awning.

Itsuki: The florist is on the left.

Ayane: You can tell?

Itsuki: The wall has the same crack. The sign is different now.

Ayane: That's impressive.

Itsuki: I walk past it.

Ayane: Would you mind showing me the place sometime? I want to check whether the wall is still there.

Itsuki looked at the calendar. The next few days were busy with school, but the following Sunday was free.

Itsuki: Sunday afternoon?

Ayane: That works. Thank you.

They met on Sunday near the florist. The wall was still there, though a new sign covered part of the old brickwork. Ayane compared the photograph with the street, then took a picture of the current view.

“It's strange,” she said. “I remember this being a much bigger street.”

“We were smaller.”

She laughed. “That's probably true.”

They walked slowly along the pavement. The florist had changed its display, and the bakery on the corner had a new awning. A small restaurant had replaced the old stationery shop.

“I thought the shop was still here,” Ayane said.

“It closed a few years ago.”

“I didn't notice.”

“You don't come this way often.”

She nodded.

“I suppose I remembered the street as it was when we used to visit.”

Itsuki looked at the shopfronts.

“It's still the same street.”

“But not the same place.”

“Not exactly.”

Ayane stopped beside the old wall. She took another photograph, then lowered her phone.

“I think I want to study something connected to places like this,” she said. “Not only history. Maybe community planning, or how local areas change.”

“That sounds interesting.”

“I haven't looked into it properly.”

“You could.”

She smiled.

“You always make the next step sound simple.”

“It usually starts with looking.”

“That's true.”

They continued toward the river. Ayane asked what Itsuki liked about the neighbourhood now. He mentioned the bookshop near the station, the small park behind the supermarket, and a café that had good tea but uncomfortable chairs.

“You notice more than you think,” she said.

“I live here.”

“You keep using that as an explanation.”

“It's a useful one.”

Ayane laughed.

At the river path, they stopped beside the railing. The water was calm, and a few leaves had collected near the bank.

“I'm glad we came,” Ayane said.

“Me too.”

She looked at him, then smiled without adding another question.

They walked back toward the station together.

Interlude II The Form on the Kitchen Table

Riko placed the school notice on the kitchen table before her mother could ask about it.

She had considered leaving it in her bag until after dinner. She had also considered asking Itsuki to check the form first, though she knew that would only delay the conversation by another day.

Her mother looked at the paper.

“What's this?”

“A notice from school.”

“About?”

“Mathematics.”

Her mother pulled out a chair.

Riko sat opposite her.

She explained the assessment result, the missing assignments, and the support sessions. She did not make a joke about the score. She did not blame the teacher or the wording of the questions. She told her mother that she had not been keeping up consistently and that the school wanted her to complete the work before the next assessment.

Her mother listened without interrupting.

“Why didn't you tell us when the earlier assignments were missing?”

Riko looked at the notice.

“I thought I could catch up.”

“Could you?”

“Not properly.”

Her mother folded her hands on the table.

“I don't want you to hide things until they become bigger problems.”

“I know.”

“Your father and I want you to do well. We also need to know when you need help.”

Riko nodded.

“I'm going to the sessions.”

“Good.”

Her mother read the schedule and signed the form. She asked whether Riko had enough time after school to attend the sessions and finish the remaining assignments.

“I think so. I'll have to move a few things around.”

“Then make a schedule you can actually follow.”

Riko almost smiled.

“Not one with three colours?”

“If it helps.”

“The last one was mostly pink.”

“I remember.”

Her mother slid the signed notice back across the table.

“You can do better than this result. But I don't want you to study only because we're watching.”

Riko looked up.

“I don't want that either.”

Her mother nodded.

“Then we'll talk again after the first session.”

She stood and returned to the kitchen.

Riko remained at the table for a moment.

She had expected disappointment. It was there, perhaps, but it had not become anger. Her mother had asked questions, listened to the answers, and signed the form.

It was not a disaster.

It was also not something she could laugh away.

Riko took out her phone.

Riko: Told my mother. She signed it.

Itsuki replied a few minutes later.

Itsuki: Good.

Riko: That's all?

Itsuki: You wanted me to say good job?

Riko: Maybe.

Itsuki: Good job telling her.

Riko looked at the message.

Then she put the phone down and opened the mathematics workbook.

There was still work to do.

Chapter 7 The Work She Chose

Riko's first support session was held in an ordinary classroom after school.

There were six students, two teachers, and a stack of worksheets arranged at the front. The room was quiet except for the sound of pencils and the occasional page turning.

Riko took a seat near the window.

She had brought her workbook, the missing assignments, and a pencil case with more pens than she needed.

Mr. Hasegawa checked the attendance list.

“We'll work through the questions you marked. If you understand a step, write it down. If you don't, ask. Don't spend twenty minutes pretending the page is going to explain itself.”

Riko looked at the worksheet.

“That's very specific,” she murmured.

The student beside her gave a quiet laugh.

They began with equations and moved on to word problems. Riko made mistakes. She also corrected several without help. When she became stuck, she raised her hand rather than turning the page.

It was slower than she wanted.

It was more useful than she expected.

At the end of the session, Mr. Hasegawa checked her work.

“This is better. You showed the steps instead of jumping to the answer.”

“I was told to.”

“And you listened.”

“Sometimes.”

He smiled.

“Keep doing it.”

Riko packed her books and left the classroom.

Itsuki was waiting near the shoe lockers. He had stayed to return a library book and was checking a message on his phone.

“You waited,” she said.

“I was already here.”

“That's not what I asked.”

“I waited.”

She smiled.

“How was your afternoon?”

“Quiet.”

“Good. Mine was full of equations.”

“How did it go?”

“I did the work.”

“That's good.”

Riko walked beside him toward the entrance.

“I think I understand why I kept getting the same questions wrong.”

“Why?”

“I knew what to do when the example looked like the one before it. When the wording changed, I tried to guess which method the teacher wanted instead of reading the conditions.”

Itsuki nodded.

“That makes sense.”

“I still don't like it.”

“You don't have to like it.”

“You make a terrible motivational speaker.”

“I wasn't applying.”

She laughed.

They reached the shoe lockers. Riko changed into her outdoor shoes and took the signed notice from her folder.

“My mother asked me to make a schedule.”

“Will you?”

“Yes. A realistic one.”

“Good.”

She looked at him.

“I don't want you to think I'm only doing this because the school told me to.”

“I don't.”

“You don't?”

“You could have ignored the form.”

“I couldn't. My mother signed it.”

“You could still have done the minimum.”

Riko considered that.

“I suppose.”

They stepped outside. The air had cooled, and the sky was already beginning to darken. A few students were leaving the sports field with club bags over their shoulders.

“Do you want to get something to eat?” Riko asked.

Itsuki glanced at the time.

“I have to go to the supermarket.”

“Then I'll come with you.”

“Why?”

“Because I want to.”

He looked at her.

Riko's expression was casual, but she did not turn it into a joke.

“All right,” he said.

They walked toward the station district. Riko told him about a classmate who had fallen asleep during the support session and woken up when the teacher said the word “fraction.” Itsuki asked whether the student had understood the problem. Riko said she had not checked.

At the supermarket, they bought rice, vegetables, and a carton of milk. Riko picked up a packet of biscuits and placed it in the basket.

“Those aren't on your list,” Itsuki said.

“They're for morale.”

“Whose?”

“Mine.”

“Then you can pay for them.”

“Cruel.”

She put the biscuits back, then picked up a smaller packet.

“These are within the budget.”

“Your budget?”

“My emotional budget.”

Itsuki did not argue.

At the checkout, Riko paid for the biscuits herself.

Outside, she held up the small bag.

“A responsible purchase.”

“Congratulations.”

“You're learning.”

They reached the crossing and stopped beneath the traffic light.

“Thanks for coming with me,” Riko said.

“You came with me.”

“I know.”

She smiled, then looked away.

For once, the joke did not arrive immediately.

Itsuki noticed.

He did not ask her to explain.

The signal changed, and they crossed with the crowd.

On Saturday, Riko went to the city library by herself.

She had told her parents she wanted to finish the next set of mathematics exercises before dinner. Her mother had offered to sit with her, but Riko had said she would be fine. She wanted to see whether she could work through the questions without someone checking every line.

She chose a table near the back and opened the workbook.

The first two questions were familiar. The third involved a train travelling at a constant speed, with a stop in the middle of the route. Riko read the problem, wrote down the given information, and drew a small line beneath the value she needed to find.

She nearly skipped a condition in the second paragraph.

Then she stopped.

“Read the question,” she whispered.

She went back and read it again.

The problem was not asking for the total time. It was asking for the time spent moving, excluding the stop. The distinction was small, but it changed the calculation.

Riko wrote the steps down.

Her first answer was wrong. She checked the units and found that she had added minutes to an hour without converting them. She corrected it and tried again.

This time, the answer matched the back of the workbook.

She looked at it for a moment.

“Huh.”

The librarian glanced over.

Riko lowered her voice.

She completed two more questions before taking a break. When she returned, she worked through the final problem slowly, writing each step in the margin.

She did not finish the entire section.

She had, however, completed the part she planned to do.

At home, her father asked how the study session had gone.

“I got through four questions.”

“All correct?”

“Three. I corrected the other one.”

He nodded.

“Good work.”

Riko placed the workbook on the table.

“I want to keep the Saturday library session for a while. It helps me concentrate.”

“Then we'll keep it in the schedule.”

Her mother asked whether she wanted a snack before dinner.

“Yes, please.”

Riko went to wash her hands.

She had not become good at mathematics in one afternoon. She had not stopped making mistakes. But she had found a way to recognise one of the habits that kept getting in her way, and she had corrected it without waiting for someone else to point it out.

That felt better than a lucky score.

Chapter 8 The Campus on the Hill

Seiran University's open campus was held on Saturday, November 7.

Akari arrived with Saki and a folder containing the two programme brochures she had printed at home. The university stood on a hill beyond the central station, with a broad entrance path lined by trees and a row of buildings that looked newer than the schools she was used to visiting.

“This is bigger than I expected,” Saki said.

“You said you wanted to see the campus.”

“I did. I didn't expect to need a map.”

Akari checked the programme sheet.

“The communications session starts in twenty minutes.”

“Then let's go.”

They followed the signs to a lecture room where a group of students were waiting. A staff member handed them a booklet and explained the schedule. The session covered the course structure, practical projects, and the kinds of work graduates sometimes pursued.

Akari listened carefully.

She liked the idea of studying how people communicated, how information moved between groups, and how public events were planned. The course included writing, research, presentation work, and collaborative projects.

It also included subjects she had not expected.

After the session, she asked a student guide how much freedom students had to choose electives.

“Quite a bit after the first year,” the guide said. “But the required courses are important. You should look at the full curriculum before deciding.”

“Thank you.”

Saki waited until they were outside.

“You looked interested.”

“I was.”

“More than the business session?”

Akari considered it.

“In a different way.”

They visited the business programme next. The presentation was clear and practical. It covered management, finance, marketing, and the role of organisations in local communities. Akari recognised several topics her parents had mentioned before.

She liked some of them.

She also found herself thinking about the communications session.

At lunch, they sat outside near the courtyard. Students were handing out campus maps, and a small group was performing music near the main building.

“So?” Saki asked.

Akari opened her folder.

“I don't know yet.”

“That's allowed.”

“I know. I just thought one of them would feel obviously right.”

“Maybe that's not how it works.”

Akari smiled.

“You sound like my mother.”

“Your mother seems sensible.”

“She is.”

They finished lunch and visited the library. Akari asked about student projects and the possibility of joining an event-planning committee. The staff member explained that students could apply for several volunteer programmes after enrolment.

Akari wrote the information in her notebook.

When they left the campus, the afternoon sun had begun to soften.

“Do you want to tell your parents?” Saki asked.

“Yes.”

“What will you say?”

Akari looked at the two brochures.

“That I want to keep both options open for now. But I want to learn more about communications before I decide.”

“Sounds good.”

“It feels strange saying it.”

“Why?”

“Because it sounds like I'm choosing something for myself.”

Saki bumped her shoulder lightly.

“You are allowed to do that.”

Akari laughed.

“I know.”

Her phone vibrated while they waited for the train. A message from her father asked how the visit had gone.

Akari: Good. I liked the communications programme. I want to compare the full course details before choosing.

His reply came a few minutes later.

Father: That sounds sensible. We can look at the information together this evening.

Akari put the phone away.

She had not chosen her future.

She had chosen what to investigate next.

For now, that was enough.

That evening, Akari showed the two brochures to her parents.

Her father placed them side by side on the dining table and read through the course summaries. Her mother brought over the notebook where Akari had written her questions.

“You have more questions about the communications course,” her mother observed.

“I think I understand the business course better because we've talked about it before.”

“That makes sense,” her father said. “Familiarity isn't the same as preference.”

Akari looked at him.

“I don't want you to think I'm rejecting what you suggested.”

“We're not asking you to accept it without thinking,” he replied.

Her mother turned a page.

“You enjoy organising events, but that doesn't mean you have to choose a course only because you're good at it. You should also find out what the daily work involves.”

“I want to.”

“Then we'll arrange another visit.”

Akari nodded.

She had expected to defend her interest. Instead, her parents were helping her examine it. Their support did not remove the pressure of choosing, but it made the decision feel like something she could approach rather than something she had to escape.

Later, Saki sent a message asking whether Akari had decided.

Akari: Not yet. But I know what I want to find out next.

Saki: That's progress.

Akari: I think so too.

She placed the brochures in a folder and left the questionnaire on her desk.

The boxes were still waiting.

She no longer felt that she had to fill them in just to make them stop looking at her.

Chapter 9 A Room Reserved for Thursday

Mio sent the message on a Tuesday evening.

Mio: The bookshop has the new edition now. Would Thursday after school work?

Itsuki was rinsing a bowl in the kitchen. He dried his hands before replying.

Itsuki: Yes. What time?

Mio: Four-thirty?

Itsuki: All right.

He put the phone on the counter and finished washing the dishes.

On Thursday, the bookshop was quieter than the previous weekend. Mio was waiting near the entrance, holding a folded umbrella and a small paper bag.

“You brought something,” Itsuki said.

“A book I borrowed from the library. I thought you might like it.”

“You carry books around in case someone wants one?”

“Only when I know someone who might.”

He accepted it.

The book was a collection of short essays about ordinary places: laundromats, bus stops, small restaurants, and the spaces between buildings. Itsuki read the title and looked at her.

“You chose this deliberately.”

“I did.”

“I see.”

“You don't have to like it.”

“I'll read it first.”

They went upstairs to the window seats. Mio found the new edition she had been looking for and compared the opening pages with the older copy on her phone. The text had not changed, but the introduction included a note from the editor.

“They added a short explanation of why the stories were arranged this way,” she said.

“Does it help?”

“A little. I liked the old order, though.”

“Then you can keep both.”

“That's an expensive solution.”

“You don't have to buy it.”

“I know.”

She put the new edition back into its paper sleeve.

They sat by the window with their drinks. Mio asked about the book he had bought at the October event. Itsuki said he had finished it and liked the chapter about the old station more than the one about the new apartment buildings.

“Why?” she asked.

“The author noticed things people use without thinking about them. The old station had a bench with a broken corner. Everyone knew where it was, so they sat around it instead of on it.”

“Did the author explain why?”

“No. That was the good part.”

Mio nodded.

“I like when a book trusts the reader to notice something.”

“You notice a lot.”

“Not everything.”

“No one does.”

She looked at the paper sleeve in her hands.

“I used to think I could understand a situation if I collected enough details.”

“And now?”

“I think details can point in several directions.”

Itsuki considered that.

“That sounds inconvenient.”

“It is.”

She smiled faintly.

“But it's more accurate.”

They stayed until the shop began closing its upper floor. Outside, the sky had turned dark enough for the streetlights to come on.

“Would you like to come next Thursday too?” Mio asked.

“If I'm free.”

“You usually are.”

“That's not guaranteed.”

“I'll ask anyway.”

“Then I'll answer.”

She nodded.

They walked toward the station together. At the entrance, Mio turned toward her platform.

“Good night, Kurose.”

“Good night.”

Itsuki watched her go down the stairs.

He put the borrowed book into his bag.

There was a small paper marker tucked between the pages. Mio had written the title of another book on it.

He smiled faintly.

Then he went to catch his train.

Chapter 10 The Envelope Between Them

Ayane brought the old photographs to school on a Wednesday.

She had placed them inside a wide paper envelope, with a sheet of cardboard to keep the corners from bending. The neighbourhood association wanted to use a few of the images for Nanami's inter-school reading project, and Ayane had offered to help identify the locations and write short captions.

Itsuki found her near the classroom windows before homeroom.

“You brought them?”

“Yes. My father found another folder last night.”

She held up the envelope.

“There are more than I expected.”

Itsuki looked at the stack inside.

“That's a lot of history.”

“Most of it is the shopping street.”

“Then it's a lot of shops.”

Ayane smiled.

“Would you have time to look at them after school? I want to check which ones you recognise before I make the captions.”

“Sure.”

“Thank you.”

They arranged to meet in the library after the final lesson.

During lunch, Riko noticed the envelope on Ayane's desk.

“Are those secret documents?”

“Old photographs,” Ayane replied.

“That's less exciting.”

“They're for a local-history display.”

Riko leaned over to look at the top picture.

“Is that the station?”

“Before the renovation.”

“It looks smaller.”

“It was.”

Riko looked at Itsuki.

“Were you alive then?”

“Yes.”

“I was checking.”

“Thank you.”

Riko laughed and returned to her desk.

After school, Itsuki met Ayane at the library. They took a table near the reference shelves and spread the photographs across the surface. Ayane had brought a notebook with a list of places the association had already identified.

“This one is the old post office,” she said.

Itsuki looked at the picture.

“I think so. The sign changed, but the doorway is the same.”

Ayane wrote it down.

They worked through the envelope one photograph at a time. Some were easy. Others showed buildings that had been replaced or roads that had changed direction. Itsuki recognised the wall near the bakery and the narrow lane behind the florist. Ayane remembered which families had lived above certain shops.

“You know more than you think,” she said.

“I walk around.”

“That's how you know.”

They were comparing a picture of the river path when Mio entered the library.

She saw them from the doorway.

Itsuki had leaned over the table to point at a corner of the photograph. Ayane was holding the envelope open, listening. The two of them were close enough to read the small writing on the back of the image.

Mio stopped for a moment.

She recognised the photographs. Nanami had mentioned that her school was preparing a local-story display with help from the other school. Mio had not known Itsuki was involved.

Ayane said something too quietly for Mio to hear.

Itsuki replied, “I said I'd help.”

Ayane smiled.

“I'm glad you did.”

Mio looked at the table again.

She knew they had been childhood friends. She knew their families were close. She had seen them talk at school and had heard Itsuki mention the bakery visit.

She did not know what the photographs meant to them.

She could have walked over and asked.

Instead, she went to the return desk and placed her books on the counter.

Itsuki did not see her leave.

When Mio had returned her books and gone upstairs to the quiet reading area, she opened her notebook.

She wrote the date.

Then she closed it again.

She had no reason to feel unsettled.

She knew that.

But she had begun to expect the Thursday meetings. She had begun to think of the bookshop as a place where Itsuki would probably be, if she asked.

Perhaps she had mistaken convenience for something dependable.

Perhaps he had only been coming because no one else had asked him to do something more familiar.

Mio looked at the blank page.

She had collected two details and made a conclusion from them.

She knew better.

She did not ask anyway.

When she left the library, Itsuki was still at the table with Ayane, sorting the last few photographs.

He did not notice her pass the entrance.

That evening, Mio sent him a message.

Mio: I don't think I can make it tomorrow. Something came up.

Itsuki replied after a few minutes.

Itsuki: All right. Is everything okay?

She read the message.

Then she turned off her screen without answering.

Chapter 11 The Message Left Unanswered

Itsuki noticed the change on Thursday.

He had gone to the bookshop after school anyway. He needed a notebook, and the shop was on the way to the station. The upper floor was quiet. The window seats were occupied by two students from another school, and the table Mio usually chose was empty.

He bought a notebook and stood near the staircase for a moment.

There was no reason to wait.

He left.

At home, he sent Mio another message.

Itsuki: Did you find the book you wanted?

Her reply arrived later that evening.

Mio: Not yet. I'll look another time.

It was a normal answer.

It was also shorter than usual.

Itsuki looked at the screen, then put the phone down.

He did not know what had changed.

On Friday, he saw Mio in the library. She was sitting near the reference shelves with a book open in front of her. He approached the table.

“Kanzaki.”

She looked up.

“Kurose.”

“Are you busy?”

“Not particularly.”

He pointed to the empty chair.

“Can I sit?”

“Of course.”

He sat down.

For a moment, neither spoke.

“Did you find the book?” he asked.

“No.”

“The one from the event?”

“Yes.”

“I thought you said the shop had it.”

“They had one copy. It was reserved.”

“I see.”

Mio turned a page.

Itsuki looked at the book, then at her.

“Did something happen?”

“No.”

“You cancelled yesterday.”

“I couldn't make it.”

“All right.”

He waited.

Mio kept her eyes on the page.

He could have asked again. He could have suggested another day. He could have assumed she was busy and left her alone.

Instead, he closed his own book.

“If you don't want to meet, you can tell me.”

Mio looked up immediately.

“That's not it.”

“Then what is it?”

She hesitated.

“I thought you might have other plans.”

“I usually have plans.”

“I know.”

“That doesn't mean I don't want to meet.”

Mio looked down again.

“I saw you with Fujimoto-san on Wednesday.”

Itsuki considered the answer.

“The photographs?”

She nodded.

“I thought you were spending more time together. You have known each other for a long time. Your families are close. I assumed you were rebuilding that connection.”

“We were sorting photographs for Nanami's project.”

“I know that now.”

“You didn't ask.”

“No.”

Itsuki rested his hands on the table.

“You thought I wouldn't want to keep meeting you?”

Mio was quiet for a moment.

“I thought I might be asking for time that you would rather spend elsewhere.”

“You asked whether I wanted to go to a bookshop.”

“Yes.”

“I said yes.”

“I remember.”

“I still meant it.”

Mio looked at him.

Itsuki adjusted his glasses.

“Ayane and I are getting to know each other again. That doesn't mean I have to stop knowing other people.”

“I didn't think you had to.”

“You acted as if you did.”

She lowered her eyes.

“I know.”

There was no accusation in his voice. That made the words harder to dismiss.

Mio closed her book.

“I saw something and decided what it meant without asking. I thought I was being considerate by not interrupting.”

“You could have asked.”

“I could have.”

“You can still ask.”

She looked at him.

For a moment, she seemed to be deciding whether to use the opportunity.

“Do you still want to go to the bookshop next week?” she asked.

Itsuki answered without hesitation.

“Yes.”

Mio's shoulders eased.

“Then I'd like to go too.”

“All right.”

They sat quietly for a moment.

Then Mio said, “I'm sorry.”

“It's okay.”

“I don't think I should have made you guess why I was avoiding you.”

“I didn't know I was supposed to guess.”

She looked at him.

“That's fair.”

Itsuki opened his book again.

“You can tell me if something bothers you.”

“I will try.”

“You don't have to make it sound like homework.”

Mio's expression shifted.

“I'll try not to.”

They returned to their books.

The library remained quiet around them. A student at the next table turned a page. The librarian moved a trolley between the shelves. Outside, the afternoon light rested on the window frame.

Nothing had been solved permanently.

But the question had finally been asked.

Interlude III The Answer She Didn't Send

Mio Kanzaki did not like leaving messages unanswered.

She liked even less knowing that she had chosen not to answer one.

At home, the apartment was quiet. Her mother had returned from work earlier than usual and was preparing dinner. Her father was still at the office, though he had sent a message saying he would be home before nine.

Mio put her bag beside the desk and took out the book she had borrowed from Itsuki.

She opened it to the page she had marked the previous evening.

Then she closed it again.

Her mother looked into the room.

“You're home.”

“Yes.”

“How was school?”

“Normal.”

Her mother smiled.

“You always say that.”

“It usually is.”

“Did anything interesting happen?”

Mio considered the question.

“I saw someone I know with an old friend.”

“And?”

“I thought I understood what it meant.”

Her mother waited.

“But I didn't ask.”

“Did you want to?”

Mio looked down at the book.

“I think so.”

Her mother returned to the kitchen, then called back a moment later.

“If you want to know something, you can ask. You don't have to wait until you're certain the answer will be comfortable.”

Mio did not reply immediately.

“I know.”

She sat at her desk and opened the message thread with Itsuki. His last question was still there.

Itsuki: Is everything okay?

She had read it several times and answered none of them.

She typed a reply.

Mio: I made an assumption. I should have asked you.

She looked at the sentence.

It was accurate, but incomplete.

She added:

Mio: I thought you might prefer to spend your time with someone who already knew you better.

She read it once more, then sent it.

The reply did not arrive immediately.

Mio put the phone down and went to help her mother set the table.

Her father came home at eight-forty. He asked about school, listened to her mother's account of the day, and told them about a meeting that had run longer than expected. They ate together and talked about an upcoming visit to relatives.

Nothing about the evening was unusual.

After dinner, Mio returned to her room.

Her phone had a new message.

Itsuki: You can ask me. I don't know what I would prefer unless you ask.

She read it twice.

Then she typed:

Mio: I will.

She did not add anything else.

For once, she left the message there without trying to improve it.

Chapter 12 The Question in the Margin

The following Thursday, Mio arrived at the bookshop before Itsuki.

She had chosen the same window seat, though she had left the opposite chair empty. A paper cup of tea stood beside her bag, and the book she wanted was placed on the table.

Itsuki came upstairs at four-thirty.

“You're early,” he said.

“I know.”

“The train was on time?”

“It was.”

He sat down.

For a few minutes, they talked about the book. Mio had found the edition she wanted at another shop, and she had brought it to compare the introduction with the older version.

Itsuki read the first page.

“The editor changed the order.”

“Yes. I think the old version was better.”

“You could write a complaint.”

“I could.”

“Would you?”

“No.”

“Then the publisher is safe.”

She smiled.

They browsed the shelves and bought a small collection of essays. Afterward, they returned to the window seat and read until the afternoon became evening.

At one point, Mio closed her book.

“Can I ask you something?”

Itsuki looked up.

“Yes.”

“When you and Ayane were children, were you always together?”

He considered the question.

“Often. Our families visited each other. We went to the same neighbourhood events. Sometimes we played at her house.”

“Do you remember much?”

“Some things. Not everything.”

“Does that bother you?”

“No. I don't think I need to remember every afternoon.”

Mio nodded.

“I thought you might.”

“Why?”

“Because she remembers a lot.”

Itsuki looked at the window.

“She remembers things differently from me. That's normal.”

“I suppose.”

He turned back to her.

“You can ask her too.”

“I could.”

“You don't have to ask me to find out what she thinks.”

Mio considered that.

“That's true.”

She opened her notebook and wrote something in the margin.

Itsuki looked at the page.

“What did you write?”

“A reminder.”

“About?”

“Ask the person.”

He read it again.

“That's a useful reminder.”

“I thought so.”

They returned to their books.

When the shop began closing its upper floor, Mio packed her things.

“Would you like to come again next week?”

Itsuki stood.

“If I'm free.”

“I'll ask.”

“Then I'll answer.”

She nodded.

They left the shop together. At the crossing, Mio turned toward the station.

“Good night.”

“Good night.”

Itsuki watched her walk away.

He had not known she was worried about losing their meetings. He would not have guessed it from the way she had behaved. He was glad she had said something.

He put his hands into his jacket pockets and headed toward the bus stop.

There were still things they did not know about each other.

That was not a problem.

It only meant there were more questions to ask.

Interlude IV The Space Beside the Schedule

Nanami crossed out the word reserve beside her name.

Yui watched the pencil move.

“You're taking the reading corner?”

“Yes.”

“You said you wanted to coordinate.”

“I can do both, as long as we have enough volunteers.”

“That's what I said.”

“You said I should do it.”

“Same thing.”

Nanami gave her a look.

They were in the school library, checking the preliminary schedule for the December event. The teacher had approved the working title, A Day of Local Stories, and the community library had confirmed the date. Students from Hoshimiya Municipal High School would join them for the afternoon programme.

Nanami had been asked to coordinate the student groups.

She had initially planned to remain in the background, where she could check the timetable and solve problems before anyone noticed them. Yui had pointed out that Nanami enjoyed reading to younger children and was good at making them feel comfortable.

It was a fair point.

“I want to read the first story,” Nanami said.

Yui smiled.

“Then you should.”

“I also want to make sure the younger students have a choice. Some might not want to sit in a group.”

“We can have a quiet table.”

Nanami wrote it down.

“And the local-history corner needs someone who knows the photographs.”

“Ayane's helping with that.”

“I know. We should ask whether she can bring the captions early.”

“I'll message her.”

Nanami looked at the schedule.

There were still empty spaces. The Municipal High group had not confirmed its final numbers, and the library had not yet said how many tables could be moved into the hall. The event was beginning to feel larger than the first page of the plan.

“Maybe we should ask Itsuki to help with the setup,” Yui said.

Nanami shook her head.

“Not unless we need him.”

“You're learning.”

Nanami: We're preparing the December reading day. Would you be free to help with setup? It's okay if you're busy.

Itsuki: I can help for a while. Send me the time.

She added her name beside the reading corner, then wrote Yui's name beside the display table. Beneath them, she left a blank space for the students from Municipal High.

But she had decided where she wanted to be.

Chapter 13 A Place for the Stories

The community library had fewer tables than Nanami remembered.

She stood near the entrance with a clipboard and looked into the main room. The children's shelves were still at the back, the reading rug was still beside the window, and the information desk still had a small bell that made a sound like a spoon tapping a glass. But the tables had been moved to make room for the winter reading day, and the open space looked smaller than it had on the planning sheet.

“We can make it work,” Yui said.

Nanami checked the schedule.

“We have four tables for the display, one for sign-in, and two for the activity corner. The quiet reading space is beside the shelves.”

“And the local-history photographs?”

“Along the wall. Ayane said she'll bring the captions.”

Yui looked around.

“It feels real now.”

“It is real.”

“I know. I mean, we can see it.”

Nanami smiled.

Itsuki arrived a few minutes later with a roll of tape and a small box of clips. He had agreed to help with the setup for an hour, though Nanami suspected he would stay longer if something needed doing.

“You're early,” she said.

“The bus was on time.”

“You sound like Mio.”

Itsuki looked at her.

“That's a new accusation.”

Yui greeted him and handed him a list of things to move. He took the list without complaint.

“Where do you want the tables?”

Nanami showed him the plan.

“Two here, one beside the window, and one near the shelves.”

Itsuki studied the room.

“The window table will block the notice board.”

Nanami looked again.

He was right.

“Then move it closer to the wall.”

“That will narrow the path.”

“How much?”

“Enough for someone to complain.”

Yui considered the space.

“What if we put the display table here and move the chairs to the other side?”

Itsuki looked at the suggestion.

“That works.”

Nanami crossed out one line on the plan.

“All right. We'll do that.”

They moved the tables and checked the walking space. The arrangement was not perfect, but it left room for people to pass without crowding the entrance. Nanami marked the new positions on her clipboard.

“You should write that down,” Yui said.

“I am.”

“I meant the reason.”

Nanami added a note.

Itsuki put the tape on the counter.

“What else?”

Nanami checked the volunteer list. “The local-history captions need to be attached. The reading corner needs a sign. And the activity table needs the number cards.”

“I can do the sign,” Yui said.

“I'll sort the number cards.”

Itsuki glanced at the list.

“You have to coordinate the volunteers too.”

“I know.”

“Then don't take every job.”

Nanami looked at him.

“I'm not.”

Yui raised her hand.

“You are.”

Nanami stared at her.

“You're supposed to be on my side.”

“I am. That's why I'm telling you.”

Itsuki returned to the table.

“Ask someone else to sort the cards.”

Nanami looked at the volunteer list. A student from the other school had offered to help with the activity corner. She had not assigned him anything yet.

She sent a message asking whether he could prepare the number cards with Yui.

His reply came a few minutes later.

Yes. I'll be there early.

Nanami checked the box beside the task.

“There.”

Itsuki nodded.

“Good.”

Near the entrance, a familiar voice greeted the librarian.

Ayane came in carrying a flat folder and a paper bag. She wore a dark green cardigan, and her hair was tucked behind one ear.

“Nanami-chan.”

“Ayane-san. Thank you for coming.”

Ayane placed the folder on the table.

“I brought the captions and the photographs your teacher asked for. My father checked the dates.”

Nanami opened the folder.

“These are perfect.”

“There are a few places we couldn't identify. I marked those as unknown instead of guessing.”

Itsuki looked at her.

“Good idea.”

Ayane smiled.

“I learned that from someone.”

“I don't remember teaching you anything.”

“You didn't. You just said it was better not to guess.”

“That sounds like something I would say.”

They began arranging the photographs. Nanami chose the pictures that showed the station, the river, and the old shopping street. Ayane explained the captions while Itsuki checked the placement against the wall space.

Yui came over with a sketch for the event sign.

“What do you think?”

The sign showed a small open book with a line drawing of the river running across both pages. The title, A Day of Local Stories, was written above it in neat letters.

Nanami smiled.

“I like it.”

“It's clear,” Itsuki said.

Yui looked at him.

“That's your version of a compliment.”

“It's a good sign.”

“Thank you.”

They attached it beside the entrance.

Before leaving, Nanami checked the schedule one more time. The room was beginning to look like the plan she had imagined, but there were still details to confirm. The Municipal High students had not sent their final volunteer list. The library had not confirmed whether the back room would be available for the quiet reading group.

She wrote both items at the bottom of the page.

Then she added a third.

Ask, don't assume.

Itsuki glanced at the note.

“What's that?”

“A reminder.”

“For the event?”

“For me.”

He nodded.

“Useful.”

Nanami closed the clipboard.

The event was still three weeks away.

For once, she was looking forward to the work as much as the day itself.

Chapter 14 The Half Hour We Hadn't Planned

The schedule problem appeared on Monday, November 30.

Nanami had been checking the library's room booking confirmation when she noticed that the back room was reserved until half past one on the day of the event.

The reading day was scheduled to begin at one.

She checked the date again.

Then she checked the room number.

It was the same room.

“Yui?”

Yui looked up from the display sketch.

“What?”

“The back room is booked until one-thirty.”

“For us?”

“No. Another group.”

Yui leaned over the confirmation sheet.

“But our event starts at one.”

“I know.”

“Can we use the main room?”

Nanami looked at the floor plan. The main room could hold the display and activity tables, but the quiet reading group needed a separate space. The library's small meeting room was also being used for storage.

“We could move the quiet group to the children's corner,” Yui suggested.

“That would be too close to the activity table.”

“Then we can start the reading group later.”

Nanami looked at the schedule.

“That might work.”

She checked the volunteer list. The first group of readers was supposed to begin at one-fifteen. If the room opened at one-thirty, they would need to move the first session and adjust the next one.

She could do that.

She could also move the entire event start time, but the posters had already been printed.

“I'll ask the librarian,” she said.

The librarian confirmed that the booking could not be changed. The other group had reserved the room several months earlier. However, the library could allow the reading day to use the main room from one and open the back room at one-thirty.

“We should have checked the room schedule earlier,” Nanami said.

“The booking was confirmed when the main room was approved,” the librarian replied. “The back room is separate. It's an easy detail to miss.”

Nanami thanked her and returned to the planning table.

Yui was waiting.

“What did she say?”

“We can use the main room at one. The back room opens at one-thirty.”

“Then we need a new schedule.”

Nanami nodded.

She began moving the time blocks.

After a few minutes, she realised the first reading group would overlap with the introduction. The volunteers from Municipal High had been assigned to the back room, but the main room needed someone to guide visitors and explain the display.

She looked at the volunteer list.

Several names had been marked as available, but she had not confirmed what each person expected to do.

She took out her phone and sent a message to the group.

Nanami: The back room will open at 1:30 instead of 1:00. Could everyone confirm which part of the event they can help with? I'll send the revised schedule tonight.

Replies began arriving.

One student could stay only until three. Another was happy to help with the display. A third had assumed that the reading corner would start later, because the room booking was different from the main event time.

Nanami read each reply and updated the list.

Yui watched her.

“You look less worried.”

“I'm fixing it.”

“That's not what I meant.”

Nanami looked at the schedule.

“I thought I had to solve it before telling everyone.”

“Why?”

“Because I'm coordinating.”

Yui tilted her head.

“That doesn't mean you have to do everything alone.”

Nanami smiled faintly.

“I know.”

She sent the revised schedule to the teacher and asked whether the main-room introduction could be shortened by five minutes. The teacher agreed. The librarian confirmed that the back room could be prepared while the first visitors browsed the display.

It was not the original plan.

It was workable.

That evening, Nanami called Itsuki.

“Do you still have time to help with the setup?”

“Yes.”

“The schedule changed. The back room opens later.”

“What do you need me to do?”

“Could you help move the display boards after the first group arrives?”

“Sure.”

“Thank you.”

“You sound tired.”

“I am.”

“Did something go wrong?”

“The room booking was different from what I expected. I had to move the reading sessions.”

“Can it be fixed?”

“Yes.”

“Then it's fine.”

Nanami leaned back in her chair.

“You make that sound simple.”

“It sounds like a schedule problem.”

“It is.”

“Then you fixed it.”

She smiled.

“I suppose.”

Before ending the call, she asked whether he had eaten.

“Yes.”

“Properly?”

“I had rice and vegetables.”

“Good.”

“You're becoming a teacher.”

“Someone has to do the job.”

Itsuki sighed.

Nanami laughed and ended the call.

She returned to the schedule and checked the new times.

The half hour had not been planned.

It had not ruined anything either.

She saved the file and sent the revised version to everyone.

At the next planning meeting, Nanami asked each volunteer to confirm one task they could take responsibility for.

She had prepared a short list, but she did not read it out herself. Instead, she passed the sheet around and waited while the students checked their names.

One of the Municipal High students, a boy named Kenta, raised his hand.

“I can help with the activity table, but I have to leave at three.”

Nanami checked the schedule.

“That's fine. Could you show the next volunteer where the spare cards are before you go?”

“Sure.”

Another student said she could help with sign-in but did not want to read aloud. Nanami moved her to the welcome table and asked whether she would be comfortable explaining the programme to visitors.

“Yes. I can do that.”

Yui watched Nanami update the list.

“You're asking everyone now.”

“It's easier than finding out on the day.”

“You sound like the teacher.”

“That's not a compliment.”

“It is if the teacher is organised.”

Nanami shook her head.

After the meeting, she and Yui walked to the station. They stopped at the bakery and bought two small rolls to share on the way home.

“You were good today,” Yui said.

“I just checked the list.”

Yui smiled.

“You used to decide the whole thing before asking anyone.”

Nanami looked at her.

“I did not.”

“You did.”

“I made a suggestion.”

“You made a schedule.”

Yui laughed.

Nanami took a bite of her roll.

“I think I was worried that if I didn't organise everything, something would be forgotten.”

“Something probably will be.”

Nanami looked at her.

“That's not reassuring.”

“But then we can fix it.”

Nanami considered that.

“I suppose.”

They continued toward the station.

She still liked having a plan. She still wanted the event to go well. But she was beginning to understand that a plan was supposed to help people work together, not make it unnecessary for them to speak.

“I'll send you the date.”

Itsuki sat down opposite her.

“What are you doing over the break?”

“Reading. Visiting my parents' friends. Maybe going to the bookshop.”

“That sounds busy.”

“Not particularly.”

“You have a list.”

“A short one.”

He nodded.

“What about you?” Mio asked.

“Assignments. Groceries. Nanami might come over.”

“Anything you want to do?”

Itsuki looked at the book in front of him.

“I haven't decided.”

“You could make a list.”

“I could.”

“You don't have to.”

“I know.”

Mio returned to her reading.

Itsuki opened his notebook.

He had written down several questions about university courses and post-graduation options. He had not chosen a direction, but he had decided to visit the school guidance office after the break and ask what programmes might suit his interests.

It was not a plan.

It was a step.

The final day of term arrived on December 24.

The school held a short assembly, returned the last assessment papers, and reminded students about the winter assignments. The corridors were full of conversations about plans, family visits, and whether the weather would stay cold enough for the forecasted snow.

Riko found Itsuki near the shoe lockers.

“Happy almost-holiday.”

“That's not a greeting.”

“It is now.”

She held up her workbook.

“I finished the missing assignments.”

“Good.”

“And I have the next section marked.”

“You're organised.”

“Don't sound so surprised.”

Itsuki looked at the workbook.

“You did the work.”

Riko smiled.

“I did.”

She put it away.

“Are you doing anything tomorrow?”

“Probably staying home.”

“That's not a plan.”

“It's a destination.”

“You and your destinations.”

She started toward the gate, then turned back.

“Have a good break, Itsuki.”

“You too.”

“And don't forget to answer messages.”

“I usually do.”

“Usually.”

She waved and left.

Itsuki walked to the station.

His phone vibrated before he reached the ticket gate. Nanami had sent a photograph of the blue folder from the reading day, now filled with notes and returned forms.

Nanami: The teacher said we can do another event in spring if we want.

Itsuki: Do you want to?

Nanami: Maybe. Yui already has ideas.

Itsuki: That sounds dangerous.

Nanami: It is. I'll tell you later.

He put the phone away.

At the station, he saw Akari with Saki near the ticket machines. Akari was holding a folder and speaking animatedly about something on the page. She noticed him and waved.

“Kurose-kun. Have a good break!”

“You too.”

“I'll send you the course information when I get the details.”

“All right.”

Saki smiled.

“We're going to compare notes.”

“Apparently.”

Akari laughed and returned to her conversation.

Itsuki went through the gate.

The train arrived a few minutes later.

He found a seat near the window and looked out at the city as it moved past.

There were a few days before the year ended. The school would reopen in January, and the next term would bring more forms, more decisions, and the first real pressure of graduation.

He had not found an answer to every question.

He had started asking some of them.

That seemed like progress.

Before the final bell, Itsuki found himself in the classroom with Riko, Ayane, and several other students who were finishing their winter-break notices.

Riko was trying to fold the assignment sheet into a shape that would fit inside her pencil case.

“Why are you doing that?” Itsuki asked.

“Storage.”

“You have a bag.”

“I have standards.”

Ayane looked over.

“It might be easier to put it in your folder.”

Riko considered the suggestion.

“That's annoyingly sensible.”

“Thank you,” Ayane said.

Itsuki packed his own papers.

“You could try folding it normally.”

Riko looked at him.

“I could. But then we'd lose the mystery.”

Ayane laughed.

For a few minutes, they talked about winter break. Ayane planned to help her family sort the remaining photographs. Riko intended to finish her assignments early, though she admitted that the phrase early was open to interpretation. Itsuki said he wanted to visit one of the university information sessions.

“That's good,” Ayane said.

“It's only a visit.”

“It still counts.”

Riko nodded.

“If you find a university with a course in avoiding people, tell me.”

“I'll send you the brochure.”

“Thank you.”

They left the classroom together. At the shoe lockers, Riko went toward the bus stop, while Ayane headed for the library. Itsuki walked with her as far as the entrance.

“Would you like to look at the next set of photographs after the holidays?” she asked.

“Sure.”

“I'll message you.”

“All right.”

Ayane smiled and went inside.

Itsuki continued toward the station.

The day had not ended with a promise or a dramatic farewell. It had ended with a few ordinary conversations and several plans that would need to be kept.

He found that he liked that.

Epilogue The Things We Said Before Winter

On the evening of December 24, Itsuki returned to his apartment with a bag of groceries and a message from Nanami.

Nanami: I'll come by tomorrow. Dad said dinner is on Sunday, so I have time.

Itsuki: Bring something useful.

Nanami: I am useful.

Itsuki: The groceries are useful.

Nanami: I'll bring pudding.

Itsuki: That's acceptable.

She sent a sticker of Professor Whiskers wearing a paper scarf.

Itsuki put the phone down and unpacked the groceries.

The calendar on the refrigerator had December nearly full. The reading day was marked in blue. The school deadlines were written in black. A small note beside the twenty-fourth said Term ends, and another marked Nanami's visit.

January was mostly empty.

He took the pen from its hook and opened the school information sheet he had brought home. There were several guidance sessions listed, along with a note about individual appointments.

He chose one and wrote the date on the calendar.

Ask about options.

It was a small thing.

It was something he wanted to do.

His phone vibrated again.

Mio: I found the date for the bookshop event. January 9. Would you like to go?

Itsuki looked at the calendar.

The date was free.

Itsuki: Yes. I'd like to.

He waited a moment, then sent another message.

Itsuki: What are you hoping to find there?

Mio's reply arrived a little later.

Mio: I'm not sure yet. I thought I'd look around and see what catches my attention.

Itsuki: Then tell me when you find something.

Mio: I will.

He put the phone down.

There had been a time when he thought the easiest way to avoid trouble was to let other people decide how close they wanted to be. He could answer when asked, leave when the conversation ended, and keep his own thoughts to himself.

That had worked for a while.

It had also left several things unsaid.

He was not suddenly good at explaining himself. He still needed time to understand what he wanted, and he still preferred quiet evenings to crowded rooms. But he had begun to notice when silence was useful and when it was only an excuse not to ask.

Outside, a car passed along the street. The refrigerator hummed. The kettle clicked as it cooled.

Itsuki opened the calendar once more.

January 9.

He had written the date himself.

He closed the calendar and returned the pen to its hook.

Winter had arrived.

The year was almost over, but the next one had already begun to take shape in small, ordinary ways.

There were still questions to ask.

This time, he intended to ask them.
