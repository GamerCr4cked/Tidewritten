Prologue The First Square on the Calendar

July 1 arrived without asking whether Itsuki Kurose had made plans for it.

The calendar on his refrigerator had a blue fish magnet holding it in place. June was covered in small marks: school deadlines, a library appointment, Nanami's reminder to return a borrowed umbrella, and one note in Riko's handwriting that simply said Don't forget you're a terrible influence. Itsuki had never discovered what it referred to.

July, by comparison, was empty.

He stood in front of it with a mug of tea in one hand and considered whether the blank spaces were a good sign.

“You're staring at it like it owes you money.”

Nanami sat at the low table, turning a page in the paperback she had brought. Her warm-brown hair was tied loosely behind her head, and a pencil rested across the open book. She had arrived less than twenty minutes earlier, carrying a bag of groceries and a list of things she claimed he had forgotten to buy.

“I'm checking the month.”

“It's July.”

“Thank you.”

“You're welcome. I also checked the weather. It's hot.”

Itsuki looked toward the window. The morning sky was pale, and the balcony rail had already begun to warm in the sunlight. “A thorough report.”

“I take my responsibilities seriously.”

“You wrote 'buy pudding' on my shopping list.”

“That was a responsibility.”

He returned to the kitchen. The refrigerator contained enough food for breakfast and dinner, though Nanami had added two cartons of milk and a packet of chilled noodles without asking. She did that often. Itsuki had stopped objecting because the groceries were useful and because arguing with her about a packet of noodles required more energy than accepting it.

His phone vibrated beside the sink. A message from the class group announced a short homeroom notice for the following morning. Another notification came from Riko, who had sent a photograph of a workbook held upside down.

Summer preparation. I'm already suffering.

Itsuki typed back: You're holding it upside down.

Three dots appeared, vanished, and returned.

That's not the point.

He set the phone down.

There was also a message from Mio, sent the previous evening. She had found a book she thought he might like and asked whether he had read it. The question had nothing to do with the festival rumour. Itsuki had noticed that before answering. He was still getting used to the distinction.

“You have messages,” Nanami said.

“I can see that.”

“You have the face you make when you don't know which one to answer first.”

“I have one face.”

“No. You have at least four. This is the one where you pretend a phone is a difficult household appliance.”

Itsuki picked it up. “I'm answering Riko.”

“Of course you are.”

He sent a second message, this time advising her to rotate the workbook. Nanami returned to her book, satisfied with whatever conclusion she had reached.

Outside, a bicycle passed along the street. Somewhere below, a delivery truck reversed with a series of electronic beeps. The city had settled into summer without ceremony.

Itsuki looked at the calendar again.

He had wanted a quiet final year. The first three months had not exactly cooperated. Still, the rumour was becoming old news, the festival had passed, and the people who had entered his routine were beginning to feel less like interruptions.

He took a pen from the drawer.

July was still mostly empty.

For now, he left it that way.

Chapter 1 A Request Made After Class

The first time Akari Fujisaki asked Itsuki for help, she did it while the classroom was emptying.

It was Wednesday, and the afternoon light had turned the windows of Class 3-B into bright rectangles. Chairs scraped across the floor. Someone near the back was arguing about summer homework, while two students had already begun discussing where they would go during the break.

Itsuki packed his notebooks into his bag. Riko had disappeared before the teacher finished speaking, probably in pursuit of something that would later become a disciplinary issue. Ayane was helping a classmate gather loose worksheets. The room was returning to its usual noise.

“Kurose-kun?”

Itsuki looked up.

Akari stood beside the next row of desks, one hand resting on the strap of her schoolbag. Her dark reddish-brown hair fell neatly over her shoulders. She looked as composed as she usually did when speaking to someone, but her eyes moved briefly toward the door to check whether anyone was listening.

“Yes?”

“Could I ask you something? Not here, if that's okay.”

He glanced around. “Is it about the homework?”

“Not exactly.”

They stepped into the corridor, where the afternoon crowd had thinned. Akari stopped near the open window at the end of the hall. The breeze moved the loose ends of her hair.

“I heard you helped explain the English reading to someone in class,” she said.

“I answered a question.”

“You explained it. That's different.”

Itsuki waited.

Akari took a breath. “My English is fine until I have to work through a long passage under time pressure. I understand the sentences separately, but sometimes I lose the thread. And my maths result wasn't what I wanted, either.”

“You want help studying?”

“Would you be willing to tutor me for a little while?”

Itsuki considered the request. He had expected a question about the old rumour, or perhaps a request to confirm something that had already been discussed by half the school. This was more straightforward.

“Why me?”

“Because you explain things without making people feel stupid for not understanding them.”

He did not know what to do with that compliment, so he left it where it was.

“I can't promise I'll be a good tutor.”

“You don't have to promise that. We can try one session.”

She was quick to add, “I'd prefer to keep it quiet. Not because I'm embarrassed to ask for help. I just don't want it becoming another school topic. People already make assumptions about everything I do.”

Itsuki understood that part. He had no interest in becoming a subject of conversation either.

“The city library has public study rooms,” he said. “We could use one of those. If there's a space available.”

“That would be perfect.”

“Bring the material you're having trouble with. We'll see how it goes.”

Akari's expression eased. “Thank you.”

“Don't thank me yet. I haven't done anything.”

“You agreed to try.”

“That's a very low standard.”

“It's still a start.”

They exchanged contact details for scheduling. Akari sent him a short message immediately so he would have her number, then checked the screen as if making sure the arrangement had become real.

When Itsuki returned to the classroom, Ayane was waiting near the door with her bag over one shoulder.

“Everything okay?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“You were talking with Fujisaki-san.”

“She asked about studying.”

Ayane nodded. She did not ask for details. Itsuki noticed the restraint and appreciated it.

“I'll see you tomorrow, then.”

“Tomorrow.”

On the way downstairs, his phone vibrated again.

Akari: Would Saturday afternoon work for a first session? I can bring my English textbook and the last practice test.

Itsuki replied that two o'clock would be fine.

He put the phone away before reaching the entrance.

It was only one afternoon a week, he told himself.

There was no reason to write it on the calendar yet.

Chapter 2 A Table Beside the Clock

The city library had a clock above the information desk that was four minutes fast.

Itsuki knew this because he had once arrived early for a group assignment and spent twenty minutes watching the second hand move. The clock had not been corrected since then. He suspected the staff knew. Perhaps they preferred it that way.

Akari was already waiting when he arrived on Saturday.

She had chosen a table in the public study area beside the wall clock. Her textbooks were arranged in a neat stack, with a notebook open and several coloured page markers visible along the edge. A bottle of water stood beside her pencil case.

“You're early,” she said.

“The bus was on time.”

“That's not usually how people explain being early.”

“It's the explanation I have.”

Akari smiled and moved her bag from the chair opposite him. “I brought the practice test.”

They began with an English passage about urban green spaces. Akari could translate most of the sentences, but her answers to the comprehension questions missed the connections between them. She underlined individual words, then returned to the beginning of the passage whenever a question referred to the author's argument.

Itsuki read the page once.

“What do you think the writer is trying to say?” he asked.

“That parks are useful in cities.”

“That's true, but it's broad.”

“They're useful because people can relax and exercise.”

“Also true.”

Akari looked at the passage again. “You're going to tell me I'm missing something.”

“I'm trying not to.”

He pointed to the first paragraph. “This gives the common argument. The second paragraph introduces a problem with it. The third one explains what the writer thinks cities should do differently. If you read each sentence separately, you can understand the words and still miss the direction.”

He drew three small boxes in the margin of his scrap paper and labelled them claim, problem, and response.

“Don't translate everything first. Find what each paragraph is doing.”

Akari studied the boxes. “So I should read for the structure before worrying about every word.”

“Try it.”

She did. Her first attempt was still hesitant, but she identified the writer's shift from general benefits to the lack of accessible green spaces in crowded neighbourhoods.

“That's it,” Itsuki said.

“I got the answer?”

“You got the argument. The answer comes after.”

Akari looked down at her notebook and wrote the three labels in a clean column. “I think I understand what you mean now.”

“Good.”

“You make it sound easy.”

“It wasn't easy when I first learned it.”

She looked up. “Really?”

“I had to practise.”

“That's reassuring.”

They worked through two more passages. Akari asked questions whenever a step was unclear. She did not pretend to understand simply to move on, and she did not wait for Itsuki to supply every answer. When she made a mistake, she tried to identify where her reasoning had changed direction.

After an hour, Itsuki realised he had been speaking too abstractly. Akari's notes were accurate, but her answers became less certain whenever the passage used unfamiliar vocabulary.

“Let's change the method,” he said.

He took a short paragraph and asked her to summarise it in one plain sentence before looking at the questions. Then they compared that sentence with the next paragraph. The process was slower, but Akari began to see how the ideas connected.

“This is better,” she said.

“Then we'll use it.”

When the study-room booking ended, they packed their things. The clock above the desk was still four minutes fast.

“Same time next week?” Akari asked.

“If the room is available.”

“I'll book it.”

They walked as far as the library entrance together. Akari thanked him, then turned toward the station. Itsuki watched her join the small stream of people crossing the plaza.

His phone vibrated before he reached the bicycle parking area.

Akari: I wrote down the method. I'll practise before next week. Thank you for taking it seriously.

Itsuki typed a brief reply.

Itsuki: Practise with a few passages. Don't just reread the notes.

He put the phone away.

For the first time in several weeks, a Saturday afternoon had a clear purpose.

Interlude I The List on Nanami's Desk

Nanami Kurose kept three lists in the notebook beside her bed.

The first was for school assignments. The second was for things she wanted to remember. The third was labelled Things Yui Says Are Important, although Nanami had never agreed that all of them were important.

At the moment, the third list contained: buy more coloured paper, ask the library about the return date, and find out whether Professor Whiskers could be used as a reading-event mascot without looking like a suspicious cat.

Nanami considered the last point unresolved.

“He's a paper cat,” Yui said from the other side of the desk. “He can't look suspicious.”

“That's exactly what a suspicious cat would want you to think.”

Yui laughed and placed another sheet of paper beside the notebook. They were finishing the paperwork for the library-exchange project, a small activity that had begun with picture books and had grown into a summer reading corner for younger students.

Nanami liked the project because it gave her something to organise that belonged to her. It was not a school assignment, and it was not something her brother had asked her to do. She and Yui had chosen the books, sorted the labels, and written the little recommendation cards themselves.

“We need to return the borrowed books before the event,” Yui said.

“I know. The librarian said the old branch is open on Saturday.”

“Are you going alone?”

“Probably. Onii-chan said he could come if I needed help carrying everything.”

“Then ask him.”

“He'll say yes.”

“That sounds convenient.”

Nanami gave her a look. “You say that as if I have discovered a secret.”

Her phone lit up with a message from her father. He asked whether she had received the dates for the summer event and whether she might like to have dinner with him sometime the following week. He added that his girlfriend would be joining them, if Nanami was comfortable with that.

Nanami read the message twice.

She was not angry. Her father had been careful to ask rather than assume, and he had never told her that she needed to feel a particular way about his new relationship. Still, the invitation made her pause. Dinner with her father had once meant the same familiar table, the same house, and a routine she did not have to think about. Now it meant deciding what would feel comfortable.

Yui noticed her expression. “Something happened?”

“Dad asked if I want to have dinner next week.”

“Do you?”

“I think so.”

“Then you can say yes.”

“It's not that simple.”

Yui waited rather than filling the silence.

Nanami looked down at the message. “His girlfriend will be there.”

“Oh.”

“She's nice. I just don't know what I'm supposed to do.”

“You don't have to do anything special. You can have dinner.”

Nanami considered that. It was a surprisingly useful answer.

She typed that she would like to come, then asked what day would suit him. The reply arrived a minute later, warm and uncomplicated. Her father thanked her and said he would arrange the details.

Yui leaned over the notebook. “Now we can finish the list.”

Nanami picked up her pencil. “Professor Whiskers still needs a less suspicious expression.”

“He's a cat.”

“And that's why the standards are high.”

They returned to the paperwork. On the desk, the three lists remained open, each with a few empty lines left.

Nanami did not need to decide everything about her family that afternoon. She only needed to finish the library forms and remember to bring the books on Saturday.

For now, that was enough.

Chapter 3 The Seat We Chose

The school library was quieter after the last bell, though not empty.

Itsuki found Mio Kanzaki at the table near the reference shelves. Her midnight-blue hair fell straight past her shoulders, and a paperback lay open beside a notebook. She had placed a small paper marker between two pages.

“You're early,” she said.

“I had no reason to stay in the classroom.”

“That's a reason.”

He sat opposite her. The chair made a faint sound against the floor.

For a while, neither spoke. Mio turned a page. Itsuki opened the book he had brought. The silence was not awkward, which was one of the reasons he had begun to enjoy these meetings.

After several minutes, Mio closed her paperback.

“I think the original source of the rumour may never be clear.”

Itsuki looked up. “You haven't found it?”

“No. I've spoken to the people who saw parts of the incident. The accounts explain how the story changed, but none of them can identify one person who started it.”

“Does that bother you?”

“A little.”

“Because the puzzle is unfinished?”

“Because I thought there would be a point where all the pieces fit.”

Itsuki considered the answer. “Sometimes the pieces are missing.”

“I know.”

She said it without frustration, but her fingers remained on the cover of the book.

“I don't want to keep asking people questions just because I started,” she continued. “The rumour is already fading. If I keep bringing it up, I'll be the one making it important again.”

“That would be inconvenient.”

“For you?”

“For everyone.”

Mio looked at him for a moment, then nodded. “Then I think I'll stop.”

It was a small decision, but Itsuki understood that it had cost her something. She liked knowing how things connected. Leaving a question unresolved was not her preferred outcome.

“What are you reading?” he asked.

Mio glanced at the paperback. “A collection of short stories. One of them has a very ordinary ending.”

“Is that a complaint?”

“I'm deciding.”

“You can dislike an ordinary ending.”

“I can. But I think I wanted it to be more complicated than it was.”

She pushed the book a little closer so he could see the cover. Itsuki recognised the author but had not read the collection.

“Would you recommend it?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“Even with the ending?”

“Especially because of it.”

He wrote down the title.

They spent the rest of the hour reading. When the librarian announced closing time, Mio placed a bookmark between the pages and packed her things.

“Are you coming here next week?” she asked.

“Probably.”

“Then I'll see you.”

“If we're both here.”

“That's generally how meeting works.”

Itsuki adjusted the strap of his bag. “I thought there might be a more mysterious explanation.”

Mio's expression changed just enough to suggest a smile.

They left the library together. At the entrance, she turned toward the station while he headed for the bicycle parking area.

There had been no questions about the festival, no notes, and no attempt to reconstruct an afternoon that had already passed.

It was a meeting about books.

Itsuki found that he wanted another one.

Chapter 4 A Schedule in Three Colours

Riko Tachibana's summer study schedule had three colours and no visible connection to reality.

“Blue is English, yellow is maths, and pink is everything else,” she explained, holding the page against the classroom window.

Itsuki examined it. Several pink blocks were labelled with activities such as walk around, snack break, and think about studying.

“You made a schedule for avoiding your schedule.”

“It's called balance.”

“It's called a calendar with decorations.”

Riko lowered the page. Her short caramel-brown hair shifted as she tilted her head. “You could be more supportive.”

“I could.”

“And yet?”

“I chose accuracy.”

She laughed and folded the paper. The final school assessments were approaching, and Riko had begun talking about her study plan more often. Her mathematics results had not been disastrous, but they were low enough to attract the attention of her family. She wanted to improve before the next report.

“I need a new workbook,” she said. “The one I have is full of exercises I don't understand.”

“That's what the explanations are for.”

“The explanations are written by people who already understand the exercises.”

“A common problem.”

“Come with me to buy one.”

Itsuki looked at her. “Why?”

“Because you know which ones are useful.”

“You could ask a teacher.”

“I could. But you're here.”

It was difficult to argue with the practical simplicity of that statement.

They agreed to meet at the shopping street after school the next day. Riko insisted that he should choose the workbook, then complained when he selected one with a plain cover and clear worked examples.

“This one looks boring.”

“It's a maths workbook.”

“It could have a little personality.”

“The equations have enough.”

She flipped through the pages. The exercises were arranged by topic, with short explanations and space to show each step. Riko paused at a section on equations and read one example twice.

“This might be okay.”

“Then get it.”

“You're very decisive when spending my money.”

“You asked me to choose.”

At the stationery shop next door, Riko picked up a packet of coloured page markers. She held them beside the workbook.

“These are for organising the chapters.”

“Good idea.”

“You sound surprised.”

“I'm trying to encourage you.”

“Try harder.”

They left the shop with the workbook and a small bag of stationery. The shopping street was busy with people buying groceries and summer supplies. A shopkeeper was watering the plants outside a florist, and the smell of grilled food drifted from a restaurant near the corner.

Riko slowed near a vending machine.

“Do you want something?” she asked.

“No.”

“I do.”

She bought a peach drink and stood beside him while opening it. For once, she did not immediately find another joke.

“My parents asked what I was doing after the tests,” she said.

“What did you tell them?”

“That I'd study.”

“That sounds reasonable.”

“They meant the whole summer.”

Itsuki looked at the workbook in her bag. “You don't have to plan every day.”

“I know. They think if I don't plan it, I'll waste it.”

“Will you?”

Riko gave him a sideways look. “Probably some of it.”

“Then plan the part you don't want to waste.”

She considered that, then nodded once.

“You're surprisingly useful for someone who looks like he'd rather be at home.”

“I would rather be at home.”

“See? Honesty. That's why I like asking you things.”

They continued toward the station. Riko walked a little more slowly than usual, matching his pace without mentioning it.

Itsuki did not point it out.

Chapter 5 The Last Test Before Summer

The last assessment before summer break began with a sheet of paper and a room full of people trying not to look nervous.

Itsuki finished the English section with time to check his answers. He read the final passage again, corrected one careless mark, and placed his pencil beside the paper when the teacher called time.

Across the aisle, Akari closed her booklet carefully. Riko leaned back in her chair with the expression of someone who had survived a natural disaster. Ayane was already arranging her stationery.

“I think I did better than last time,” Riko whispered as they left the room.

“You said that last time.”

“And I was right about one question.”

“A strong foundation.”

She nudged his shoulder with her notebook.

The results were returned two days later. Itsuki's marks were steady. Riko's mathematics score had improved, though not enough to satisfy her. Akari's English result was better than her previous attempt in the sections they had practised, but the longer reading questions still cost her time.

She studied the paper in silence.

“It's not bad,” Itsuki said.

“It's not where I want it.”

“Both can be true.”

Akari looked at the marks again. “The paragraph structure helped. I lost time on the last passage because I kept going back to check vocabulary.”

“That's something we can work on.”

“Would you be willing to keep going during summer?”

“If our schedules match.”

“I'll send you the dates I'm free.”

She folded the result sheet and placed it in her folder. There was disappointment in her expression, but not defeat. Itsuki thought that mattered more than the score itself.

Riko appeared at his desk with her own paper.

“I improved.”

“I saw.”

“Say something encouraging.”

“You improved.”

“Something more encouraging.”

“Keep doing what worked.”

She stared at him. “You really need a manual.”

“For what?”

“Compliments.”

Ayane joined them, smiling at the exchange. “You did improve, Riko. That's good.”

“Thank you, Fujimoto-san. At least someone appreciates progress.”

“I appreciate it too,” Itsuki said.

Riko looked at him for a second, then turned away. “You can say that more often.”

“I'll consider it.”

When the final homeroom notice ended, the class began to empty. Summer break was close enough for people to speak about it as if it had already started.

Akari sent Itsuki a message with several possible library dates. Riko asked whether he could check one exercise from her new workbook sometime next week. Ayane mentioned a neighbourhood activity later in the month and said he could come if he was free.

Itsuki looked at the three messages while standing beside his desk.

They were all ordinary requests.

That was becoming the problem.

He could no longer pretend his calendar was empty because nothing was happening.

He took out his phone and began adding the dates.

When he finished, July had several blue marks.

He did not dislike the colour.

Chapter 6 The Books That Came Back

The old library near the east station had a narrow entrance and a sign that looked as if it had been painted by hand several times.

Nanami stood beneath it with two cloth bags full of picture books. Itsuki carried the heavier box, which contained the remaining books and the display materials for the summer reading corner.

“You said there were only a few,” he said.

“There are only a few.”

“This box has its own centre of gravity.”

“That's because the books are important.”

“I see.”

Yui was waiting inside with the librarian. She waved when they entered and immediately took one of Nanami's bags.

“You brought everything!”

“Most of it,” Nanami said. “The cat is still in the notebook.”

“Professor Whiskers is a separate department,” Yui replied.

Itsuki placed the box on the counter. The librarian checked the return list while Nanami explained which books were being used for the summer event. She had prepared the paperwork and knew exactly which titles belonged to the exchange project.

Itsuki stood nearby, useful only when something needed lifting.

“You can go if you want,” Nanami told him.

“I'm not in a hurry.”

“You look like you're waiting for a bus.”

“I'm waiting for you.”

She gave him a small, satisfied smile before returning to the librarian.

Once the books were checked in, Yui showed them the revised event poster. Nanami had designed the layout with a pale blue border and small drawings of stars. Professor Whiskers appeared in the corner, wearing a paper crown that made him look less suspicious and more confused.

“He looks better,” Itsuki said.

Nanami folded her arms. “Thank you. I worked hard on his expression.”

“He looks like he has questions about the management.”

“That's his personality.”

They finished the last of the arrangements and stepped outside. The afternoon heat had settled over the street. Yui had to leave for a family errand, so Nanami and Itsuki walked toward the station together.

Her phone rang before they reached the crossing.

“Dad,” she said after answering. “We're done at the library.”

Itsuki slowed his pace.

Nanami listened, then glanced at him. “Yes, I remember. Next Thursday is fine.”

She ended the call and slipped the phone into her bag.

“Dinner?” Itsuki asked.

“Yes. Dad booked a place near his office. His girlfriend will be there.”

“You sound prepared.”

“I am prepared. I chose to go.”

“Good.”

She looked ahead. “I don't know if it'll feel normal.”

“It doesn't have to feel normal immediately.”

Nanami considered that. “You make things sound simple.”

“I didn't say easy.”

“That's true.”

They waited for the crossing signal. A group of students passed behind them, talking about a summer event. Nanami adjusted the strap of her bag.

“I want to see Dad,” she said. “I just don't want anyone to expect me to act like nothing changed.”

“Then don't.”

She turned toward him. “You're allowed to give advice that isn't three words long.”

“I used four.”

“A generous day.”

Itsuki glanced at the traffic light. “You can have dinner with him and still miss how things used to be.”

Nanami was quiet for a moment.

“I know.”

When the signal changed, they crossed together.

At the station entrance, Nanami stopped and held out one of the cloth bags.

“Can you take this?”

“I thought you had it.”

“I did. Now you do.”

He accepted it.

“Thank you, Onii-chan.”

“You're welcome.”

She smiled and went down the station stairs.

Itsuki watched until she disappeared into the crowd. Then he turned toward his own platform, carrying a bag that contained nothing but a few folded posters and a paper cat.

It was lighter than the box.

Somehow, it felt more important.

Chapter 7 A Question in the Margins

Akari brought mathematics to their next library session in a folder with a small gold clasp.

“I know we planned to work on English,” she said, sitting down. “But the assessment showed that I need to fix this too.”

Itsuki opened the folder. The mistakes were mostly in multi-step word problems. Akari could perform the calculations once she knew which operation to use, but she sometimes chose the wrong starting point or skipped a condition hidden in the wording.

“Let's take one,” he said.

He read the problem, wrote down the known values, and began explaining the sequence. Halfway through, Akari stopped him.

“Wait. Why did you use that value first?”

“Because it gives us—”

He paused. The answer he had been about to give assumed she already saw the relationship between the quantities.

“Let me start again.”

He drew a simple diagram and labelled each part. Akari followed, then pointed to one of the arrows.

“I thought this meant the total.”

“That makes sense. The wording is easy to read that way.”

“So I wasn't making the calculation wrong. I was setting up the wrong relationship.”

“Exactly.”

They worked through the problem again, more slowly. Akari wrote each step in her notebook and explained why she had chosen it before moving to the next line.

Her first independent attempt was incorrect, but she found the mistake after comparing the diagram with the wording.

“I see it,” she said.

“Where?”

She circled the condition she had overlooked. “Here. I treated these two things as if they were the same.”

“Good.”

Akari leaned back. “I think I need to stop trying to finish the problem before I've understood what it's asking.”

“That would help.”

“You could have said that earlier.”

“I was trying to let you discover it.”

“How generous.”

“I try.”

She laughed softly and turned to the next question.

They spent most of the session on three problems. It was not much material, but Akari's explanations became clearer with each attempt. When she could not identify the first step, she learned to mark the information she knew and ask what the problem wanted her to find.

Near the end, she closed the workbook.

“I used to think I was bad at maths because I couldn't do it quickly.”

“Speed and understanding aren't the same thing.”

“I know that now.”

“You still have to practise.”

“I know that too.”

Itsuki gathered the loose sheets. “Then we're making progress.”

Akari checked her notes. “Could we do both subjects next week? Maybe alternate which one we spend more time on.”

“That sounds reasonable.”

She looked relieved, though she tried not to make it obvious.

Outside, the sky had clouded over. They walked to the library entrance together and paused beneath the awning while a brief shower began.

“I didn't bring an umbrella,” Akari said.

“There's a convenience store across the street.”

“I know. I was hoping it would stop.”

“It might.”

They watched the rain for a minute. Akari's phone buzzed with a message. She read it, then put it away.

“My friends are waiting at the station,” she said. “I'll go when it gets lighter.”

“All right.”

She looked at him. “You don't have to wait.”

“I'm going that way.”

“Then I won't argue.”

The rain softened a few minutes later. They crossed the street separately, each heading toward a different part of the station plaza.

Itsuki realised he had not once thought about whether anyone might see them together.

It was a small change.

He suspected Akari had noticed it too.

Chapter 8 The Way Home We Remembered

The neighbourhood activity was a small summer clean-up organised by the residents' association.

Itsuki had agreed to help because Ayane's family had asked whether he could carry a few boxes from the community centre. He had not expected the task to take more than an hour. By the time he arrived, however, Ayane was already sorting supplies and greeting people by name.

“You came,” she said.

“I said I would.”

“I know. I just wasn't sure whether the time had changed.”

“It didn't.”

Ayane smiled, a little embarrassed. “Right.”

They carried the boxes to the storage room and helped arrange the tables. The work was ordinary: folding signs, stacking gloves, moving bottles of water, and answering questions from residents who had forgotten where the collection point was.

Ayane seemed comfortable here. She knew the older neighbours and remembered which children belonged to which families. Itsuki had seen that side of her before, though not often in recent years.

When the activity ended, they walked back through the residential streets with a bag of unused supplies between them.

“Do you remember this route?” Ayane asked.

Itsuki looked at the narrow road ahead. A low wall bordered one side, and a row of hydrangeas grew near the corner. “We used to take it after visiting your house.”

“You always walked on the outside.”

“The pavement was uneven.”

“You said it was because the bicycles came too close.”

“That too.”

Ayane laughed. “You were very serious about it.”

“You were very bad at looking where you were going.”

“I was a child.”

“So was I.”

“I used to think we'd always walk this way,” Ayane said.

Itsuki looked at her.

“Not every day,” she continued. “Just… I thought we'd keep knowing where the other person was. Then school got busy, and we stopped asking.”

“I thought you were busy.”

“I thought you wanted to be left alone.”

Neither sounded surprised. The explanation was simple, almost disappointingly so.

“We were both wrong sometimes,” Itsuki said.

Ayane nodded. “I think so.”

They began walking again.

“I don't want us to pretend we're exactly the same as before,” she said. “I don't think we are.”

“We aren't.”

“But I'd like to talk more. If that's okay.”

“That's okay.”

She looked ahead, and the tension in her shoulders eased.

At the next crossing, a child ran past them with a paper pinwheel. Ayane stepped aside to let him pass, then glanced at Itsuki.

“Do you still like the bakery near the station?”

“The one with the melon bread?”

“Yes.”

“It's fine.”

“That's a very strong review.”

“It's a good bakery.”

“Would you go sometime?”

“If I'm free.”

“I'll message you.”

They reached the street where their paths separated. Ayane took the bag of supplies from him.

“Thank you for today.”

“It was only boxes.”

“It wasn't only boxes.”

Itsuki did not ask her to explain. She seemed content to leave the sentence where it was.

He watched her walk toward her house, then turned toward his apartment.

The route home was familiar.

The person walking beside him had become familiar again, though not in the same way as before.

That seemed like a better place to begin.

Interlude II The Schedule on the Refrigerator

Riko's house was quiet in the early evening.

At school, she was used to filling silence with jokes, questions, and whatever small disruption happened to be available. At home, she moved more carefully. “You have maths today?”

“Yes.”

“Tomorrow.”

“I know.”

She returned to the workbook.

Itsuki had told her to write down what the question actually asked before trying to solve it.

She took a clean sheet and drew a small table.

One value at a time.

She found the first relationship, checked the units, and corrected a sign she had copied incorrectly. The answer appeared at the bottom of the page, not especially impressive, but correct.

Riko stared at it.

Then she checked it again.

Still correct.

She took a photograph and sent it to Itsuki.

Riko: One problem. No casualties. I think I did it right.

His reply came several minutes later.

Itsuki: The setup looks right. Check the final line once more, but yes.

Riko smiled at the screen before she could stop herself.

She typed: You're allowed to say good job.

Itsuki: Good job. Check the final line.

For the first time, she thought she might be able to leave a little space on purpose.

Chapter 9 A Day That Wasn't on the Schedule

“Today wasn't on my schedule.”

Itsuki watched the signal change.

It had been an ordinary afternoon.

Riko seemed to have wanted one.

Chapter 10 An Afternoon Without Questions

Mio chose the bookshop because it had a narrow second floor and a window seat that overlooked the shopping street.

Itsuki arrived a little after three. She was already there, reading a book with a paper cup of tea beside her.

“I thought you might be late,” she said.

“The train was on time.”

“Maybe it gets better.”

“Maybe. But I don't want to spend my afternoon waiting for it.”

Itsuki found a book he had been meaning to read and added it to his pile.

“Usually.”

“I could.”

There was a brief pause.

“Did I misunderstand?” she asked.

“I thought you were leaving because you didn't want to keep meeting.”

“No. I have vegetables to buy.”

“It happens.”

“You did.”

Itsuki adjusted the strap of his bag. “You don't have to guess.”

Mio nodded. “Then I'll ask next time.”

He left the shop and went to the supermarket. On the way home, he received a message from her.

Mio: I found the second book. I'll tell you whether the ending is ordinary too.

Itsuki replied that he would wait for the review.

He bought the vegetables, returned to his apartment, and put the groceries away.

There were no questions waiting for him.

Only a book recommendation he had begun to look forward to.

Chapter 11 A Summer Reading Corner

Nanami arrived before the volunteers, carrying a box of paper bookmarks and a folder of handwritten recommendations. Yui came shortly afterward with Professor Whiskers attached to the front of her bag.

“You seem busy.”

“I am.”

“Thank you for helping today,” he said.

“Nanami organised most of it.”

“I know. I'm proud of her.”

Itsuki nodded.

Her father glanced toward the reading corner. “She seems comfortable.”

“She is.”

“I'm glad.”

There was no request for Itsuki to change his own arrangements, no suggestion that he should visit the house more often, and no attempt to turn the conversation into a family discussion. Itsuki appreciated that.

When the event ended, Nanami and Yui counted the returned books and packed the activity sheets. Itsuki carried the remaining boxes to the storage room.

“You did well,” he told Nanami.

“I know.”

“Modest.”

“I learned from the best.”

“That's unfortunate.”

She laughed.

On the way home, Nanami talked about the children who had chosen the animal books and the little girl who had insisted Professor Whiskers needed a friend. Her voice was bright with the satisfaction of a day that had gone as planned.

Itsuki listened.

She had made the day her own.

That was what he had wanted for her.

Interlude III A Different Kind of Invitation

Akari Fujisaki liked the sound of a busy room.

It meant people were talking, plans were being made, and someone would probably suggest going somewhere after lunch. She enjoyed moving between groups, remembering what everyone had said, and making sure nobody was left out of a conversation.

Akari: Yes. Let's go somewhere.

She did not have to choose only one.

Chapter 12 The Explanation That Didn't Work

Itsuki's explanation failed halfway through the second problem.

He had written the relationship on the whiteboard in the library study room, marked the known values, and begun to show how the expression could be rearranged. Akari followed the first two steps, then stopped.

“I don't understand why that changes the answer.”

“Because we're moving this term to the other side.”

“I know that part. I don't understand why we're doing it before substituting the value.”

Itsuki looked at the board.

He had skipped a step.

“I thought it would be clearer this way.”

“It isn't.”

“Then it isn't.”

He erased the last line and returned to the original expression. This time he asked Akari what she thought the problem was asking them to find. She explained the situation in her own words, then identified which quantity was still unknown.

“What do we know about it?” he asked.

She pointed to the conditions in the question. “This value changes when the first one changes. But I don't know how to write that.”

“Let's use a table.”

“That's the answer,” she said.

“Yes.”

“And I understand why.”

Itsuki set the marker down. “Good.”

Akari looked at the erased board. “Your first explanation was too fast.”

“I noticed.”

“You don't have to get it right on the first try.”

“I know.”

“I mean it. I can tell you when I don't understand.”

Itsuki nodded. “Then do that.”

“You understood more of it.”

“That's true.”

“Are you busy?” Itsuki asked.

“Not today. My friends are meeting near the station.”

“Then you should go.”

She smiled. “I was planning to.”

For a moment, she looked as if she might say something else. Instead, she adjusted the strap of her bag.

“See you next week, Kurose-kun.”

“See you.”

Itsuki went in the opposite direction.

The explanation had failed.

The lesson had not.

Chapter 16 The First Bell Back

“Morning,” Itsuki replied.

Riko leaned over the desk. “Did you have a good summer?”

“It was nice,” Ayane said. “The neighbourhood event went well.”

“I heard. Kurose was recruited too.”

“I carried boxes.”

“A vital contribution.”

Akari arrived a few minutes later with two friends. She greeted the room, then noticed Itsuki and gave him a small wave.

“Good morning, Kurose-kun.”

“Good morning.”

There was no pause in the conversation around them. No one turned to look. The old rumour had become a story people remembered only when someone brought it up, and most had found other things to discuss.

Itsuki found that he preferred it this way.

After homeroom, Akari approached his desk.

“The library has our usual room next Saturday,” she said. “I booked it.”

“All right.”

“I also made a list of the questions I want to review.”

“Bring it.”

She hesitated, then smiled. “I don't think I need to hide the fact that I'm studying anymore. I just don't want everyone asking about it.”

“That's reasonable.”

“I thought you'd say that.”

She returned to her friends.

During lunch, Mio sat at the library table with a book beside her. Itsuki joined her after buying a drink. They spoke about the next book she had chosen, then read quietly until the bell.

Riko sent him a photograph of her workbook with a completed page. Ayane messaged later about the bakery near the station. Nanami sent a reminder about a school form she needed her father to sign.

By the end of the day, Itsuki had answered all four messages.

He looked at the list on his phone while waiting for the train.

None of the conversations had been urgent.

None had been particularly dramatic.

He had still wanted to answer them.

The train arrived, and Itsuki stepped inside.

September had begun.

Chapter 17 A Result She Earned

Akari received her next assessment result on a Friday.

She read the paper at her desk, then looked at the score again. It was not the highest mark in the class. It was not a dramatic improvement. But the long reading passage that had caused trouble before was now one of her strongest sections, and her mathematics answers showed fewer errors in setting up the problems.

She found Itsuki after class.

“I did better.”

He looked at the paper she held out. “You did.”

“Not perfect.”

“You weren't aiming for perfect.”

“I was aiming for better.”

“Then you got it.”

Akari smiled. “I wanted to show you.”

He returned the paper. “You did the work.”

“You helped me understand how to do it.”

“That's what the sessions were for.”

She folded the paper and placed it in her folder. “I think I can manage most of the practice on my own now. Could we change the sessions to every other week? Or whenever I get stuck?”

“That sounds fine.”

“I don't want to stop meeting entirely.”

Itsuki looked at her.

Akari's expression remained open and matter-of-fact. “I like studying with you. It's useful, and it's easier to ask questions when I don't have to worry about whether I'm slowing someone down.”

“Then we can keep meeting.”

“Good.”

She thanked him and returned to her friends, who were waiting near the classroom door. They began discussing an upcoming outing. Akari joined the conversation without looking back.

Itsuki put the result out of his mind and packed his bag.

Later that afternoon, he met Mio at the library. She was reading near the window, a notebook open beside her.

“You look less tired,” she said.

“I slept.”

“That's a useful habit.”

“I recommend it.”

She asked about the book he had borrowed. He told her he had finished it and liked the middle more than the ending. Mio disagreed, and they spent several minutes comparing the parts they had marked.

At the end of the conversation, she asked whether he would come again next week.

“Probably.”

“I'll be here.”

Itsuki closed his book. “Then I'll see you.”

Outside, the school term continued around them. Assignments accumulated, club activities resumed, and the afternoons grew a little shorter.

Akari no longer needed a weekly tutor.

That did not mean the time they had spent together had become unnecessary.

It had become something else.

Chapter 18 The Things We Put on the Calendar

The career guidance questionnaires were handed out during homeroom in the middle of September.

Itsuki read the first page and found a list of questions about university, vocational study, employment, and possible fields of interest. The final section asked students to describe what they wanted their lives to look like five years from now.

He stared at the blank line.

Five years was a long time to imagine.

“You look like the paper insulted you,” Riko whispered.

“It asked a difficult question.”

“Write 'peace and quiet.'”

“That's not a career.”

“It could be.”

Ayane leaned over from the next desk. “Have you thought about what you want to do?”

“Not properly.”

“Me neither,” she admitted. “I have a few ideas, but I don't know which one fits.”

Riko tapped her pencil against the desk. “I know what my parents want me to do.”

“Is it what you want?” Itsuki asked.

She stopped tapping.

“I don't know yet.”

The teacher began explaining the submission date. Itsuki returned to the questionnaire, but the blank line remained blank.

At lunch, he found Akari speaking with two classmates about a school information session. She had a brochure open and was comparing several programmes. When she noticed him, she asked whether he had received the questionnaire.

“Yes.”

“Do you know what you'll write?”

“No.”

“I'm still deciding too.”

She showed him the brochure. One programme was related to business management, another to communications, and a third to a subject she had recently become interested in.

“My family has ideas,” she said. “I'm trying to work out which parts are actually mine.”

“That sounds difficult.”

“It is. But I think I can take my time.”

She returned to her friends when they called her.

Later, Itsuki met Mio in the library. She had already filled in part of her questionnaire.

“You seem prepared,” he said.

“I wrote down what I know. The rest can wait.”

“What do you know?”

“I want to keep reading. I want to learn more about writing and editing. I don't know what job that becomes.”

“That's more than I have.”

“You have time.”

“Everyone keeps saying that.”

“Because it's true.”

He sat opposite her and took out his own questionnaire.

“What would you ask if you didn't have to choose immediately?” Mio asked.

Itsuki looked at the blank line. “I'd ask what options I have.”

“Then start there.”

He wrote it in the margin.

That evening, Riko sent a message saying she had asked her parents about a school information session. Ayane sent a link to a local open day. Akari shared the dates for a university fair. Nanami asked whether he knew where to find the old family documents she needed for a school form.

Itsuki answered them one by one.

When he returned to the kitchen, he looked at the calendar. The next two weeks held a library meeting, a school information session, a grocery reminder, and a note to call Nanami about the documents.

He added one more item.

Look into options.

It was not a plan.

But it was a beginning.

For a long time, Itsuki had treated the future as something that would eventually arrive and require an answer. Now he wondered whether it might be easier to approach it in smaller pieces, the way he approached a difficult question in a workbook.

One thing at a time.

He put the pen back.

Outside, the evening had cooled. The last light rested on the balcony rail, and a neighbour's television played faintly through the wall.

His phone vibrated.

Mio: There's a small book event next month. Would you like to go?

Itsuki looked at the message for a moment.

Then he replied.

Itsuki: Yes. Send me the details.

He added the event to the calendar when she sent the date.

October was no longer empty either.

Epilogue A Page for October

September ended on a quiet Wednesday.

Itsuki returned to his apartment after school, put his bag beside the desk, and opened the refrigerator to see what he could make for dinner. The blue fish magnet still held the calendar in place. July and August were now filled with small notes, and September had more marks than he remembered adding.

Some were school dates. Some were library sessions. Others were reminders from Nanami, Riko, or people who had begun to assume he would be there when they asked.

He did not mind that as much as he once might have.

His phone showed a message from Akari about a practice question she had solved on her own. Riko had sent a photograph of her completed workbook page, with a triumphant note beneath it. Ayane had confirmed the date for the bakery visit. Mio had sent the details for the October book event.

Itsuki replied to each message while the rice cooked.

Nanami sent one more notification: a photograph of Professor Whiskers wearing a paper scarf.

Nanami: Yui says he's ready for the colder months.

Itsuki typed: He looks concerned.

Nanami: That's his serious expression.

Itsuki: I hope he recovers.

She sent back three laughing stickers.

Itsuki put the phone down and opened the calendar to October. There were already two dates to mark. He added the book event, then wrote the bakery visit beneath it.

For a moment, he considered the blank space between them.

There was room for other things. A school event, perhaps. A day to do nothing. A plan he had not thought of yet.

He used to think an empty calendar meant that nothing could trouble him.

Now he wondered whether it had also meant that nothing had been invited in.

The thought was not especially dramatic. It did not change the shape of the room or make the evening feel different. The rice cooker clicked, the refrigerator hummed, and a car passed along the street below.

Itsuki closed the calendar.

He had not found an answer to the question about his future. He had not solved every problem in his friendships. The rumour had faded, but the school year still had months left to go. There would be more tests, more conversations, and probably more messages sent at inconvenient times.

For now, he had a few dates worth keeping.

He set the table for one, then paused and took out a second plate. Nanami had mentioned that she might stop by after school the following afternoon.

Itsuki put the plate beside the first.

There was no need to decide anything else tonight.

October could wait until tomorrow.
