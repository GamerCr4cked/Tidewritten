Chapter One The Bus Stop

Arjun Sato's mornings were accurate to the minute.

His alarm rang at 7:15. He stood up at 7:16, dressed by 7:30, left home at 7:45, and reached the bus stop at 8:05. The bus arrived at 8:12. His desk at TechNova Solutions waited on the seventh floor at nine.

For a year and a half, very little had changed.

Arjun was not unhappy in any dramatic way. He had a steady job, a small apartment, and enough money to live without worrying about every bill. He simply moved through his days as though they were tasks on a list. He spoke when necessary and kept his headphones on the rest of the time.

One crowded morning, someone shoved past him as the bus arrived. His footing slipped near the curb. Before he could fall into the road, a hand caught his arm and pulled him back onto the pavement.

The woman who had grabbed him was breathing hard. Her bag had slid off one shoulder, and shock had widened her eyes.

“Are you all right?”

Arjun looked at the scrape on his palm, then at her.

“I'm fine. Thank you.”

He stepped toward the bus.

The woman remained where she was, stunned by how quickly he had returned to his headphones. Her name was Nisha Fujimoto, and it was her first day at TechNova.

They boarded the same bus, though she sat several rows behind him. At the office, she discovered that the quiet man from the bus stop worked on her team.

Their colleague, Sahil, introduced them.

“Arjun, this is Nisha. Nisha, this is Arjun. He's good at his job and terrible at small talk.”

“I can hear you,” Arjun said.

“Then the introduction is working.”

Nisha smiled. Arjun gave a small nod and returned to his screen.

She had saved him from a fall. He had thanked her. As far as he was concerned, the matter was finished.

Nisha, however, had begun to wonder what kind of person could be so distant after someone had reached out to help him.

Chapter Two The Lunchbox

Nisha's first week was busy. She learned the software, memorised the office layout, and tried to understand why the printer jammed whenever anyone was in a hurry.

She also learned that Arjun ate lunch alone.

At first, she assumed he preferred it. Then she noticed that he often worked through the lunch hour and returned to his desk with nothing but coffee.

One afternoon, she placed a small lunchbox beside his keyboard.

“You forgot to eat,” she said.

“I didn't forget.”

“Then you chose not to. That sounds worse.”

She explained that her mother had packed too much food. It was an excuse, and both of them knew it. Arjun looked at the lunchbox as if it were a document requiring careful review.

“Thank you,” he said.

“Please return the container. It's my favourite.”

The next day, the lunchbox came back washed and neatly dried. A small note had been tucked beneath the lid: *Thank you. The food was good.*

Nisha kept the note.

After that, their routine changed by a few minutes at a time. She asked him questions about the project. He gave useful answers. She asked whether he wanted tea. He said yes. Sometimes she sat nearby during lunch, talking about the book she was reading or the strange things her train had witnessed.

Arjun rarely volunteered personal details, but he listened. When she mentioned an important presentation, he remembered the date and checked in afterward.

“You remembered?” she asked.

“You told me.”

“Most people forget.”

“I don't see why I should.”

It was the closest thing to a compliment she had received from him, and she carried it back to her desk with an unreasonable amount of happiness.

Chapter Three Rain, Cake, and Small Changes

Nisha did not try to transform Arjun into an outgoing person. She simply kept inviting him into the small parts of life he seemed to overlook.

When rain trapped them outside the office, she shared her umbrella. When a new café opened near the station, she asked whether he would help her judge the coffee. When the team held an office party, she found him standing near the window and brought him a plate before he could escape.

He began to notice things in return.

Nisha was friendly with everyone, but she was not endlessly cheerful. On difficult days she grew quiet and worked through her worries with careful concentration. She was ambitious, sometimes stubborn, and willing to admit when she was wrong. Her kindness was not a performance; it was a decision she kept making.

One evening, he found a slice of cake on his desk. No note. No announcement. Just cake.

He asked Sahil who had left it.

Sahil looked up from his monitor. “You tell me. Who keeps bringing you food?”

Arjun glanced across the office. Nisha was speaking with a colleague, laughing at something on the screen.

For the first time, he wondered what she was like when she was not trying to include him.

He started making tea for two. He asked about her weekends. He walked with her to the station instead of leaving the building alone. His timetable changed—not because she demanded it, but because he found himself wanting to make room.

Then a new employee named Kaito joined their team. He was confident, funny, and quick to offer Nisha help. Arjun watched them laughing over a project and felt a discomfort he could not explain.

Sahil noticed.

“You're jealous.”

“I'm not.”

“You have been staring at the same spreadsheet for ten minutes.”

Arjun looked at the screen. He had not read a single cell.

He was beginning to understand that the world had become less predictable because someone mattered to him.

Chapter Four The Question He Avoided

For several days, Arjun became distant again. He told himself he was being sensible. Nisha had her own life, and he had no right to expect her attention. It was easier to return to his old routine than to admit he was afraid of wanting something.

Nisha noticed the change.

“Did I do something?” she asked one evening in the elevator.

“No.”

“Then why are you avoiding me?”

“I've been busy.”

“You've always been busy. You used to at least look at me when I spoke.”

The elevator reached the ground floor. Neither stepped out immediately.

Arjun could have offered another excuse. Instead, he admitted that he had become confused about his feelings and did not know what to do with them.

Nisha's expression softened, but she did not rescue him from the conversation.

“You don't have to decide everything tonight,” she said. “But don't punish me for something you haven't told me.”

He apologised.

They walked to the station together. At the crossing, Nisha told him about a difficult relationship from her past and how it had made her wary of people who went quiet instead of speaking honestly.

“I don't need you to be cheerful all the time,” she said. “I need to know where I stand.”

Arjun thought about that long after they parted.

The next morning, he sent her a message first.

*Would you like to have lunch with me today? Somewhere outside the office?*

Her reply arrived almost immediately.

*I was beginning to think you'd never ask.*

Chapter Five A Better Kind of Ordinary

Their lunch dates began simply. A small restaurant near the station. A walk beside the river. A bookstore where Nisha could browse for an hour while Arjun pretended he had not started enjoying it.

They learned to ask rather than assume. If Arjun needed quiet, he said so. If Nisha felt hurt, she explained why instead of hoping he would guess. Their differences did not disappear, but they became easier to understand.

One evening, Nisha told him about her friend Sneha, who had warned her not to mistake Arjun's quietness for indifference.

“She said you looked at me like you were trying to memorise everything I did.”

Arjun nearly dropped his chopsticks.

“That sounds exaggerated.”

“Is it?”

He considered lying. Then he shook his head.

Nisha smiled, not triumphantly but with relief.

They had their first serious argument a few weeks later. Arjun cancelled a plan at the last minute because of work and did not tell her until she had already arrived. Nisha was upset—not because work mattered, but because he had left her waiting without an explanation.

He apologised without defending himself. They talked about how to handle busy days, and he followed through the next time work became overwhelming.

That was when Nisha began to trust the change. Not because Arjun made grand promises, but because he practised being present.

On a rainy evening, they stood beneath the station awning. Nisha looked up at the sky.

“Do you remember the first time we met?”

“You caught me before I fell.”

“And then you walked away like I had handed you a receipt.”

“I thanked you.”

“Very politely. Like a customer service email.”

He laughed. She looked pleased, then suddenly shy.

“I'm glad I caught you,” she said.

“So am I.”

He reached for her hand, slowly enough that she could decline. Nisha placed hers in his.

The rain continued around them. Neither rushed to leave.

Chapter Six Alive

Months later, Arjun's routine still began at 7:15. He still reached the bus stop around 8:05 and arrived at the office before nine. But the schedule no longer felt like a wall around his life.

Some mornings he walked with Nisha. Some evenings they cooked together. He had begun taking photographs on the way home, noticing the light on shop windows and the colours of the sky after rain. Nisha encouraged him to keep the photographs, even when he dismissed them as ordinary.

“They're your ordinary,” she told him. “That's why they're worth keeping.”

Their relationship became official in the least dramatic way possible: Nisha asked if she could introduce him as her boyfriend at a friend's dinner, and Arjun said yes. Then they spent ten minutes debating which train to take.

A year later, they returned to the bus stop where they had first met. The bench had been repainted, and the pavement repaired. The city moved around them as it always had.

Nisha looked at the place and laughed. “You didn't even look back that morning.”

“I was embarrassed.”

“You were impossible.”

“I know.”

She slipped her hand into his. He held it, remembering the small hand that had pulled him back onto the pavement before he could fall.

A bus arrived. The doors opened. Neither moved.

“Are we taking this one?” he asked.

Nisha leaned her head lightly against his shoulder.

“Not yet.”

The bus departed, leaving the stop quiet for a moment.

Arjun had once believed a good life was one that ran on time and asked nothing unexpected of him. Nisha had not given him a new personality. She had helped him notice that life was already happening around him—in shared meals, cancelled plans repaired by honest apologies, tea made for two, and a hand held without needing a reason.

The road home was long, but they walked it together.

He was no longer simply getting through the day.

He was living it.
