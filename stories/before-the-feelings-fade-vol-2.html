CHAPTER ONE
Two Years, Three Notifications

Two years after Sota Mori gave Airi Tanaka a letter folded four times, his phone knew more about her daily life than he had ever expected to.

It knew when she posted a photograph of a cloudy sky with the caption weather that looks like it forgot its homework. It knew when she shared a picture of a dessert so carefully decorated that Sota replied, Is it cake or a school project? It knew when she sent him a reel of a cat falling asleep in a laundry basket at 1:17 a.m., followed by a message that said, this is you during exam week.

He had replied, I have never looked that peaceful in my life.

She sent back three laughing emojis.

That was what they were now: friends who had each other's contact details, who sometimes chatted on Instagram, and who could sit in the same group without the air turning strange. Sota had not confessed again. Airi had not asked him to. Her answer from middle school had been clear, and he had respected it.

He was proud of that. Mostly.

“You're smiling at your phone,” Yuto announced from the other side of the table.

Sota locked his screen. “I'm reading something.”

“Your phone is black.”

“It's a very dark novel.”

Yuto leaned forward. “Does the dark novel have a name?”

“It has a block button.”

“Ah. Romance.”

Sota threw a sugar packet at him. It missed by an embarrassing distance and landed in a stranger's shopping bag.

They were meeting their old classmates that Saturday, a loose plan that had begun in a group chat and grown into twelve people arguing about lunch. Airi had suggested a café near the station. Mei had objected because the chairs were uncomfortable. Haru had suggested a ramen place. Nobody had read the menu he linked. After forty-seven messages, they had chosen the café Airi suggested in the first place.

Democracy is beautiful, Sota typed into the group chat.

You voted for ramen, Mei replied.

Democracy has flaws.

At the café, Airi arrived with Mei and two paper bags of snacks. She waved when she saw Sota, as casually as she waved at everyone else.

“Mori-kun. You look tired.”

“Thank you. I've been cultivating that look.”

“It's working.”

He laughed before he could stop himself. She laughed too, then turned to ask Yuto whether he'd ordered anything yet. The moment passed without becoming anything more.

That was the strange comfort of being friends with someone who had once mattered so much: sometimes it felt ordinary enough to forget. Then a small thing—a laugh, a familiar expression, the way she tucked her hair behind her ear while reading the menu—would remind him that forgetting and moving on were not the same task.

He didn't want to make that her problem. So he took the seat beside Yuto, argued with Haru about whether fries counted as a meal, and let the afternoon become what it was supposed to be.

On the train home, a notification appeared.

Airi Tanaka sent you a message.

Mei says your fries argument was embarrassing. I agree.

Sota smiled and typed, Tell Mei that history will vindicate me.

History has already unfollowed you.

He laughed into his sleeve, and for a few stops, the memory in his chest felt less like a wound and more like an old song he could listen to without needing it to change its ending.

CHAPTER TWO
The Group Chat Is a Crime Scene

The group chat had no administrator in any meaningful sense. Yuto had created it, but he had no authority. Mei had renamed it six times. Haru posted blurry photographs and claimed they were artistic. Airi sent reminders about plans that everyone ignored until the last possible moment.

Its current name was People Who Cannot Pick a Restaurant.

On Tuesday night, Mei proposed a day trip to the seaside arcade and shopping street. The plan involved trains, lunch, an arcade tournament, and a sunset photo that nobody had requested but everyone was apparently expected to participate in.

“Why do we need a sunset photo?” Sota asked in the chat.

Because we are making memories, Mei replied.

Can we make memories without standing in a line while you adjust the camera for eleven minutes? Yuto asked.

No, Mei said.

Airi sent a sticker of a tiny rabbit holding a sign that read: please cooperate.

Sota saved it. Then, embarrassed by the fact that he had saved it, he stared at the screen for a full ten seconds before putting his phone down.

On Saturday, the group met outside the station. Haru arrived late with a drink he had bought at a different station. Mei had printed a schedule. Yuto immediately lost it. Airi was wearing a light jacket and carrying a canvas bag covered in small embroidered stars.

“We are already seven minutes behind,” Mei announced.

“The day has barely begun,” Sota said.

“That is why this is alarming.”

At the arcade, they divided into teams for a rhythm-game challenge. Sota was terrible at it. He blamed the buttons, the screen, the music, and one suspicious ceiling light.

“Maybe the machine senses fear,” Airi said, standing behind him.

“Then it should be afraid of me.”

His score flashed across the screen. It was the lowest score of the group.

“The machine has made its decision,” she said solemnly.

He turned around. “You're enjoying this.”

“A little.”

She was smiling. He was smiling. Behind them, Yuto was attempting to photograph the scoreboard and accidentally had his own reflection in the picture. For one bright, ridiculous moment, nothing in Sota's life needed to be more complicated than that.

Later, the group crowded around a claw machine. Airi wanted a small blue penguin plush. The claw picked it up, carried it halfway across the machine, and dropped it.

“It's a scam,” Airi declared.

“It's a machine designed to extract money from people who believe in penguins,” Sota said.

“Then help me defeat capitalism.”

He tried once. Failed. Tried again. Failed more impressively.

Yuto filmed the second failure and narrated it like a sports commentator. Mei was laughing so hard she had to sit down. Airi covered her mouth, eyes bright with amusement.

On the third attempt, Haru won the penguin by accident while trying to demonstrate that the claw was rigged.

Everyone stared at him.

“I have a gift,” Haru said.

“You have stolen my victory,” Sota told him.

Airi accepted the penguin and tucked it into her bag. “Thank you, team.”

“I demand partial credit,” Sota said.

“You may have emotional credit.”

“Can I exchange it for money?”

“No refunds.”

That evening, Mei posted the group photo. Sota stood near the edge, making a face because Yuto had counted down too quickly. Airi was holding the penguin, laughing at something outside the frame. The picture was messy, tilted, and full of people who looked as if they belonged in one another's lives.

Sota liked it. Then he put his phone away and went to help his mother carry groceries upstairs.

He could enjoy a day with Airi without turning it into a promise. He repeated that thought until it sounded less like an instruction and more like something he might eventually believe.

CHAPTER THREE
Read at 11:48

There were rules to chatting with friends online. Sota had never written them down, but he understood them well enough to pretend he did.

Rule one: a message could be answered immediately, later, or not at all, depending on life.

Rule two: a laughing emoji was not a legal document.

Rule three: if someone sent you a photograph of their lunch, you were not required to determine whether it was a hidden emotional signal.

He had invented rule three after spending seven minutes wondering why Airi had sent him a picture of a convenience-store pudding.

It was on sale, she had explained. That is the whole story.

He had replied, Thank you for this breaking news.

They messaged about ordinary things: an assignment, a film everyone was watching, a teacher's habit of saying “last point” and then speaking for another fifteen minutes. Sometimes Airi started the conversation. Sometimes he did. Most of the time, their messages ended when one of them got busy.

That was healthy. That was normal. That was the kind of friendship he had promised himself he would accept.

Then one Thursday, she sent a message at 11:48 p.m.

Are you awake?

Sota was awake, though only because he had been trying to finish an assignment while a video of a man repairing an old clock played in another tab.

Unfortunately.

Can you check whether this paragraph makes sense? I have read it so many times the words look fake.

She sent a screenshot. He read it twice, corrected a confusing sentence, and pointed out a missing word. They exchanged a few messages about the assignment. Then she sent a sticker of a sleepy bear collapsing face-first onto a pillow.

Thank you. You saved my grade.

My invoice is in the mail.

Can I pay in snacks?

Depends on the snacks.

You're difficult.

And yet you came to me for proofreading.

She sent a laughing emoji and said goodnight.

Sota put his phone face down. For a few seconds, the room was quiet except for the clock video ticking softly from the laptop speakers.

It was a normal conversation. Friends helped each other. Friends joked. Friends said goodnight.

He knew that. The problem was not that Airi had done anything unusual. The problem was how easily his heart could take an ordinary kindness and build a little house around it.

He opened his notes app and typed a sentence he had no intention of sending:

She can be kind to me without it meaning she wants me.

He read it once, then deleted it. He didn't want to turn his life into a list of rules designed to protect him from his own feelings. He wanted to learn how to live without needing every moment to prove something.

The next morning, Airi posted a story of the sunrise. Sota watched it while eating toast. He didn't reply. Not because he was punishing her or trying to appear distant, but because the sky was beautiful and he had nothing to add.

For once, that was enough.

CHAPTER FOUR
A Very Unromantic Festival

The summer festival was Mei's idea, which meant the group had a spreadsheet before they had tickets.

“Nobody is allowed to disappear,” she told them at the station.

“What if I am kidnapped by a takoyaki stand?” Haru asked.

“Then text us.”

“What if they take my phone?”

“Then you will finally be free of notifications.”

Sota arrived in a plain dark shirt and immediately regretted it when he saw everyone else. Yuto wore a shirt with a cartoon shark. Haru had dressed like he was attending a job interview. Mei wore a summer dress and had somehow convinced three people to carry things for her. Airi wore a pale blue yukata with a small white pattern that reminded Sota of scattered stars.

He noticed it. Of course he noticed it. Then he said, “Your outfit looks nice.”

“Thank you,” Airi replied. “Yours looks comfortable.”

“That's the nicest thing anyone has said to me today.”

“You should meet more people.”

She smiled and went to help Mei fix the ribbon at the back of her outfit. Sota watched for half a second, then turned toward Yuto, who was trying to eat a festival snack without getting sauce on his shirt.

“You have sauce on your face,” Sota said.

“Where?”

“Your face.”

“Be specific.”

The festival was crowded, warm, and full of things that cost more than they should. They played a ring-toss game, lost at it, bought shaved ice, and argued over which stall had the longest line. Sota and Airi ended up on the same team for a festival quiz only because Mei insisted that the teams needed “balanced levels of common sense.”

They lost by one point because Sota confidently answered a question about a historical date with a number that was not even the right century.

“I was testing the quiz,” he said.

“And the quiz passed,” Airi replied.

At the food stalls, she remembered that he didn't like overly sweet drinks and passed him a bottle of cold tea instead of the fruit soda everyone else had bought.

“You remembered,” he said before he could stop himself.

“You complained about the melon soda for twenty minutes last time.”

“That sounds like me.”

“It was a very detailed complaint.”

He accepted the tea. It was a small thing, and it was kind. He let it be both without asking it to be more.

When the fireworks began, the group gathered on a grassy slope near the river. Mei set up her phone on a tiny tripod. Haru complained about the angle. Yuto stepped on the tripod. The first photo was a blur of elbows and a very bright patch of sky.

“One more,” Mei ordered.

“The fireworks are ending,” Yuto protested.

“Then smile faster.”

The next photo caught them all laughing. Airi stood between Mei and Sota, looking toward the camera. The fireworks bloomed above them, blurred into soft circles of light.

For a moment, Sota wished the evening could stay exactly as it was—not because he believed it meant something romantic, but because he was happy and afraid that happiness always had to be followed by a question.

He didn't ask one.

On the way home, the group split across two train cars. Sota ended up with Yuto and Haru. Airi sent a message to the group chat: Everyone home safely? Reply so Mei can stop threatening us.

One by one, the replies appeared.

Sota typed, Alive. Slightly poorer. Emotionally defeated by ring toss.

Airi replied with a penguin sticker.

He saved that one too. This time, he didn't feel the need to explain it to himself.

CHAPTER FIVE
The Rumour That Lasted Twenty Minutes

The rumour began with a photograph of Sota holding Airi's bag while she tied the strap of her sandal.

It was a perfectly ordinary photograph. It had been taken during the festival, posted in a group story, and viewed by approximately thirty people. Unfortunately, one of those people was a classmate who believed every picture needed a romantic interpretation.

“So,” Haru said the next day, sliding into the seat across from Sota, “when were you planning to tell us?”

Sota looked up from his lunch. “Tell you what?”

Haru's expression suggested he had been waiting his entire life for this moment. “You and Tanaka.”

Yuto slowly lowered his chopsticks.

“Me and Tanaka what?”

“The bag. The festival. The way you were standing. The fact that she gave you tea.”

“She gave me tea because I hate melon soda.”

“That's how it starts.”

“How what starts?”

“The tea.”

Yuto pressed his lips together. His shoulders began shaking.

“Don't laugh,” Sota warned him.

“I'm not laughing.”

“Your whole body is laughing.”

Haru leaned forward. “I'm only saying people are talking.”

“People need hobbies.”

“You could always make it official.”

Sota's smile faded just enough that Yuto noticed. The conversation had crossed from ridiculous to uncomfortable, and neither of them knew how to turn it around without making the silence louder.

“There is nothing to make official,” Sota said. “We're friends.”

Haru blinked, then sat back. “Oh. Sorry. I didn't mean to make it weird.”

“You succeeded anyway.”

Yuto finally let out a laugh, though he quickly turned it into a cough. Haru apologised again, and the topic changed to a game tournament. The rumour lasted twenty minutes, mostly because Mei entered the chat and threatened to expose everyone's worst photographs if they continued discussing other people's private lives.

Later, Sota received a message from Airi.

Mei told me about Haru's theory. Sorry if that was awkward.

He typed, It's fine. Haru thinks a vending machine has a tragic backstory.

It might.

Don't encourage him.

She sent a laughing emoji, then asked whether he was coming to the next group outing. He said yes. The conversation moved on.

Sota stared at the chat for a little while after it ended. A part of him had been relieved by the rumour's stupidity. Another part had felt something sharper: the reminder that other people could imagine a future for him and Airi in a sentence, while he could not even ask whether such a future existed without risking the friendship they had built.

But he had no right to treat that imagined future as something Airi owed him. She had already answered once. He had never asked again. She was allowed to be friendly, and he was allowed to feel complicated about it, as long as he didn't turn those feelings into a debt she had to repay.

He closed Instagram and went for a walk. The evening was warm, the neighbourhood shops were beginning to switch on their lights, and a child on a bicycle was ringing the bell at nobody in particular.

By the time he returned home, the rumour had become a joke in the group chat. Haru had changed his profile picture to a cup of tea. Yuto had named him Love Detective: Unlicensed.

Sota laughed at the messages. He could laugh at the joke. He could be Airi's friend. He could also admit, privately, that a small part of him still wondered what it would feel like if the answer had been different.

He didn't need to punish himself for wondering. He only needed to remember that wondering wasn't a promise.

CHAPTER SIX
The Photo Booth Disaster

Mei declared that the group needed a proper photo together. Not a blurry festival picture, not a screenshot from a video call, and definitely not a photograph in which Yuto looked like he had just seen a ghost. A proper photo.

Her solution was a photo booth in the shopping district.

“There are eight of us,” Sota pointed out. “The booth is designed for four.”

“Then we will become closer as people.”

“That is not how physics works.”

“Physics is invited to leave.”

They squeezed into the booth in two rounds. The first group emerged with stickers of enlarged eyes, glittery crowns, and a digital moustache that had been placed over Mei's forehead instead of her mouth. She declared the machine an enemy of art.

The second group included Sota and Airi. Yuto operated the screen, selected the wrong layout, and accidentally set the timer to three seconds. Everyone scrambled. Sota tried to move out of the way. Airi grabbed his sleeve to keep him from knocking into the wall. The camera flashed while they were both looking at Yuto with identical expressions of alarm.

It was the best photograph of the day.

“Look at this,” Airi said, laughing as the print emerged. “We look like we've been caught committing a crime.”

“The crime was trusting Yuto.”

“A serious mistake.”

They split the sticker sheet between them because there was no room for everyone to take one. Airi gave Sota a tiny star sticker. He placed it on the back of his phone case without thinking.

“That's going to fall off,” Yuto said.

“Then I will mourn it.”

“You could put it in a drawer.”

“I will mourn it in a drawer.”

They spent the rest of the afternoon browsing bookstores and arguing about which film to watch next. Airi and Mei found a shelf of old mystery novels. Sota and Yuto got trapped in a conversation with Haru about whether a detective could solve a case using only convenience-store receipts. It was the kind of day that produced no grand revelation, only a handful of jokes that would probably be repeated for months.

On the train home, Airi posted the photo-booth strip to her story. She tagged everyone. Sota looked at it, smiled at the ridiculous expression on his own face, and moved on to the next post.

Then he noticed the tiny star sticker on the back of his phone. He pressed one corner down where it had begun to peel.

It wasn't a sign. It wasn't a promise. It was a sticker from a silly afternoon with friends.

He was learning to let small things stay small.

CHAPTER SEVEN
The Old Classroom

The school announced an alumni afternoon in early autumn, inviting former students to visit the campus and help with a small library fundraiser. Yuto signed up immediately because he wanted to see whether the teachers still remembered his terrible grades. Airi said she would go if Mei went. Mei agreed only after learning there would be a stall selling homemade sweets.

Sota hesitated before adding his name to the list.

He had returned to the school once after graduation, alone, and had thought that the visit would be a private farewell to the boy who had written a letter folded four times. He had not expected the past to become a place he could visit with friends.

On the afternoon of the fundraiser, the old building looked both familiar and strangely small. The corridor walls had been repainted. A new noticeboard stood near the stairs. The shoe lockers were the same, though the labels had changed.

“I can't believe they still use this staircase,” Yuto said. “I nearly died here once.”

“You slipped on a banana sticker,” Sota replied.

“A dangerous banana sticker.”

“It was a drawing of a banana.”

“Psychological warfare.”

Airi walked ahead with Mei to find the library. Sota watched them disappear around the corner, then followed Yuto toward the classroom wing. The room where he had once sat near the back was open. Inside, sunlight rested on the desks in long rectangles.

He stopped at the doorway.

For a second, he could see the old scene as clearly as a photograph: his notebook, the spaceship in the margin, Airi in the second row writing with her head bent over the page. He remembered the letter and the way his hands had trembled when he gave it to her. He remembered her answer, gentle and final in the way an honest answer could be.

“You okay?” Yuto asked.

Sota nodded. “Yeah. Just remembering.”

Yuto didn't tease him. He stepped inside and looked around. “We were so dramatic about everything.”

“We still are.”

“True. I nearly cried when the cafeteria stopped selling the good bread.”

“You wrote a petition.”

“It had signatures.”

Sota laughed, and the classroom became a room again instead of a time machine.

At the fundraiser, Airi was arranging books on a table. When she saw him, she waved him over to help carry a box. They worked with Mei and two other former classmates, sorting books and arguing over whether a mystery novel with a missing cover counted as damaged or “atmospheric.”

There was no private conversation, no dramatic pause, no sudden discovery that the past had been waiting to restart. There was only a shared afternoon, a familiar building, and the people they had become since they last sat in those desks.

When the fundraiser ended, the group took a photograph at the school gate. Yuto blinked. Haru looked away. Mei's smile was enormous. Airi stood beside Sota, holding a box of leftover sweets.

“This is going to be the worst photo yet,” Sota said.

“Then it will be our most honest one,” Airi replied.

That night, she sent him the photograph. He saved it to the same folder as the festival picture and the photo-booth strip.

For once, the memories didn't feel like evidence of a future he had missed. They were evidence of a life that had continued.

CHAPTER EIGHT
The Thing He Never Asked

Autumn turned into winter by small degrees: colder train platforms, warm drinks held between both hands, and the sudden appearance of scarves in every shop window. The group chat became a place to complain about assignments and exchange photographs of meals that looked better than they tasted.

Sota and Airi still talked. Some days they exchanged only two messages. Other days they chatted for half an hour about a film, a strange advertisement, or whether Yuto had finally learned how to use the group calendar. Nothing about their friendship had become a secret code. It was simply friendship, and Sota tried to let himself enjoy it without asking it to become something else.

Still, some evenings were harder than others.

One night, while looking for a file on his laptop, he found a photograph of the old letter. He had taken it years ago before folding it, perhaps because he wanted to remember the exact words he had been brave enough to write. The paper itself was long gone, but the image remained: his uneven handwriting, the small crease where he had pressed too hard, the sentence that said she didn't have to feel the same.

He read it again.

He had meant every word. He still did.

But there was another truth he had never written down: after Airi's answer, a small part of him had quietly left a space open. He had not decided to wait for her. He had not made a plan to confess again. He had simply never closed the door in his own mind, because closing it felt like admitting that the story had ended before it had really begun.

Two years had passed. He had laughed with her, helped her with an assignment, shared group outings, and watched her become more confident in ways he admired. He had also built a private collection of tiny moments that he sometimes revisited when he was lonely. None of those moments belonged to Airi in the way he had secretly wanted them to.

That realization hurt, but it wasn't her fault.

He opened a blank document and wrote:

I never asked for another chance. I think I was afraid of making you uncomfortable, and afraid of hearing the same answer again. Maybe I told myself that if I waited long enough, I wouldn't have to choose between hope and acceptance.

He paused, then continued.

But not asking doesn't mean I wasn't hoping. It only means I kept that hope to myself.

He didn't save the document. He didn't send it. It wasn't a confession meant for Airi; it was a truth he needed to admit to himself.

The next morning, Yuto called to ask if Sota was joining the group for lunch. Sota said yes. Yuto then spent three minutes explaining why a new restaurant's menu was “structurally suspicious.” Sota laughed until he had to move the phone away from his ear.

Life kept offering him ordinary things to do. He was beginning to understand that moving on might happen that way—not through one grand decision, but through a thousand small moments in which he chose to live the day he actually had.

CHAPTER NINE
Please Don't Get the Wrong Idea

The message arrived on a Wednesday evening, between a photograph of Mei's overcooked pancakes in the group chat and a reminder from Yuto that everyone had forgotten to choose a date for the next outing.

Sota had just returned home. His bag sat beside the desk, his shoes were still on, and a half-finished glass of water waited on the table. He opened Instagram without thinking.

Airi Tanaka sent you a message.

Hey, can I say something? I hope you won't take it the wrong way.

He sat down.

Of course. What's up?

Three dots appeared, disappeared, and appeared again. He watched them longer than he should have.

Then her message arrived.

I've been thinking about this for a while, and I just want to make sure we're on the same page. I really do like talking with you and hanging out with everyone, but I don't want you to misunderstand anything. We're just friends, okay? I don't want to give you the wrong idea.

Sota read it once. Then again.

There was nothing cruel in the message. No accusation, no mockery, no suggestion that he had done something wrong. Airi was trying to be careful. Maybe someone had made another joke. Maybe she had noticed something in his behaviour. Maybe she simply wanted to be clear before an ordinary friendship became complicated. He didn't know, and he couldn't decide what her reason had been.

What he knew was how the words felt when they reached him.

Two years earlier, she had told him she couldn't return his feelings. He had accepted it. He had not asked again, had not tried to persuade her, had not demanded a different answer. They had become friends, slowly and naturally. He had told himself that friendship was enough because he valued her presence and didn't want to make her uncomfortable.

He had not realised how much of him was still holding a tiny, unspoken possibility open.

Now it felt as though he had been rejected a second time for a question he had never asked.

He put the phone down, then picked it up again. He wanted to write something perfectly calm, something that would protect her from guilt and him from embarrassment. Every sentence sounded either too cold or too honest.

At last, he typed:

I understand. Don't worry, you haven't done anything wrong. I know we're friends, and I've never wanted you to feel pressured. Thanks for being honest with me.

He read it carefully and sent it.

Airi replied a minute later.

Thank you for understanding. I really value our friendship.

He stared at the word friendship until it stopped looking like a word and became a boundary he could finally see.

His phone vibrated again. The group chat had become active. Haru had posted a photograph of a sandwich that had somehow collapsed in the middle. Mei was blaming the bread. Yuto was proposing a scientific investigation.

Sota almost laughed. The ordinary world was still there, carrying on with its ridiculous little problems. But something inside him had gone quiet—not the kind of quiet that comes from peace, but the kind that comes when a possibility you never admitted to anyone is finally gone.

He wasn't angry with Airi. She had been honest. He had promised himself he would respect her answer, and he still meant to. What hurt was discovering that he had been grieving a future that had never belonged to him.

He turned off the screen and sat in the dim room. For the first time, he let himself feel the whole disappointment without immediately explaining it away. He had hoped, even when he had pretended not to. He had cared, even when he had been careful. And now he had to stop treating the space between them as a question that might someday be answered differently.

Outside, a train passed beyond the buildings, its windows sliding through the dark like a row of small, distant rooms.

CHAPTER TEN
The Face I Never Forgot

Sota did not wake up the next morning as a different person.

He still checked his phone before getting out of bed. He still forgot where he had put his keys. He still bought the wrong notebook for a class because he had been thinking about lunch instead of the shopping list. The world did not pause to honour the quiet ending of a hope he had never spoken aloud.

For several days, he answered Airi's messages when they came, but he stopped searching for reasons to start conversations. He wasn't trying to punish her or make her wonder what she had done. He simply needed room to hear his own thoughts without turning every notification into a small test.

He told Yuto the truth one evening while they walked from the station.

“She messaged me,” Sota said. “She wanted to make sure I knew we're just friends.”

Yuto's expression softened. “Did you tell her you were hoping for more?”

“No. I never asked again after the first time.”

“Then why does it feel like another rejection?”

Sota watched the traffic lights change. “Because I think I kept a chance alive in my head. I didn't do anything with it. I just… never let it go completely.”

Yuto was quiet for a moment. “That sounds painful.”

“It is.”

“Do you hate her?”

“No.” Sota answered immediately. “She didn't promise me anything. She was clear the first time, too. I think I just wasn't as finished with the feeling as I thought I was.”

Yuto nodded. He didn't offer a clever solution. For once, he didn't try to turn sadness into a joke.

“You don't have to stop being friends today,” he said. “You just have to stop asking the friendship to become something it isn't.”

Sota carried that sentence home with him.

A week later, he returned to the old school alone. The alumni afternoon had left him with a memory of the classroom that felt less like a shrine and more like an ordinary place. He wanted to see it once more, not to search for an answer, but to say goodbye to the version of himself who had sat there believing that courage meant finally telling someone how he felt.

The room was empty. The desks had been rearranged. Sunlight rested on the second row, where Airi had once sat with her notebook open. Sota stood by the doorway, then walked to the back and sat in his old seat.

He remembered the letter folded four times. He remembered Airi's careful answer. He remembered the small moments that had become precious to him: borrowed notes, a smile across the room, the almost-touch during a science project. He also remembered the years afterward—the jokes, the group photos, the late-night proofreading, the penguin plush, and the messages that had made them ordinary friends.

Those memories were real. Their meaning did not have to be romantic to matter.

He had once believed that moving on meant the face would fade, that the memory would become blurry enough to stop hurting. Maybe that was never the point. Airi would probably remain a face he remembered. She had been part of his first honest attempt to love someone, and part of the lesson that feelings could be sincere without being returned.

But remembering her did not mean waiting for her.

He took out his phone. The group chat was arranging another outing. Mei wanted to visit a bookshop. Haru wanted to eat somewhere cheap. Yuto had suggested a place that was neither a bookshop nor cheap. Airi had replied with a sticker of a rabbit staring into the distance.

Sota smiled. He could join them when he was ready. He could be kind to her without searching for hidden meanings. He could take a little distance when he needed it and return when friendship felt honest again. Moving on did not require turning a person into a villain or pretending that the past had meant nothing.

He typed into the group chat:

Bookshop first. Food afterward. And if anyone suggests a restaurant with a menu that looks like a tax document, I'm leaving.

Yuto replied immediately: He means you, Haru.

Haru sent a photograph of a menu.

Mei wrote: I'm muting all of you.

Sota laughed, and the sound surprised him with how normal it was.

Before leaving the classroom, he looked once more toward the window. He no longer needed the room to give him a different ending. The boy who had written that letter had done something brave. The young man sitting here now could do something brave, too: accept an answer without asking it to become a promise, and let the rest of his life continue.

Outside, the afternoon was bright. The school gate stood open, and students crossed the courtyard in noisy groups. Sota walked through it without looking back.

He would remember Airi Tanaka. Maybe for a long time. Maybe always.

But the face he never forgot did not have to be the face he spent his life waiting for.

Some feelings stay with us. Some hopes have to be put down. And sometimes growing up is not forgetting the person who changed your life, but learning to let them remain a memory without asking them to become your future.

The feeling had not been meaningless.

It was simply finished.
