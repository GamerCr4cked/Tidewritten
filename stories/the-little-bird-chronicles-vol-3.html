Chapter One The Last Year at Seiran

My final year at Seiran began with a message from Hina. *Third year of high school for you. Second year of junior high for me. Don't become too important to eat lunch with me.* I read it in the corridor and smiled at my phone like an idiot. A year earlier, I had been the boy who watched the school gate and rehearsed conversations that never happened. Now Hina knew my habits, my terrible jokes, and the exact time I usually forgot to check the weather. We still met at the cafeteria whenever our schedules allowed, and most nights we talked until her eleven o'clock limit approached. But graduation was no longer an abstract idea. Entrance exams, university applications, and the possibility of moving to another city waited at the end of the school year. I tried not to think about it. At lunch, Hina appeared with a new navy-blue hairband. I noticed immediately and said nothing. Five minutes later, she narrowed her eyes at me. “You noticed, didn't you?” “Noticed what?” “My hairband.” “I notice many things.” “Apparently not the important ones.” I laughed. She tried to look annoyed, but the corner of her mouth gave her away. “It's nice,” I said at last. “Thank you.” Her voice softened. “I wondered if you'd notice.” The words were simple, but I carried them into class like a secret. That afternoon, Daichi found me staring at my notes without turning a page. “You're thinking about her again.” “I'm thinking about exams.” “Then why did you write her name in the margin?” I closed the notebook. He laughed, then grew more serious. “You know you don't have to keep guessing forever, right?” I knew. Knowing and doing were different skills. For two years, Hina and I had learned how to be comfortable together. We shared our worries, remembered each other's small preferences, and let silences remain quiet instead of filling them with excuses. I did not want to rush her. I did not want to turn our friendship into a question she had to answer before she was ready. But I also did not want to graduate without being honest. ---

Chapter Two The Rooftop

Daichi told me about the rooftop behind the high-school building. It was technically off-limits, though the old access door was usually left unlocked. I checked the rules before inviting Hina; the last thing I wanted was to make our favourite place a reason for trouble. We went only when it was permitted and stayed away from the edge. From there, Seiran looked smaller. The courtyard, the cafeteria, the gate, and the narrow road beside the station lay below us like parts of a map we had walked a thousand times. Hina stood by the railing, watching the late-afternoon light settle over the campus. “This is a good place,” she said. “I thought you'd like it.” “You brought me here because you wanted to show me the view?” “Mostly.” She turned toward me. “Mostly?” I looked down at my hands. “I wanted to talk somewhere quiet.” “Then talk.” It was an unfairly direct instruction. I sat on the safe side of the rooftop, my back against the wall. After a moment, Hina sat beside me, leaving a little space between us. We talked about graduation, about her upcoming exams, and about the way our school would feel different once I was no longer there every day. She admitted that she had thought about it too. “I don't want everything to change at once,” she said. “Neither do I.” The sun slipped lower. I rested my hand on the ground between us, not quite touching hers. Hina looked down at the space, then placed one finger lightly against the side of my hand. It was such a small gesture that someone else might have missed it. I did not. Neither of us spoke. We watched the sky turn amber, then rose, and finally a pale evening blue. On the way downstairs, she walked close enough that our sleeves brushed. At the door, she glanced at me. “You can invite me here again.” “Really?” “If you keep asking questions like that, I might change my mind.” I laughed, and she did too. That evening, I understood that the future still frightened me—but not because it was empty. It frightened me because there were people and places I wanted to carry with me, and I did not yet know how. ---

Chapter Three Things We Had Not Said

After the rooftop, our friendship did not transform overnight. It became a little more honest. We still argued about music and cafeteria food. Hina still called me strange when I overthought something, and I still reminded her that she was the person who had drawn a bird and named it after herself. But sometimes our eyes met and neither of us looked away immediately. One evening, we talked on the phone about everything and nothing. A teacher had assigned too much homework; Yui had started a new hobby; Daichi had told a joke so bad that it circled back around to being funny. Eventually, Hina asked, “Do you remember the first time we talked?” “Near the gate.” “You looked terrified.” “I was trying to think of something intelligent.” “Did it work?” “Clearly not.” She laughed. Then her voice grew quieter. “I was nervous too, you know.” I sat up. “You were?” “Of course. You and your friends were always looking over here. I couldn't tell whether you were laughing at me.” “I was never laughing at you.” “I know that now.” I apologised for the times my friends had been too loud. She told me she had never wanted me to stop being their friend—only to remember that she did not want to become a joke everyone discussed. “I should have understood sooner,” I said. “You did understand. You changed what you did.” That mattered to me more than praise. In October, the school festival returned. Hina took part in a poetry reading, standing beneath the hall lights with a paper in both hands. Her voice was steady, and she looked up only once while reading. Her eyes found mine across the audience. Afterward, I waited by the corridor. “Was that poem about someone?” I asked. “Maybe.” “Someone I know?” “Someone who used to be very quiet.” “That could be anyone.” “Then I suppose you'll have to keep wondering.” She walked away before I could reply. I did not chase after her. I was learning that not every question needed an immediate answer. Sometimes a person gave you a little truth, and the kindest response was to hold it carefully. As winter approached, we began visiting the rooftop more often when it was allowed. We talked about university, about the things we wanted to learn, and about the places we hoped to see. Hina shared her scarf with me when the wind grew cold. I brought hot drinks from the vending machine. Our shoulders touched beneath the same scarf. I could no longer pretend that my feelings were a passing fascination. They had grown slowly, alongside a real friendship. I knew Hina as a person—not as a mystery, not as a story I had invented, but as someone who could disagree with me, surprise me, and choose her own path. That was why I wanted to tell her the truth. ---

Chapter Four A Notebook for Two

The last school day before winter break arrived with cold wind and the smell of roasted sweet potatoes from a stall near the station. At lunch, Hina handed me a small wrapped package. “Don't open it here,” she said. “I wasn't going to.” “You were absolutely going to.” I waited until I got home. Inside was a notebook with a handmade cover, decorated with a tiny bird perched on a branch. The first page held a message in Hina's careful handwriting: *You were the first person who made this school feel a little less ordinary.* The rest of the pages were blank. A small note had been tucked inside the back cover. *Write stories, thoughts, anything. I wrote the first line because someone had to begin.* I read it several times. On New Year's Eve, we spoke on the phone while our families prepared for midnight. At 11:55, Hina asked what my resolution was. “To pass my exams.” “Boring.” “To stop forgetting my umbrella.” “Impossible.” “To keep talking to you.” There was a pause. “That one's good,” she said. The minutes slipped away. Outside, distant fireworks began to crackle. We counted down together, both of us speaking too quickly and laughing when we lost track. At midnight, Hina said, “Happy New Year, Haruto.” “Happy New Year.” “And… I want you to know something.” I waited. “You matter to me. More than I usually know how to say.” My throat tightened. “You matter to me too.” Neither of us rushed to add anything else. The silence was full, not empty. In January, she sat beside me on the rooftop while I wrote in the notebook she had given me. She was sketching something in her own book. “Don't move for a minute,” she said. “Why?” “Because I'm drawing you.” I froze so completely that she laughed. She worked in quiet concentration, occasionally looking from my face to the page. When she finally turned the sketch toward me, I saw myself in a few careful lines—my messy hair, the shape of my eyes, the small smile I had not realised I was wearing. “You made me look kinder than I am,” I said. “You are kinder than you think.” I did not know how to answer that, so I wrote a sentence in the notebook. *Sometimes another person sees the good in you before you learn how to see it yourself.* Hina read over my shoulder. “That's a good line,” she said. “I'll try not to let it go to my head.” “Too late.” We laughed. Outside, the winter sky was pale and wide. The notebook rested between us, its first pages no longer empty. ---

Chapter Five The Truth on the Page

February brought exam revision, cold mornings, and Valentine's Day decorations that seemed to appear overnight. I did not buy a grand gift. I wrote. On the next blank page of Hina's notebook, I described the small things I had come to treasure: the way she remembered details, the way she could make a rainy lunch break feel warm, the way she told me the truth even when a joke would have been easier. Then I wrote what I had been afraid to say. *Hina, I like you. Not because I gave you a nickname before I knew you, and not because I wanted every glance to mean something. I like the person I have come to know. I don't want you to answer because you feel you owe me one. I only wanted to be honest.* I gave the notebook back to her after class. She read the page on the rooftop. I waited, trying not to rehearse a dozen possible endings. When she finished, she held the notebook against her coat. “You really took your time,” she said. “I know.” “I was wondering when you'd tell me.” I looked up. “You were?” She nodded, a little shy now. “I like you too, Haruto. I just wanted us to understand what we were saying, not rush because everyone else seems to have a name for everything.” The relief that moved through me was so quiet I almost mistook it for calm. “So… what happens now?” I asked. “We keep being ourselves.” She smiled. “And maybe we stop pretending that sharing an earphone is an accident.” I laughed. She did too, and the tension between us softened. There was no audience, no announcement, no dramatic music. Just two students in winter coats, a notebook between them, and a truth that had taken years to find the right words. We stood beside the railing, hands close together. This time, I asked before taking hers. “May I?” Hina nodded. Her hand was warm inside mine. ---

Chapter Six Growing Up

March arrived with exam schedules and the uneasy feeling that every ordinary day had become a countdown. Hina had her own tests. I had university entrance exams. We could not meet at lunch as often, but our messages continued: a good-luck note before an exam, a complaint afterward, a photograph of a ridiculous vending-machine drink, a reminder to sleep before midnight. When I came out of a physics exam convinced I had forgotten everything I had ever learned, I found Hina waiting on a bench behind the school building. She did not ask how it went. She sat beside me and let the silence settle. After a while, she held out her hand. I took it. “Bad?” she asked. “Very.” “Then today was bad. It doesn't mean every day after this will be.” I looked at her. “You make it sound simple.” “It isn't simple. I'm just here.” We stayed there for a while, listening to traffic beyond the school wall. Her hand remained in mine—not as a promise that nothing would change, but as proof that I did not have to face every difficult thing alone. When exams ended, the question we had avoided finally came up: where would I go to university? I was accepted into an engineering programme in the same city, about forty minutes away by train. It was not the future I had pictured when I first started worrying, but it was a future I could imagine. Hina listened as I told her. “So you'll still be nearby,” she said. “Nearby enough to visit.” “Good.” She smiled. “I refuse to let you forget your umbrella from a different city.” We laughed, but I could hear the relief in her voice. I felt it too. Growing up was not a promise that nothing would change. It was learning to talk about change before fear made the decisions for us. Hina had her own studies, friends, family, and plans. I had mine. We would not always be in the same place at the same time. But we had learned how to say what mattered. That was a beginning, not a guarantee. It was enough. ---

Chapter Seven Where Our Story Begins

The final day at Seiran arrived under a clear evening sky. My friends took photographs in the courtyard. Teachers offered last-minute advice. Students who had complained about school for years suddenly stood around the classrooms as if they wanted to memorise every wall. For me, there was one place I wanted to visit before leaving. The rooftop. Hina met me there after the campus had begun to empty. The late sunlight touched the railing, turning it gold. Below us were the courtyard where I had first noticed her, the cafeteria where she had challenged my excuses, and the gate where she had once asked why I went quiet whenever she came near. Three years of ordinary moments seemed to fit inside that view. “I thought you'd be here,” she said. “I hoped you'd come.” She stood beside me. Our hands rested on the railing, close but not touching. “Are you scared?” she asked. “A little.” “Me too.” I turned toward her. “I don't know exactly what next year will look like. I can't promise we'll never have difficult days.” “I wouldn't believe you if you did.” “But I want to keep choosing this. To keep talking, even when we're busy. To tell you when I'm worried instead of disappearing into my own head.” Hina looked at me for a long moment. Then she took my hand. “I want that too.” The sun lowered beyond the rooftops. We stood quietly as the sky shifted from gold to orange and then to a soft pink. I thought of the boy I had been—the one who watched from a distance and believed a feeling became safer if he never spoke it aloud. I was still nervous. I still did not know every answer. But I no longer needed to know every answer before taking the next step. Hina leaned her forehead gently against mine. We closed our eyes for a moment, sharing the quiet while the last light of the day faded around us. When she stepped back, she was smiling. “Don't make this sound too dramatic when you write about it,” she said. “I make no promises.” “Haruto.” “Fine. I'll try.” We left the rooftop together and walked toward the gate. At the same place where our story had once begun with a nervous question, we stopped before going our separate ways. Hina turned toward her street. I turned toward the station. After a few steps, we both looked back at the same time. She laughed. I did too. Then she waved, and I waved back. I had once called her the Little Bird because I saw her from a distance and did not know how else to hold the feeling. Now I knew her name, her laugh, her stubbornness, her quiet kindness, and the dreams she carried beyond the walls of Seiran. She had never belonged to my imagination. She had always belonged to herself. And somehow, after three years of small conversations, awkward silences, rainy afternoons, shared songs, and words finally spoken, she had chosen to walk beside me. The school year was over. Our lives would keep changing, and neither of us could promise that every day ahead would be easy. But we had learned to speak honestly, to let each other remain fully ourselves, and to choose one another without pretending that choice could freeze time. Our story would continue in all the ordinary days we had not yet lived. This was the end of the chapter I had once been too afraid to begin—and a complete ending to the years at Seiran that taught me how to love someone as a real person, not an idea.
