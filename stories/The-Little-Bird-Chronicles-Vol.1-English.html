Chapter OneThe Girl by the School Gate

Seiran Academy was a private combined junior-high and senior-high school on the edge of a quiet Japanese city. The junior-high students wore their own uniform variations, the older students gathered around classroom windows before the first chime, and everyone left their outdoor shoes in the genkan before changing into indoor slippers. Beyond the school wall, a local train line rattled toward the city centre.

My second year of high school at Seiran Academy had settled into its usual rhythm: the morning chime, crowded corridors, teachers reminding us about exams, and my friends finding new ways to make an ordinary day louder than it needed to be.

I was good at being ordinary. I finished my work, laughed when everyone else laughed, and went home without anything worth telling anyone about. My life had no dramatic soundtrack. Most days, I was content with that.

Then, one afternoon in early June, I noticed a girl on the road outside Seiran Academy, beneath the rain-darkened branches of the zelkova trees.

It was not even a proper meeting. One of my friends had been hoping to catch a glimpse of someone he liked, so a few of us had lingered near the gate after classes. A girl with a similar bag came down the road, and we confidently decided she was the person we were waiting for. The next day, we learned we had been wrong.

That should have been the end of it. A small mistake, a joke for the group, nothing more.

A few days later, I took the quieter route toward the station. It was longer, but it passed a small shrine, a row of vending machines, and a residential street where the evening traffic was light. A girl approached from the other direction, her face partly hidden by a mask. I glanced at her as we passed, then looked back at the road.

For no sensible reason, the moment stayed with me.

It was not love at first sight. I did not know her name, her class, or a single thing about her. All I knew was that something about that brief encounter had interrupted the usual noise in my head.

I told myself I was being ridiculous and forgot about it—or tried to.

The next morning, our homeroom had just begun. Sunlight lay across the courtyard, and students hurried into their classroom after changing into indoor uwabaki. I was half-awake, listening to our teacher remind us about exams.

Then a late student hurried through the gate and joined the younger girls' line.

She was the girl from the road.

This time, she was not wearing a mask. I saw her face clearly for the first time, and the restless courtyard seemed to narrow around that single, ordinary detail. She was not trying to attract attention. She simply stood with her classmates, adjusting the strap of her bag while our homeroom teacher continued speaking.

I looked away before anyone noticed me staring.

Or so I hoped.

After that, I began to notice her in the small spaces between the important parts of the school day: near the stairs, crossing the courtyard, waiting by the gate. I never planned to look for her. My attention simply found her before I had decided where to put it.

Eventually, I learned that her name was Hina. She was in the first year of junior high school; I was in the second year of high school. We were at different stages of school, with different routines and different worlds around us. That distance mattered, even if my confused mind did not know what to do with the feeling yet.

I did not know enough about her to call it anything grand. I had a passing fascination with a person who was still a stranger. So, privately, I gave the feeling a name instead.

The Little Bird.

She seemed to appear and disappear among the crowds without asking anyone to notice her. The name belonged to my imagination, not to her. I kept it to myself, certain that if I said it aloud, my friends would turn it into a joke.

They found out anyway.

One afternoon, Daichi spotted her walking past the edge of the playground. His grin arrived before his words did.

“There she is,” he said, nudging my shoulder. “Your little bird.”

“Keep your voice down,” I muttered.

“Why? You look like someone just announced the results of your final exams.”

“You're making things up.”

“Of course. And you just happened to look at the gate six times in one minute.”

The others laughed. I tried to look annoyed, but that only encouraged them.

When Hina passed at a distance, I kept my eyes on the ground. It was easier to endure my friends' teasing than to risk making a stranger uncomfortable. I had no right to expect anything from her—not a smile, not a conversation, not even the smallest sign that she knew I existed.

Still, once in a while, our eyes met.

It lasted only a second. She would look away; I would remember the moment for the rest of the day. It was an embarrassing amount of meaning to assign to something so small, and a part of me knew that.

Then, one afternoon, she stopped near the gate.

My friends were beside me, but for once none of them spoke quickly enough to ruin the moment. Hina looked at me with a hint of uncertainty, as though she were matching my face to something she had heard.

“You're a second-year high school student, aren't you?” she asked.

“Yeah.” My voice came out quieter than I expected.

She glanced at my friends, who were making a heroic effort to pretend they were not listening. “You all seem to laugh a lot.”

There was a small smile in her voice. Before I could think of a reply, she continued on her way.

The whole exchange might have lasted ten seconds. I replayed it in my head for hours.

Not because it meant she liked me. It did not prove anything like that. But it was the first time the person I had been quietly noticing had spoken to me as though I were a real person, rather than another face in the crowd.

That was enough to make the day feel different.

Chapter TwoSmall Conversations

After that, I became painfully aware of how often our paths crossed. Sometimes I saw Hina near the stairs. Sometimes she was with her friends in the courtyard. Sometimes we passed each other without speaking at all.

I tried not to turn every coincidence into a clue. I reminded myself that we attended the same school, used the same corridors, and followed schedules that overlapped. Of course we would see each other.

My friends, unfortunately, had no interest in being reasonable.

“Did you talk today?” Daichi asked one morning.

“No.”

“Did you look at her?”

“I'm looking at you right now. Do you want a medal?”

“That's not an answer.”

“It's the only one you're getting.”

He laughed so loudly that a teacher glanced in our direction. I decided that if embarrassment were a school subject, Daichi would have graduated at the top of the class.

A few days later, I stopped by the courtyard drinking fountain after morning homeroom. I had no particular reason to linger there, but the courtyard was crowded and I wanted a moment away from the noise. Hina arrived with her bottle a little later.

For several seconds, we concentrated very hard on the tap.

Then she looked over. “You're always with your friends, aren't you?”

“Most of the time,” I said. “I'm used to it.”

“You all seem to have fun.”

“Mostly they make fun of me.”

That earned a quiet laugh. She filled her bottle, thanked me when I moved aside to let her pass, and returned to her friends.

It was not a turning point. It was not a secret signal. It was a normal conversation between two students, and I was beginning to understand that normal things could be meaningful without needing to become something else.

After that, saying hello became easier. Some days she greeted me first; other days I managed it before my courage disappeared. We exchanged a few words when our paths crossed, never for long. I learned that she liked sitting with her friends near the courtyard garden, where the hydrangeas had begun to bloom and that she had a habit of listening carefully before answering a question.

I did not try to learn everything about her. I did not need to. There was a difference between being interested in someone and treating them like a mystery to solve, and I was slowly learning where that line belonged.

One afternoon, as she passed my classroom, she paused.

“Your name is Haruto, right?”

I stared at her for half a second too long. “Yeah.”

“I heard someone call you that.”

“That would be Daichi. He calls me lots of things.”

She laughed, then hurried away to catch up with her friends.

I spent the rest of the day trying not to smile at absolutely nothing.

That evening, I thought about the difference between being noticed and being known. She had heard my name, and now she remembered it. It was a tiny thing, but it made the school feel a little less like a crowd of strangers.

Not long after, I was walking home alone when I heard my name behind me.

“Haruto.”

I turned. Hina was a few steps away, holding the strap of her schoolbag with both hands.

“You might be changing schools soon, won't you?” she asked.

“Probably. I'll have to go somewhere else for the next stage.”

She nodded and looked down the road. “That's how it goes, I guess. Everyone moves on eventually.”

I wanted to say something clever, something that would make the conversation last. Instead, all I managed was, “Yeah. I guess.”

For a moment, neither of us spoke. The silence was awkward, but not unpleasant. Then she wished me a good evening and continued on her way.

I walked home thinking about how little we knew about each other, and how easily a small conversation could make an ordinary road feel unfamiliar.

My private name for her stayed the same: the Little Bird. But now the name came with a reminder. She was not a character in a story I could write however I wanted. She was a person, living a life I knew almost nothing about.

Chapter ThreeThe Question I Couldn't Dodge

For the next few days, I kept wondering what our conversations meant. Were we becoming friends? Was I simply happy that someone I had noticed had started noticing me too? I had no clear answer, and perhaps there was no answer yet.

Then, on a quiet afternoon, we met on the narrow street that led toward the station—the same quiet street where I had first noticed her.

Hina was walking alone. I considered stepping aside and letting the moment pass, as I had done so many times before. Instead, I stopped at a respectful distance and waited to see whether she wanted to talk.

She stopped too.

“Can I ask you something?” she said.

“Sure.”

Her expression was curious, not angry. “Why do you always go quiet when I come near?”

There was no easy joke that could save me. I looked down at the road, then back at her.

“I get nervous,” I admitted.

“Because of me?”

“A little.”

She studied my face, as if deciding whether I was being honest. Then she asked, “Your friends call me the Little Bird, don't they?”

My stomach dropped. “They do.”

“That's what you call me too?”

“Only in my head,” I said quickly. “It's just a nickname I made up. I didn't mean to make you uncomfortable.”

She was quiet for a moment. I resisted the urge to fill the silence with excuses.

“It's a strange name,” she said at last.

“I know.”

“But at least you told me the truth.”

I let out a breath I had not realized I was holding. “I think you're nice. That's all. I didn't know how to say it without making everything awkward.”

Hina glanced toward the road ahead. “I'm not looking for a relationship,” she said plainly. “I have school, my friends, my own things to figure out.”

“I understand.”

“But we can talk when we see each other. If you want.”

“I'd like that.”

Her smile was small, but it was genuine. We said goodbye and went our separate ways.

For the first time in weeks, I felt calmer. Nothing had been promised. Nothing had been settled into a label. She had told me what she was comfortable with, and I had listened. That mattered more than the imaginary answers I had been trying to find in every glance.

When I told Daichi what had happened, he nearly fell off the bench.

“You actually talked to her?”

“Yes.”

“And you didn't faint?”

“I'm considering it now, just to stop you talking.”

He laughed, but his expression softened after a moment. “Seriously, though. Good for you.”

“For what?”

“For finally saying something instead of building an entire film in your head.”

I wanted to argue, but he was right.

After that, I tried to be less afraid of ordinary conversation. I said hello when I saw Hina. I asked how her day was. Sometimes she answered with a story about her friends; sometimes she was in a hurry and only waved. I learned not to treat every quiet day as a setback.

We were not suddenly inseparable. We were not secretly destined for anything. We were two students who had started to recognize each other, one small conversation at a time.

For now, that was enough.

Chapter FourA Different Kind of Familiar

Seiran Academy did not change after our conversation. The same teachers gave the same warnings about exams. The same chime marked the end of lessons. Daichi still found a way to turn almost anything into a joke.

But the space between Hina and me had changed. We no longer had to pretend we had never spoken. When we passed each other in the corridor, one of us would say hello. Sometimes we talked for a minute near the gate before heading home.

One day, her friend Yui called her toward the school cafeteria, where the lunch queues had already begun to form. Hina turned to follow, then noticed me standing nearby.

“Hi, Haruto.”

“Hey.”

Yui looked from her to me with an expression that suggested she had already written a whole story about the situation. Hina ignored the look and asked whether my exams were going badly.

“They're going normally,” I said. “Which means I'm worried.”

She laughed. “That sounds about right.”

Then she left with Yui, and I went back to class.

It was the sort of moment I might once have replayed until it became something it never was. Now I let it be what it was: a pleasant conversation on an ordinary school day.

My friends continued teasing me, of course. Daichi considered it his duty to announce whenever Hina came into view. I began telling him to stop when the jokes got too loud. If I wanted her to feel comfortable around me, I had to make sure my friends did not turn her into the center of a performance she had never agreed to join.

Hina noticed.

“You tell them to calm down now,” she said one afternoon.

“They don't always listen.”

“Still. You try.”

“They're idiots.”

“That much is obvious.”

We laughed, and for once I did not feel the need to search the moment for a hidden meaning.

As the term moved forward, I started thinking more seriously about the future: exams, results, and the choices waiting for me after graduation. Exams, results, a new school, unfamiliar classrooms. Everyone kept talking about the future as if it were a door we would all walk through together, even though each of us would end up somewhere different.

One afternoon, Hina asked whether I was nervous about leaving.

“A little,” I said. “I've been here so long that I can't imagine the next place yet.”

“You'll get used to it.”

“You sound very confident.”

“I'm not. I'm just saying it so you don't worry too much.”

That answer stayed with me longer than I expected. It was kind without being dramatic, and honest without asking for anything in return.

There were days when Hina was cheerful and days when she kept to herself. I stopped trying to interpret every change. She was allowed to have a mood that had nothing to do with me. The thought should have been obvious, but understanding it made the world feel wider.

I still called her the Little Bird in my private thoughts. The name had become a marker for a particular season of my life: the year when an ordinary school routine taught me to pay attention, to speak honestly, and to leave room for another person's choices.

It was not the whole story of her. It was only the small part of her story that had crossed mine.

Chapter FiveThe Last Afternoon

Time kept moving, even when I wanted a few days to last longer.

By the end of the term, talking to Hina no longer felt like stepping onto a stage. We exchanged greetings when we met, and sometimes we stood by the gate for a few minutes before heading home. There was no grand declaration between us, no promise that the future would bring us closer. There was simply a quiet familiarity, and I had learned to value it without demanding more.

One afternoon, I was waiting near the gate after most of my friends had left. I was not expecting anyone in particular. I had just grown used to pausing there before taking the narrow street that led toward the station.

Hina approached, her schoolbag resting on one shoulder. She looked thoughtful.

“You don't go completely silent anymore,” she said.

“I'm improving.”

“Slowly.”

“I'll take it.”

She smiled, then adjusted the strap of her bag. “I'm changing schools tomorrow.”

For a moment, I thought I had misunderstood her.

“Tomorrow?”

“It's a family decision. We have to move to a different area.”

I searched for a question that might make the news less final, but none of them would have changed anything. I could feel the old instinct returning—the urge to say something dramatic, to ask her not to go, to make the moment about the feeling I had carried around for weeks.

Instead, I asked, “Are you okay with it?”

She considered that. “I think so. It's strange. I'll miss my friends, but maybe a new place will be good.”

“Yeah,” I said. “I hope it is.”

We stood in silence while students streamed past us. The school sounded exactly as it always did: footsteps, distant laughter, the scrape of a gate being pulled open. Nothing around us knew that a small part of my routine was about to disappear.

“You know,” Hina said, “I thought you were difficult to talk to at first.”

“That seems fair.”

“But you never pushed me to talk when I didn't want to. I noticed that.”

I did not know what to say, so I told her the truth. “I was nervous. But I didn't want to make you uncomfortable.”

“I'm glad.”

There was no romantic confession hidden in those words. No sudden change in the world. Just a person acknowledging something another person had tried to do right.

She took a small step back, ready to leave.

“Remember the Little Bird,” she said, smiling a little. “At least make the nickname less embarrassing when you tell the story.”

I laughed. “I can't promise that.”

“Then don't tell it badly.”

“I'll try.”

She waved and started down the road. After a few steps, she turned once to wave again, then continued until the crowd swallowed her.

I stayed by the gate for a little while. I did not run after her. I did not ask for a promise or pretend that a goodbye could be undone if I found the perfect words. Her life was moving in a direction she had not chosen alone, and the kindest thing I could do was let her go without turning her decision into a burden.

Eventually, I took the narrow street that led toward the station.

The road looked the same as it had on the afternoon I first noticed her. The same uneven pavement, the same shops, the same patches of sunlight between the trees. I walked slowly, thinking about how a person could enter your life without a dramatic beginning and still leave a mark.

I had given her a name before I knew her. Over time, I learned that a name could hold a memory, but it could never contain an entire person.

Hina was more than the girl I noticed in the school courtyard. She was a student with her own friends, her own worries, and a future that did not revolve around my feelings. I hoped the next school would be kind to her. I hoped she would find people who made her laugh.

And I hoped I would remember this year honestly—not as a love story that almost happened, but as a small chapter about growing up.

Some people do not arrive to stay. Sometimes they cross your path, change the shape of an ordinary day, and keep walking.

The next morning, students changed into their uwabaki and filed into homeroom as usual. I looked toward the younger students out of habit, then reminded myself that the habit would fade. The school bell rang. My friends started talking. The day carried on.

For the first time, I understood that an ending did not have to turn a memory into a tragedy.

The Little Bird had flown toward a sky I could not follow.

And I could wish her well without asking her to turn back.

I thought that was the end of the story. I did not know that, a year later, I would find myself looking toward the school gate again—not because the past had changed, but because life had quietly brought Hina back into view.
