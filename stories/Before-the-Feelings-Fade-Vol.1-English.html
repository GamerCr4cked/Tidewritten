Chapter One The Girl by the Window

Second year of middle school began with the usual smell of chalk, old desks, and rainwater drying near the entrance. The ceiling fan above Sota's seat made a tired clicking sound. His notebook contained half a page of class notes and a drawing of a spaceship escaping the planet.

“How long until this period ends?” he whispered.

Yuto, sitting beside him, rested his cheek on his folded arms. “Ask me again in twenty minutes. I'll have suffered through the answer by then.”

Sota nearly laughed aloud. He turned toward the window to hide it—and noticed the girl in the second row.

Airi Tanaka was writing in a neat, steady hand. Her hair fell forward whenever she leaned over her notebook, and she tucked it behind her ear without looking up. She was not doing anything remarkable. She was simply there, focused on her work while the rest of the classroom drifted through another ordinary morning.

Sota looked away almost immediately.

Just a classmate, he told himself. I'll probably forget about this by tomorrow.

He did not.

Over the next few weeks, he learned small things without meaning to. Airi was one of the best students in class, but she never made anyone feel foolish for asking for help. She spoke softly when called on, yet her answers were clear. Sometimes she wrote in the margins of her notebook long after the teacher had moved to the next topic. Whatever she was writing made her smile to herself.

One afternoon, the English teacher asked for a volunteer to read a passage aloud. The room became a landscape of lowered eyes and suddenly fascinating pencil cases.

Airi raised her hand.

Her voice was quiet, but every word reached the back of the classroom. Sota found himself listening more carefully than he had listened to anything all day.

When she finished, her gaze briefly crossed his. He immediately looked down at his own notebook as if the spaceship had become urgent business.

Nothing had happened. Still, something had begun.

Chapter Two The Small Things That Stayed

A school year is made mostly of things too ordinary to remember: attendance calls, borrowed erasers, lunch bells, and the long walk between classrooms. For Sota, those small things gradually collected around Airi.

One morning, he discovered that he had left his history notebook at home. The teacher told everyone to share with a classmate. Yuto lifted his own notebook and whispered, “I've got one. Use mine.”

Sota thanked him, but his feet had already carried him toward Airi's desk.

“Tanaka-san? Could I look at your notes for a bit?”

She glanced up, surprised, then moved the notebook between them. “Sure. Just give it back before lunch. I still need it.”

“Of course.”

That was all. She returned to her work, and he copied the notes with the concentration of someone entrusted with a national secret.

Another time, the class was asked to work in groups on a science project. Airi leaned across the table to point at a diagram just as Sota reached for the ruler. Their fingers nearly touched. He pulled his hand back so quickly that Yuto stared at him.

“Are you afraid of stationery now?” Yuto asked later.

“Be quiet.”

“I didn't say anything.”

“You said it with your face.”

Yuto laughed, but he did not tease him in front of Airi. That kindness made Sota grateful—and even more embarrassed.

When the rainy season arrived, Sota began carrying an extra foldable umbrella. He told himself it was practical. He never quite found the right moment to offer it to her.

He rehearsed ordinary sentences on the walk to school: Did you understand the homework? That book looks interesting. The rain is really coming down, isn't it?

When he finally stood close enough to speak, the words usually became a polite “Good morning.” Airi always answered with the same gentle smile, then continued on her way.

He had begun to look forward to a smile that lasted only a second.

Chapter Three A Letter Folded Four Times

By March, the school year was almost over. Exams filled the classroom with restless energy, and everyone was talking about the next grade. Sota had another worry: if he said nothing, the year would end with the truth still trapped inside him.

Yuto listened to him circle the same question for the third time after school.

“If you like someone, tell them,” Yuto said. “The worst that can happen is that they don't feel the same way. It won't destroy the planet.”

“Easy for you to say.”

“It is. I'm not the one writing a speech in my head every time she asks to borrow a pencil.”

Sota had no defence.

That night, he sat at his desk under a small lamp. The first draft sounded like a line from a bad romance drama. He tore it up. The second sounded like a formal application. He tore that one up too. The third became two pages of every little moment he had remembered, which felt less like a confession and more like a diary he had accidentally addressed to someone else.

At last, he took a clean sheet and wrote simply:

Airi, I don't know if this is the right thing to say, but I don't want to keep hiding it. I like you. I think I have for a long time. You don't have to feel the same, and I'm not asking you to give me an answer I want. I just wanted you to know.

His handwriting wavered on the last line. He folded the letter four times and placed it in his pocket. He barely slept.

The next morning, every corridor seemed too crowded. At lunch, he finally approached Airi while she was putting her books away.

“Tanaka-san… could you read this when you have time?”

He held out the folded paper. His hand felt strangely distant from the rest of him.

Airi looked from the letter to his face. Surprise crossed her expression, followed by something careful and kind.

“All right,” she said quietly.

He spent the rest of the day unable to remember what any teacher had said.

Chapter Four Her Answer

Airi did not mention the letter in class. She did not tell their classmates or treat Sota differently. For the next two days, the ordinary rhythm of school continued, and every ordinary moment made him more nervous.

On the third afternoon, she asked if they could speak briefly near the shoe lockers, where students were changing into their outdoor shoes and the hallway smelled faintly of wet umbrellas.

“Thank you for telling me honestly,” she said, holding the folded letter carefully between the pages of her notebook. “I was surprised, but I'm glad you trusted me enough to say it.”

Sota tried to breathe normally. “Okay.”

She paused, choosing her words. “I'm sorry. I don't want to be in a relationship right now. I have a lot I want to focus on, and I don't think I can return your feelings the way you hope.”

There was no cruelty in her voice. She did not laugh, and she did not pretend that she might say yes later just to make the moment easier.

That made the answer both kinder and harder to hear.

“I understand,” Sota said. His voice sounded steadier than he felt. “You don't have to apologize.”

“I hope we can still be classmates. I don't want you to feel you have to avoid me.”

He nodded. “We can.”

They returned to their separate routines. Airi thanked him once more, then left to catch her train. Sota stood by the lockers until the hallway began to empty.

He had imagined rejection as a dramatic scene: a long speech, a sudden storm, a moment that would divide his life into before and after. Instead, it was a quiet conversation between two students at the end of an ordinary school day.

The next morning, Airi greeted him as usual. He answered, and she went on to class.

Nothing had changed for her—or at least, nothing she wanted to show. For Sota, the classroom was the same room with a new distance inside it.

Chapter Five The Same Face, Years Later

Years later, Sota returned to his old school on a late spring afternoon. He was in college now, living among new streets, unfamiliar faces, and schedules that no longer revolved around a school bell. He had not thought about Airi in months—or at least, he had not thought about her often enough to admit it.

Then he saw the gate.

The walls seemed shorter than he remembered. The zelkova trees along the street had grown thicker, and the courtyard looked smaller than the world it had once contained. A school event was underway, so the gate stood open. Without deciding to, Sota stepped inside.

He walked past the shoe lockers, the windows that caught the afternoon sun, and the corridor where he had practised sentences he never managed to say. Near the classroom stairs, he slowed down.

The old room was empty. Desks stood in neat rows, and a pale rectangle of sunlight stretched across the floor. He recognised the second row, the window seat, and the place where Airi had once sat with her notebook open.

Sota took his old seat near the back.

For a moment, he could remember everything: the first time he noticed her, the borrowed history notes, the almost-touch during science, the letter trembling in his pocket, and her gentle refusal by the shoe lockers.

He was surprised by how clearly he remembered it all. The feeling had changed over time, but it had never become a story he was ashamed of. He had liked someone, told her honestly, and accepted that she did not feel the same. It had hurt, but it had also taught him that honesty did not have to end in the answer he wanted to be worthwhile.

Outside, a ball bounced across the courtyard. A student ran after it, laughing, and the sound carried through the open window.

Sota smiled. He stood, brushed a little dust from his trousers, and looked once more at the empty desk by the window.

He was about to leave when a familiar voice called from the corridor.

“Mori-kun?”

He turned. Airi stood near the classroom door with another former student beside her. She looked older, of course, but her expression still held that quiet, attentive warmth he remembered. For a second, neither of them seemed to know which version of their past to greet first.

Then Airi smiled. “It's been a long time.”

“Yeah. It has.”

Their conversation was a little awkward at first. They talked about college, teachers, and how the classroom looked smaller than it used to. The other former student soon called Airi to help with the event, and she excused herself. Nothing dramatic happened. There was no sudden return of the old feeling, no new confession waiting behind the door.

Before she left, Airi asked, “Are you still using Instagram? We should keep in touch with everyone from class.”

“I am,” Sota said. “Sure.”

They exchanged contact details and joined a group chat that Yuto had apparently created without asking anyone. By the end of the afternoon, the chat had twelve members, three different names, and a debate about where to get food next weekend.

“This is already a mess,” Sota wrote.

Airi replied with a sticker of a tiny rabbit holding a sign that read please cooperate.

He laughed. It was a familiar kind of laugh, but it belonged to the present now, not to the boy who had once rehearsed a sentence for weeks before speaking to her.

Over the following months, their messages became ordinary. They shared jokes, replied to stories, and joined outings with their friends. They rarely went anywhere alone; the group was always there, arguing about restaurants, missing trains, or taking photographs in which somebody blinked. Sota and Airi became friends—not because his old feelings had been magically erased, but because he chose to respect her answer and let their relationship become what it could honestly be.

Sometimes a memory still caught him off guard. Sometimes her kindness reminded him of the girl by the window. But he was learning that remembering a feeling did not mean he had to pursue it again.

He did not know what the years ahead would bring. For now, there were messages to answer, friends to meet, and ordinary days that were slowly becoming memories of their own.

The feeling had changed.

The story had not finished teaching him how to remember.

Two years later, Sota would learn that being friends with someone was not the same as being finished with the feelings they once inspired.
