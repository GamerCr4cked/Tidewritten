Chapter One The Wrong Flat

Aarav Mehta arrived at B-204 with two suitcases, a backpack, and a plan to spend his first night in Delhi doing absolutely nothing.

He was a freelance designer. Work came in waves, money followed its own unpredictable calendar, and the only thing he needed from an apartment was a desk, reliable Wi-Fi, and enough quiet to think. The broker, Pandey, had assured him that the flat was perfect.

“Nothing to worry about, sir.”

Aarav should have been suspicious of anyone who said that before being asked.

He unlocked the door, set down his bags, and discovered a pink suitcase in the bedroom.

Then the front door opened.

A young woman stepped inside carrying groceries. She stopped when she saw him.

“Who are you?”

“I live here.”

“No, you don't.”

They compared agreements. Same flat. Same move-in date. Same broker. Two security deposits had vanished into the same dishonest arrangement.

The owner did not answer. Pandey's phone was switched off. Night was approaching, and neither had enough money to solve the problem with a hotel room.

The woman introduced herself as Zara Khan. She was a junior architect, exhausted from moving, and in no mood to be generous toward a stranger who had appeared in her home.

Aarav felt the same way.

They argued over rooms, keys, and who had arrived first. Eventually, the practical truth won: until they could reach the owner, they would have to share the flat and document everything.

They wrote rules on a sheet of paper and stuck it to the refrigerator. Knock before entering the other person's room. Replace what you use. No borrowing without asking. Keep work calls quiet after midnight.

Zara added one more rule.

“No pretending we're friends.”

“Easy,” Aarav said.

It was the first promise either of them would find difficult to keep.

Chapter Two Rules and Exceptions

The first week was a cold war conducted through household chores.

Aarav labelled his shelves. Zara labelled hers. They took turns cleaning the kitchen with the solemnity of diplomats negotiating a border. Their conversations consisted mostly of reminders about groceries and the Wi-Fi router.

Then small accidents began to interfere.

Zara worked late on a building model and forgot to eat. Aarav left a plate of rice and vegetables outside her door, then sent a message saying the extra food was going to waste. She thanked him the next morning without mentioning that she had eaten every bite.

Aarav once spent an entire evening trying to fix a stubborn design file. Zara noticed the light beneath his door at two in the morning and knocked with a mug of tea.

“It's not a solution,” she said. “It's just tea.”

“That's usually how tea works.”

She almost smiled.

They learned each other's routines. Zara hummed when she concentrated. Aarav paced when he was waiting for a client response. She liked the windows open; he preferred the fan. They argued about the temperature, then discovered that both of them were more reasonable after dinner.

The owner finally replied and promised to investigate the broker's fraud. The money would take time to recover, and neither could afford to move immediately.

By then, the flat had begun to feel less hostile.

Aarav helped Zara carry a heavy model to her studio. She reviewed his portfolio and pointed out that his best designs were the ones he had made for himself, not clients who wanted every idea to look expensive.

“You're good,” she told him. “You just keep hiding behind what people ask you to make.”

He remembered the sentence long after she left the room.

They still called themselves temporary roommates. But when Aarav came home to find Zara laughing at a terrible cooking video, he caught himself wanting to tell her about his day.

The rules remained on the refrigerator.

The exceptions were multiplying.

Chapter Three The Almost

Months passed. Aarav's work became steadier, and Zara began receiving more responsibility at the architecture firm. They celebrated small victories with takeaway food on the balcony.

Aarav helped her prepare for a presentation. Zara helped him negotiate a difficult client contract. When one of them had a bad day, the other knew whether to offer advice, tea, or silence.

One evening, a power cut left the flat lit by the city outside. Zara sat beside him on the sofa, looking through a folder of his early designs.

“You were happier when you made things without worrying whether someone would buy them,” she said.

“Rent is not paid in artistic fulfilment.”

“No, but it shouldn't cost you everything you like about yourself.”

He turned to answer. She was closer than he expected. For a moment, neither moved. The space between them felt full of everything they had never discussed.

Then Zara stood.

“I should finish my presentation.”

“Right.”

They both pretended the moment had not happened.

A week later, Zara began seeing Dev, a confident colleague who knew how to make every room notice him. Aarav tried to be supportive. He told himself he had no right to feel disappointed; he had never told Zara what she meant to him.

Dev began visiting the flat. He was charming in public, but small things troubled Aarav: the way he dismissed Zara's work, the way he treated her time as something he owned, the way she apologised for inconveniences that were not her fault.

Aarav kept his concerns to himself. He did not want to become the jealous roommate who mistook his own feelings for wisdom.

When Zara finally asked what he thought of Dev, Aarav answered carefully.

“I think you deserve to be able to disagree with someone without being made to feel guilty for it.”

She looked away.

“I know he's not perfect.”

“Neither am I. That's not the point.”

For the first time, Zara seemed to understand that Aarav was not trying to win an argument. He was worried about her.

Chapter Four The Other Guy

The truth surfaced on a Thursday night.

Zara came home late, left her bag on the floor, and sat at the kitchen table without turning on the light. Aarav found her there when he went for water.

She had discovered that Dev had been seeing someone else. When she confronted him, he blamed her for being busy, for working too much, for not giving him enough attention. He apologised only after she said she was leaving.

Aarav listened. He wanted to tell her he had known something was wrong. Instead, he asked whether she wanted him to stay or give her space.

“Stay,” she said.

He made tea. She talked until the kitchen clock passed midnight. He did not tell her that he had feelings for her, not while she was hurt and trying to understand what had happened.

Over the next days, he helped her return the things Dev had left at the flat and reminded her that ending a relationship was not a failure. Zara began to feel like herself again. She laughed more. She worked without checking her phone every few minutes.

Then, one evening, she asked Aarav why he had never dated anyone.

“I don't know,” he said. “I suppose I don't want to be chosen only because I'm convenient.”

Zara stared at him.

“Is that what you think happened with us? That we're just convenient?”

“No. I think living together made it easy to confuse comfort with something else.”

“And what do you feel?”

He could have hidden behind a joke. Instead, he told her the truth: he cared about her, but he did not expect an answer, and he did not want her to choose him just because Dev had hurt her.

Zara's eyes softened. “I need time to understand what I feel.”

“Take it.”

For once, neither of them tried to force the next chapter.

Chapter Five A Split in the Road

Time made the answer clearer, though not the one Aarav had secretly hoped for.

Zara told him she cared about him deeply. The flat had become a home because he was in it. She trusted him, admired him, and felt safe around him. But she could not honestly say that she was in love with him.

“I don't want to lose what we have,” she said. “But I don't want to pretend either.”

Aarav felt the disappointment, let it exist, and did not turn it into a punishment.

“Then we don't pretend.”

They agreed that one of them should move when the lease allowed it. Not because either had done something wrong, but because staying in the same arrangement would make it harder to build separate lives.

The move happened in November. Zara found a small apartment closer to her office. Aarav helped her pack books and plants, and they ate their last dinner in the flat on the floor because the table had already been moved.

At the door, Zara hugged him.

“Thank you for never making me owe you anything,” she said.

Aarav swallowed the words he had prepared and answered honestly.

“Thank you for telling me the truth.”

They stayed friends, though their conversations became less frequent. Zara dated again eventually. Aarav poured his energy into his work, built a stronger portfolio, and won his first major branding award.

When Zara called months later, newly single and wondering whether they could try again, Aarav listened kindly. He realised he no longer wanted to return to the question of whether she might choose him. He had finally built a life he did not need someone else to validate.

“I care about you,” he told her. “But I don't think going back is right for me.”

There was silence on the line. Then Zara said she understood.

After the call, Aarav made tea and stood on the balcony. The November sunlight fell across his new desk and the framed award on his shelf.

He was alone, but loneliness no longer felt like proof that something was missing.

The flat had split their lives into before and after. The betrayal had taught Zara what she would no longer accept. The almost-relationship had taught Aarav that caring for someone did not mean waiting indefinitely to be chosen.

He had not received the ending he once wanted.

He had found something he needed more: himself.

Some people enter your life to stay. Others help you discover where you stand. And sometimes the most honest ending is not revenge, not reunion, but the quiet decision to walk forward without carrying resentment.

One flat. Two people. Two separate futures.

Both finally their own.
