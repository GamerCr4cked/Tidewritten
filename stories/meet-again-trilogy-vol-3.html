Chapter One Colours of Silence

Two years after the night everything broke, Akito Mori had built a life that worked. He had finished his degree, moved to another city, and found an internship at a small photography studio. His room was plain: a bed, a desk, a camera bag, and a shelf of books he meant to read. He earned enough to pay rent and buy film for his camera. He spoke to Kenta most weeks. His photography series, *Colours of Silence*, had become quietly popular. Empty stations, closed classrooms, a bench after rain. He photographed places that looked as if someone had just left them. People called the images beautiful. Akito accepted the compliment without explaining how often a particular face appeared in his thoughts while he edited them. Mio had also graduated. She worked as a junior editor for a small publishing company in another city. She was more careful with her words than she used to be, and she had learned that feeling guilty was not the same as making amends. She had not contacted Akito. Then an invitation arrived from a gallery in their old college town. The curator had selected several photographs from *Colours of Silence* for a group exhibition. Akito nearly declined. Kenta told him that refusing every place connected to his past was not the same as moving forward. So he went. The gallery was bright, crowded, and full of conversations. Akito stayed near the wall until he noticed a familiar figure standing before one of his photographs. Mio. She was looking at a photograph of a bench beneath bare winter trees. He had taken it long after their last conversation, but the place was the one where they had once sat together after a difficult day. The caption read: *Where it began. Where it ended.* Mio studied it for a long time. When she turned, their eyes met. Neither moved immediately. Then Akito walked over. “Hello, Mio.” “Hello, Akito.” Two years of unsent messages, regret, and imagined conversations had narrowed to four ordinary words. “How have you been?” she asked. “I'm working. Taking photographs.” “I saw the series online. It's beautiful.” “Thank you.” She did not ask whether the photographs were about her. He did not ask why she had come. They spoke for a few minutes about work, the gallery, and the town that had changed less than either expected. When they parted, Akito felt shaken—but not undone. The past had found them in the same room. For the first time, neither had tried to rewrite it.

Chapter Two The Apology That Owed Nothing

Mio sent one message the next day. *Thank you for speaking to me yesterday. I won't keep contacting you. I only wanted to say that I was glad to see you doing well.* Akito read it, thought for a while, and replied: *I'm glad you're doing well too.* The exchange was small. It did not reopen their relationship. It simply allowed them to acknowledge that they were two people living beyond the worst moment of their lives. A week later, Akito returned to the gallery to collect a print. Mio happened to be there too, helping a colleague prepare for another event. They ended up walking to the station together. She asked whether he would be willing to hear a proper apology—not because she expected forgiveness, but because she no longer wanted to hide behind the vague words she had used before. Akito considered it. “All right,” he said. “But I can't promise what I'll feel afterward.” “I understand.” They sat at a quiet café near the station. Mio told him the truth plainly. She had enjoyed being wanted by someone new. She had been afraid of losing Akito, so she tried to keep both the comfort of their relationship and the excitement of Ryo's attention. She had hidden things, dismissed Akito's concerns, and apologised only when the truth could no longer stay hidden. “I kept telling myself I was confused,” she said. “I was confused, but I also made choices. I hurt you, and you didn't deserve it.” Akito listened without interrupting. “I can't ask you to trust me because I feel sorry,” she continued. “I know I have to accept what my choices cost.” For a while, he watched the steam rise from his coffee. “Thank you for finally saying it clearly,” he replied. “I believe you mean it. That doesn't mean I can go back to how things were.” “I know.” They left the café separately. Akito felt no sudden release, no cinematic moment when the past became harmless. But the apology had not asked him to comfort her or make her guilt disappear. It had given the truth its proper name. That mattered.

Chapter Three Rebuilding Without Shortcuts

Their next meeting happened by chance, then another by choice. They began with occasional messages about ordinary things: a photograph Akito had exhibited, a book Mio had edited, a café they both remembered. Neither demanded an immediate reply. If one of them needed space, they said so. Akito was careful not to confuse kindness with a promise. Mio was careful not to confuse access with forgiveness. At first, their conversations were polite. Gradually, they became more natural. They remembered how to laugh together, though the laughter no longer erased the difficult things they knew about each other. One evening, Mio asked if she could visit the small studio where Akito worked. He agreed, and showed her the prints he was preparing for a new series. She stopped in front of a photograph of sunlight crossing an empty room. “This one feels different,” she said. “How?” “It doesn't look like someone has just left. It looks like someone might arrive.” Akito looked at the photograph again. “I hadn't thought of it that way.” “Maybe that's why I like it.” He smiled, surprised by how much he had missed talking about something without having to defend himself. Trust did not return because they wanted it to. It grew through small, observable choices. Mio told the truth even when it made her look bad. Akito said when he felt uneasy instead of pretending he was fine. They discussed boundaries without treating them as punishment. Some days, old hurt returned. When it did, they did not pretend it had vanished. They slowed down, spoke honestly, and allowed the feeling to pass without forcing an answer. Rebuilding was less like repairing a broken vase and more like learning whether a new house could be built on ground that had once shifted. Neither of them knew how it would end. For once, not knowing did not mean they had to run.

Chapter Four A Visit in Winter

In January, Akito travelled to Mio's city. He had a free weekend and a question he wanted to ask in person. He told her he was coming. He did not appear unannounced at her door or turn the visit into a test. They agreed to meet near a park beside the river. Mio arrived wearing a grey coat and the scarf she had owned during their college days. The sight of it made Akito remember the person she had been—and the person she was now. They walked beneath bare branches. The air was cold enough to make their breath visible. “I don't know if we can become what we were,” Akito said. Mio looked at him. “I don't think we should try to become exactly that.” He had expected her to ask for another chance. Instead, she was giving him room to decide what a chance might mean. “I still care about you,” he admitted. “But I don't want to ignore what happened just because I miss you.” “You shouldn't.” “And I can't promise that I'll never be afraid of being hurt again.” “I can't promise you that you'll never be hurt. I can promise to be honest with you, even when the truth is difficult.” They stopped beside the river. The water moved beneath the winter light, steady and indifferent to the decisions being made along its banks. Akito took a breath. “I'd like to try getting to know you again. Slowly. Not as if nothing happened.” Mio's eyes filled with tears, but she did not rush toward him or ask him to make the decision bigger than it was. “I'd like that too,” she said. They began walking again. Their hands brushed once, then again. Mio looked down at the space between them, giving him the choice. Akito reached for her hand. She held it gently. It was not a return to the past. It was a small, careful step into something neither of them could guarantee. For now, that was enough.

Chapter Five Phir Milenge

Spring returned to the old college town. The cherry trees flowered along the station road, and students hurried beneath them with their bags bouncing against their shoulders. Akito and Mio did not announce that they were together again. They were still learning what their relationship could become. They made plans and kept them. They spoke when something felt wrong. They did not use affection to silence difficult questions. Some days were easy. Some were not. When Akito felt old doubts return, he tried to remember that caution was not the same as certainty. When Mio made a mistake, she learned to admit it instead of hiding until it became a larger problem. Neither could undo the past; both could decide what to do next. One afternoon, they visited Seihoku College. The cafeteria had new tables, and the old vending machines were gone. The auditorium looked smaller than Akito remembered. Mio stood beside the seat where they had first met. “You were so quiet,” she said. “You talked enough for both of us.” “I still do.” “Some things don't change.” She smiled. “Some things do.” They walked outside and sat beneath the cherry trees. A few petals drifted onto the path. Akito thought of the first year, when the future had seemed simple because neither of them knew what it would ask of them. “Do you regret meeting me?” Mio asked. He considered the question honestly. “No. I regret the ways we hurt each other. I regret the things we hid. But I don't regret that we met.” Mio nodded, taking the answer as it was rather than asking it to mean more. They watched the afternoon pass. Eventually, she leaned her shoulder against his. He let it rest there. The future was still uncertain. They would have to keep earning each other's trust, and love would not excuse them from the work of growing up. But they were no longer pretending that a second chance meant forgetting. When they reached the station, Mio looked back toward the campus. “Phir milenge,” she said. Akito smiled. “We just did.” “I mean next time.” “Next time too.” The train arrived. They boarded together, not because their story had become perfect, but because they had chosen to continue it with their eyes open. Some people meet, part, and never return to each other's lives. Some meet again and discover that the past has changed what they can offer. And sometimes, after truth has been spoken and time has done its quiet work, two people decide to begin again—not from the beginning, but from where they truly are. Phir milenge. When we meet again.
